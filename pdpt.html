🌳 The story: therapy in the woods

Tucked into two acres of woodland at Friday Woods, just outside Colchester, is Kate's Garden — by its own account, the only practice of its kind in the country. Founder Kate Beckwith trained on Essex's MA in Psychodynamic Counselling, and now runs a psychodynamic and psychoanalytic practice for children and young people (up to 25) that works through the outdoors, animals, art and play rather than a consulting room — though a homely playroom and sensory room are there too, for when a session needs walls.

Worth 15 minutes Beckwith made the case for the approach herself at TEDxUniversity of Essex — "Opening the Doors: Revolutionising Child Therapy." If you watch one thing from this issue, it's probably this.

Also findable: a Counselling Directory profile and a Facebook page with more day-to-day updates.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

Two 2026 papers landed that are relevant to the field, plus one older paper that's worth knowing as background if you don't already.

Does taking the body seriously help with depression? A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology looked specifically at psychodynamic treatments that take an embodied perspective — working with the subjective, felt experience of depression in the body, not just the narrative of it — across a set of randomised controlled trials. The embodied-perspective angle is the interesting part: it's a specific methodological lens, not just "psychodynamic therapy works," and worth reading if that framing is new to you.
How do therapists themselves define "change"? A 2026 phenomenological study on PubMed interviewed experienced psychodynamic psychotherapists about how they conceptualise change in their clients — a first-person, practitioner-side view rather than an outcome-measure study. Useful counterweight to the usual efficacy-trial framing.
Background, if useful Efficacy comparisons against CBT and others generally show no significant difference — psychodynamic work holds its own. The therapeutic alliance itself seems to matter a lot, indirectly driving improvement by deepening emotional experience. If you want the classic, oft-cited reference point for "does this actually work," Jonathan Shedler's The Efficacy of Psychodynamic Psychotherapy (APA) is still the paper people reach for. A 2024 meta-analysis specific to young adults adds some early support there too, though the authors flag the evidence base as still thin for that age group.
