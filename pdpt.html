🏛️ The story: Colchester's psychodynamic hub, fifty years on

Tucked into a Georgian townhouse on East Stockwell Street, The Stockwell Centre has been quietly running since 1976, founded by Dr Deirdre Paulley — which puts it within a year or two of its 50th anniversary. It's a registered charity rather than a single practitioner's practice, and that's what makes it worth a closer look: its therapist roster reads like a who's-who of the local psychodynamic scene, including several graduates of the University of Essex's MA Psychodynamic Counselling (Justin Hayes, Rachel Irvine, Jeanette Johnston, Tisha Kachhapati among them), a Tavistock-trained psychotherapist (Emily Mercer, registered with the British Psychoanalytic Council), and a psychoanalytic psychotherapist supervisor (Dr Seb Randall, College of Psychoanalysts UK). For a town the size of Colchester, that's an unusual concentration of psychodynamic training under one roof.

Worth knowing The Centre also runs training and consultative work for medical and caring professions alongside its clinical work — so it functions as much as a local hub for the profession as a place to see a client. 🔬 Research worth knowing about

Two 2026 studies landed that speak to different ends of the evidence question: one on durability of gains in adolescents, one on a specific anxiety disorder.

Does internet-delivered psychodynamic therapy hold up a year later, in teenagers? A 2026 Swedish RCT in Frontiers in Psychiatry followed 272 adolescents (15–19) with major depressive disorder for 12 months after a 10-week course of guided, internet-delivered therapy — half got a psychodynamic version, half got CBT. At the year mark, both groups had held onto their gains equally well, and cost about the same to deliver (roughly $3,237 vs $2,717 per adolescent). The finding isn't "psychodynamic wins" — it's that a therapist-guided psychodynamic approach delivered remotely is just as durable as CBT for teenagers, which matters for anyone weighing which modality to commission or train in.
Does psychodynamic therapy actually help with social anxiety? A 2026 meta-analysis in BMC Psychology pooled 11 randomised trials (1,167 patients) of short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy for social anxiety disorder. Against waitlist controls, the effect was large (g = −0.97) — a clear signal the approach does something real for social anxiety specifically, an area psychodynamic research has covered less than depression.
Background, if useful Efficacy comparisons against CBT and others generally show no significant difference — psychodynamic work holds its own, and this week's adolescent trial is another data point for that pattern. The therapeutic alliance itself seems to matter a lot, indirectly driving improvement by deepening emotional experience. If you want the classic, oft-cited reference point for "does this actually work," Jonathan Shedler's The Efficacy of Psychodynamic Psychotherapy (APA) is still the paper people reach for.
