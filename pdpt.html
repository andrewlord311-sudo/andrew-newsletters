💔 A national suicide-prevention charity has closed — Papyrus and HOPELINE247 are gone

Papyrus Prevention of Young Suicide entered administration on 7 September 2026, with joint administrators Emma Mifsud and Charles Turner of Opus Restructuring & Insolvency appointed that day. Its services — including HOPELINE247, the national helpline for young people at risk of suicide and for the adults supporting them — are no longer operating, and more than 120 jobs have been lost. The charity, which had an annual income of over £5.3m, had run for almost 30 years.

Why this belongs here, not just in the national news This isn't a Colchester or Essex story, and this newsletter's default is local — but HOPELINE247 was a standard referral point for anyone doing risk-aware talking-therapy work anywhere in the country, this area included, and it disappeared overnight. The administrators cite rising demand, increased trading costs and "a reduction in reliable funding across the sector" as the reasons a way forward couldn't be found. UKCP responded on 10 September, and the National Child Mortality Database also issued a statement mourning the loss of a partner it says contributed "invaluable expertise and lived experience perspectives" to youth suicide prevention work. Anyone who had HOPELINE247 saved as a referral number should update it — Samaritans (116 123) and Shout (text 85258) remain available.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

New this week: does short-term psychodynamic therapy help with social anxiety? A meta-analysis in BMC Psychology (published March 2026) pooled 11 randomised controlled trials, covering 1,167 people, testing short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy (STPP) against waitlist and other control conditions for social anxiety disorder. STPP came out clearly ahead of waitlist controls, with a large effect on social anxiety symptoms and — reassuringly for anyone wary of longer-format therapy — no higher drop-out rate than the comparison treatments. In plain terms: for social anxiety specifically, a relatively short course of psychodynamic therapy looks like a genuine, tolerable option, not just a slower alternative to CBT.
New this week: does focusing on bodily experience help psychodynamic treatment for depression? A systematic review and meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology (Heider, Sulz & Franzen, published 26 March 2026) looked at 11 trials, 1,624 patients, of psychodynamic treatments that deliberately work with bodily sensation and awareness — using physical signals diagnostically, noticing gaps between what a client says and how their body looks, and linking sensation back to underlying feeling — as an approach to depression. These "embodied" approaches showed meaningful symptom reduction against control conditions, holding up or improving at follow-up, and came out roughly level with established therapies like CBT where compared. The authors are careful to call this preliminary rather than conclusive: 11 trials is a modest evidence base, and they say the technique itself still needs "further conceptual elaboration" before it's clearly defined enough to test properly. Worth knowing about, not yet worth treating as settled.
