🎓 Essex's psychodynamic training department gets an answer on restructuring — folded into a new Faculty

The University of Essex announced on 3 August 2026 that it has established a new Faculty of Arts, Humanities and Social Sciences, merging two existing faculties into 11 academic departments under one structure. The Department of Psychosocial and Psychoanalytic Studies — the Colchester-based department that trains many of the area's psychodynamic counsellors and psychotherapists, and holds the Freud and Balint archive papers — is confirmed as one of the departments now sitting inside this new Faculty.

Why this matters Since February 2026, alumni and professional contacts have been campaigning to keep the department independent amid university-wide restructuring that saw roughly 400 posts cut and the Southend campus close. This is the first concrete, public sign of where the department has actually landed: not closed, not merged into a single unrelated department, but one of 11 within a larger new Faculty that the university says brings together "leading disciplines to drive research, education and impact." The announcement itself doesn't name the 11 departments or address the campaign directly, so what day-to-day teaching and research autonomy looks like under the new structure is still worth an applicant's own due diligence — but the headline uncertainty about whether the department would survive at all appears to be resolved.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

Two genuinely new items this week, both on postnatal depression — this newsletter's standing priority — rather than psychodynamic therapy specifically.

New this week: does brief talking therapy in pregnancy protect against depression a year after birth? A randomised controlled trial in the Journal of Mood and Anxiety Disorders (Hankin, Demers, Grote et al., 2025/26) followed 230 pregnant people with depression, randomised to either brief Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT) or enhanced usual care, through to one year after birth. Those who received IPT had significantly lower depression scores throughout the postpartum year, and a Major Depressive Disorder rate of 4.3% at 12 months versus 12.9% for usual care — roughly a three-fold difference. In plain terms: a relatively brief course of talking therapy given during pregnancy, not after birth, looks like it keeps protecting against depression well into the following year.
New this week: which non-drug treatment works best for postpartum depression? A network meta-analysis in Iranian Journal of Psychiatry (Haq, Nurulhaq, Abdullah et al., 2026) pooled 13 randomised trials of non-drug treatments for postpartum depression and ranked them against each other. Therapist-assisted internet CBT (TA-iCBT) came out on top, with the largest effect and the most consistent evidence across studies. Worth the caveat this newsletter always applies to network meta-analyses of this size: only 13 trials feeds the comparison, and a smaller regional journal published it, so treat the ranking as a reasonable steer rather than the final word — but it's a genuine, new data point for anyone weighing therapy options after birth, alongside the IPT finding above.
