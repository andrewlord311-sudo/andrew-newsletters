🌳 The story: from Essex MA to the only therapy garden of its kind

Kate Beckwith graduated from the University of Essex's MA Psychodynamic Counselling and Psychotherapy with Distinction in 2023 — profiled by the university at the time — after an earlier career as a First Class Fine Art graduate running the university's Arts Education Team. She's since built Kate's Garden, a psychodynamic psychotherapy practice set in private woodland near Layer-de-la-Haye on the outskirts of Colchester that works with infants, children and young people outdoors — blending talking therapy with art, play, forest-school techniques and equine-assisted work. It's described as the only practice of its kind in the country. Beckwith has since taken the model to TEDxUniversity of Essex, arguing for a more holistic, dynamic model of child therapy than the traditional consulting room allows.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

Two genuinely new 2026 studies this week — both are results, not protocols, and both are the kind of long-run, large-sample data psychodynamic therapy doesn't always get credit for.

New this week: does internet-delivered psychodynamic therapy for teen depression last a year? A randomised controlled trial in Frontiers in Psychiatry followed 272 Swedish adolescents (aged 15–19) with depression for 12 months after they'd had either internet-delivered psychodynamic therapy (IPDT) or internet-delivered CBT (ICBT). Both groups held their improvement steady over the full year, with no meaningful difference between them on depression or anxiety scores — and no meaningful difference in cost either (roughly $2,500 per adolescent to deliver, and similar downstream healthcare costs at 12 months). The finding isn't that one approach beats the other; it's that a remotely-delivered psychodynamic treatment produces durable, affordable results for teenagers that hold up just as well as the more commonly-offered CBT alternative — useful evidence for anyone weighing which approach to make available for young people who won't or can't attend in person.
New this week: how do personality-focused talking therapies compare at scale? A 2026 study in Frontiers in Psychiatry, run across 16 specialist mental health units in the Norwegian Network for Personality Disorders, tracked 954 patients with personality disorders (565 on Mentalization-Based Treatment, a structured long-term psychodynamic approach; 389 on locally-adapted, non-manualized therapy) between 2017 and 2024. Both groups improved by a large margin on standard measures of personality functioning, and roughly half in each group saw their self-harm stop altogether. MBT pulled ahead specifically for patients with borderline personality disorder; the flexible, non-manualized approach did better for avoidant personality disorder. The headline isn't "MBT wins" — it's that a manualized psychodynamic treatment holds its own against real-world clinical flexibility at a scale (nearly a thousand patients, seven years of data) that's rare in this field, and that matching the approach to the specific personality disorder may matter more than which camp it comes from.
