🧭 The Fonagy review: diagnosis "must not be the primary gateway" to help

Professor Peter Fonagy's independent review for the government, published 9 October 2026, examines the scale of mental health, ADHD and autism need — among children and young people especially — and the strain it puts on existing services. It recommends a more flexible, needs-led approach: people should be able to get help based on what they are experiencing rather than waiting for a formal diagnosis first, while recognising diagnosis still matters for understanding experience and accessing some specific support. The Government has already responded, pledging earlier intervention and a stronger focus on prevention. The same day, BACP welcomed the review's direction, with Dr Lisa Morrison, its Chief Regulation and Standards Officer, calling it "one of the most significant policy reviews of the decade" and arguing that "a diagnosis can be valuable, but it must not be the primary gateway to getting timely access to mental health support."

Why it matters to practice here, not just in Whitehall BACP cites its own member survey — 97% of therapists said long diagnosis waits are a barrier for clients, 75% cited the cost of private assessment — and its estimate that trained counsellors and psychotherapists could provide roughly 100,000 extra therapy hours a week to the NHS if given the opportunity. Much of that capacity, including the practitioners listed below, sits outside the NHS and outside official data entirely — exactly the gap a needs-led system would need to draw on.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

On this newsletter's standing priority: a six-session therapy for mothers in disadvantaged circumstances A study in Frontiers in Psychology (Gomà, Arias-Pujol, Llairó, Prims, Ferrer, Sastre, Glover & Nanzer, published 25 March 2026) followed 160 pregnant women with depressive-anxiety symptoms at a primary care centre in a disadvantaged Barcelona neighbourhood, comparing six sessions of Parenthood-Centred Psychotherapy — a psychoanalytically-informed, manualised approach — against standard perinatal care. By 18 months the therapy group showed significantly lower depression and anxiety, a markedly better mother–infant relationship on a standard clinical rating scale (85.4 versus 67.7), and infants scoring better on three of five child-development domains; only 1.8% of their children were rated high-risk, against 44.1% in the comparison group. The authors describe the allocation as "quasi-randomised" — participants were assigned by order of recruitment rather than true randomisation — so the effect sizes deserve some caution, but the direction and consistency across outcomes is striking for a six-session intervention.
Psychodynamic therapy versus CBT, this time for social anxiety A meta-analysis in BMC Psychology (Norén et al., published March 2026) pooled 11 randomised controlled trials, 1,167 participants, of short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy for social anxiety disorder. In the subset of five trials comparing it head-to-head with CBT, the pooled difference was essentially zero (Hedges' g = 0.00, 95% CI −0.23 to 0.23) — no statistically significant advantage either way. The authors note the CBT comparison was a post-hoc addition rather than the study's main design, and trial quality varied, so this doesn't establish equivalence between the two — but it's another head-to-head result finding less daylight between psychodynamic and CBT approaches than their differing reputations might suggest.
