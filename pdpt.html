💼 "Keep Britain Working" — UKCP says therapy needs to be built in, not bolted on

Sir Charlie Mayfield's independent review for the government, Keep Britain Working, is tackling a problem the review itself calls "a quiet but urgent crisis": 2.8 million working-age people are now out of work long-term sick — 800,000 more than in 2019 — with poor mental health a significant driver, and Deloitte estimating the cost to UK employers at £51 billion a year. On 24 September 2026, UKCP responded to the review's latest update, welcoming the new Workplace Health System and arguing that "earlier intervention, personalised support and bringing together employers, the NHS, local authorities and mental health services must be central to this approach" — with "appropriate and tailored psychotherapeutic support" embedded in workplace health programmes through proper referral pathways, not added on afterwards.

Why it matters to practice here, not just in Whitehall If employer-funded referral routes into therapy expand off the back of this review, it changes how clients reach practitioners like the ones listed below — through workplace health schemes rather than self-referral or NHS waiting lists. Worth watching for anyone doing private or EAP-linked work locally.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

On this newsletter's standing priority: which therapy actually helps postnatal depression? A network meta-analysis in Archives of Women's Mental Health (Öztürk, Yüksel & Balmumcu, published 8 January 2026) pooled 8 randomised controlled trials, 2,919 participants, comparing six approaches to maternal depressive symptoms — CBT, mindfulness-based mobile interventions, music therapy, web-based interventions, WeChat-delivered counselling, and usual care — all measured on the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale. CBT was the only intervention that showed a statistically significant reduction in depressive symptoms against usual care, with the best overall ranking of the six. The authors flag substantial heterogeneity across the trials (83.6%) and call for larger, more methodologically consistent studies before treating this as the final word — but among talking therapies with trial evidence behind them for PND specifically, CBT currently has the strongest support.
What patients themselves say long-term psychodynamic therapy is like Barrett, Campbell, Luyten, Fonagy & Moser's qualitative meta-aggregation in the Journal of Counseling Psychology (2026) pulls together findings across multiple qualitative studies of patients who underwent long-term psychodynamic psychotherapy (9+ months, 30+ sessions) for mood and personality disorders. Rather than measuring symptom change, it synthesises patients' own accounts of the process — what the experience of extended psychodynamic work actually feels like from the inside, including what helped and what was difficult about it. A useful counterweight to outcome-measure research: the "does it work" question answered from the client's own description rather than a rating scale.
