🏥 What the government's NHS mental health plans actually mean for therapists

BACP used its 17 September Q&A to answer the questions members have actually been asking about the government's plan to expand community mental health services across England: "no wrong door or threshold" services meant to offer more than crisis management alone, delivered locally through NHS partnerships with charities and community interest companies. The numbers underneath are stark — a third of BACP members work in the third sector, and of those, a third say they can't maintain current provision levels while 86% are already operating beyond capacity. Two-thirds of members surveyed said they'd be interested in NHS employment, but the funded, salaried training route into it — the psychotherapeutic counselling core training pathway launched in 2022 — takes only around 60 trainees every three years.

Why this belongs here, not just in the member briefing This isn't Essex-specific, but it's the funding and workforce picture that charity-run local practices sit inside — the Stockwell Centre below has been a charity since 1976, and its situation is exactly the third-sector sustainability squeeze BACP is describing nationally. The Q&A also notes roughly 20 NHS services have now resolved pay equity issues worth about £10,000 a year per role, and sets out how the devolved nations differ: Wales made mental health a legal right in 2010, Scotland runs Health and Social Care Partnerships, and Northern Ireland began rolling out 17 Integrated Neighbourhood Teams from March 2026.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

Both new items this week bear directly on this newsletter's standing priority: mothers with postnatal depression.

New this week: a brief psychodynamic intervention for mothers in a disadvantaged population A trial in Frontiers in Psychology (Gomà et al., published 25 March 2026) tested "Parenthood-Centred Psychotherapy" — a brief psychoanalytic intervention working with a mother's internal beliefs about parenting and her relationship with her infant — in a deprived Barcelona neighbourhood (a deprivation index of 1.46, 56% migrants, 45% unemployment), replicating an earlier Geneva model with a proper control group for the first time. 160 pregnant women who screened positive for depression or anxiety were split 75 treatment/85 control; the treatment group showed significantly lower depression and anxiety that held up at 18 months, and — more striking — only 1.8% of their children showed cumulative developmental risk at 18 months, against 44.1% in the control group. It's one study in one specific population, but the child-development gap is large enough to be worth flagging rather than filing away.
New this week: interpersonal therapy for postnatal depression, out to nine months A systematic review and meta-analysis in the Journal of Psychiatric Research (2026) pooled 9 randomised controlled trials, 1,269 women, testing interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) against routine care for postpartum depression. IPT came out ahead at every follow-up point measured, from 4 weeks out to 9 months postpartum, with the effect holding up rather than fading — a reasonably robust answer, this time for IPT rather than psychodynamic work specifically, to the question of what actually helps in the months after birth.
