🤱 Nurses, midwives and doulas treated perinatal depression as well as specialists — for under half the cost

A secondary analysis of the SUMMIT trial, published this month in the Lancet Obstetrics, Gynaecology, and Women's Health (Singla et al.), is the strongest item this newsletter found all week, and it goes straight to the standing postnatal-depression priority rather than to a local story. The trial, led by the University of Toronto across Toronto, Chapel Hill and Chicago, had already shown that non-specialists — nurses, midwives and doulas with no prior psychotherapy training — could deliver a brief course of behavioural activation therapy to pregnant and postpartum adults with depression (EPDS ≥10) as effectively as mental health professionals with five-plus years' experience; this analysis follows the same 1,230 participants out to 12 months. Both groups' depression and anxiety scores had fallen to subclinical levels by then, with no meaningful difference by provider type or by whether sessions ran over telemedicine or in person — and non-specialist care cost around $442 per participant against roughly $1,020 for specialist delivery, with telemedicine cheaper again than in-person care. As Physician's Weekly's write-up notes, only 10–20% of people with perinatal depression currently get any evidence-based psychotherapy at all — the authors argue task-sharing and telemedicine are what could actually close that gap, provided training and supervision are funded properly. It's a US/Canadian trial, not a UK one — admissible here because the postnatal-depression priority is explicitly UK-wide-or-broader for research, and a good national (here, North American) finding beats a thin local one.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

What do therapists actually need to know to work well with people living with dementia? A new open-access paper in Counselling and Psychotherapy Research (Charura, Griffiths and colleagues), covered by BACP on 5 November, built a framework of therapist competences for working with people with dementia and their families — drawn from interviews and workshops with people living with dementia, family members, and counsellors, psychotherapists and psychologists, using tools including drawing, writing and Lego to help people share experiences that are hard to put into words. The headline finding is uncomfortable: people with dementia value therapy, but experiences are inconsistent — some felt understood, others had to keep re-explaining their diagnosis or felt services simply fell short. The resulting framework runs from a broad baseline understanding (how dementia affects thinking, emotion and communication; adapting sessions so they're easier to follow; building trust; involving family where helpful) up to specialist-level skills, and is aimed at therapists, supervisors, trainers and service managers rather than being an official BACP competence framework itself. Only one genuinely new research item turned up this week beyond the perinatal-depression trial above — not padding this section with a repeat is the point.
