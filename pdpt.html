🕰️ Fifty years quietly training Colchester's therapists

The Stockwell Centre, on East Stockwell Street in the heart of Colchester, was founded by Dr Deirdre Paulley in 1976 — which makes 2026 its fiftieth year as a working charity. It's registered with the Charity Commission as The Stockwell Centre (Colchester) Limited, charity number 291433, and today runs around ten psychodynamically-trained therapists seeing clients from the same building. It's also, this year, home to something newer: Dr Syd Hiskey, a consultant clinical psychologist who trained at Essex and UCL and now splits a CBT, EMDR and Compassion Focused Therapy practice between the Centre and The Oaks Hospital on the edge of town.

Worth knowing Fifty years without a rebrand or a name change is unusual for a small mental-health charity — most either fold, merge, or get absorbed into a larger provider long before that. That Stockwell now houses a CBT-trained clinical psychologist alongside its founding psychodynamic therapists, under the same roof, is a small but concrete example of the modality-mixing this newsletter's scope was widened to track: psychodynamic as the anchor, other evidence-based talking therapies increasingly sitting alongside it in the same local practices.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

Two genuinely new 2026 papers this week, both focused on depression — one revisiting an existing trial's data more closely, the other pooling results across trials for the first time.

New this week: does intensive short-term dynamic psychotherapy's effect on depression keep growing? A reanalysis in Frontiers in Psychiatry revisited the public dataset from the Halifax Depression Study — 86 adults with treatment-resistant depression randomised to either 20 sessions of Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy (ISTDP) or a waitlist. The original large effect on depression symptoms didn't fade after treatment ended; it grew, from a large effect straight after treatment (d = 1.68) to a very large one three months later (d = 2.50). The more interesting finding for clinicians is what didn't hold up: ISTDP theory predicts a specific sequence, defences softening first, then buried feelings surfacing, then symptoms lifting — but the reanalysis found no evidence patients actually improved in that order. Instead, people seemed to get better broadly and simultaneously across several fronts at once, which is a real challenge to how the therapy's own mechanism is usually explained, even as the outcome itself looks strong.
New this week: do "embodied" psychodynamic techniques help with depression? A systematic review and meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology, from the Psychotherapy Science research group at Sigmund Freud Private University in Vienna, pooled randomised trials of psychodynamic treatments that work explicitly through the body — techniques using posture, breath, physical sensation and movement as routes into a patient's inner life, rather than talk alone. Set against control conditions and other established therapies, embodied psychodynamic approaches showed a genuine benefit for depressive symptoms. It's an early but useful piece of evidence that body-aware technique isn't a fringe add-on to classical psychodynamic work — it has its own, now systematically reviewed, evidence base for one of the commonest reasons people seek therapy in the first place.
