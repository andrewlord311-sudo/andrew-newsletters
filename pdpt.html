🧭 BACP takes its case to the party conferences

Following on from last week's coverage of Professor Peter Fonagy's government review and BACP's response to it, BACP spent this year's autumn party conference season pressing the same argument on people who write manifestos rather than policy papers. BACP's own account of the conferences describes a mixed field: at Labour's conference, Prime Minister Andy Burnham pledged that young people should no longer face long waits for mental health support, as part of a wider package for those not in education or employment, and BACP hosted a roundtable there — chaired by its Vice President, Baroness Berger — bringing together service providers, youth and employment groups, and people with lived experience to discuss how counselling fits into joined-up support. The Liberal Democrats, Greens and Plaid Cymru each called for a more preventative, patient-centred approach to care, while the Conservatives and Reform UK's conference messaging focused on tightening sickness-benefit and welfare thresholds instead.

The line BACP is running across all of it BACP's Policy and Public Affairs Lead, Martin Bell, argues that counsellors and psychotherapists are a "highly mobile yet underutilised membership" — a workforce that should be factored into policy, commissioning and investment decisions as part of preventive mental health care, rather than treated as a resource outside the system. It's the same argument as last week's BACP response to the Fonagy review, now aimed at party platforms rather than a government review.

🔬 Research worth knowing about — a thin week

On this newsletter's standing priority: does group prenatal care beat individual care for postnatal depression? The EMBRACE randomised clinical trial (Felder, León-Martínez, Karasek et al., JAMA Network Open, published 16 February 2026) randomised 674 low-income pregnant people — most of them Latine — across clinics in California's San Joaquin Valley to either enhanced group prenatal care (which added childcare, transport stipends, free groceries, mental health screening and referrals on top of standard care) or enhanced individual prenatal care. Depressive symptoms fell meaningfully in both arms between early pregnancy and three months postpartum, but the group-care arm's larger average improvement (a 2.2-point drop on the PHQ-9 depression scale, against 1.6 in the individual-care arm) was not statistically significant — in plain terms, the richer, more resource-intensive group model did not actually outperform the simpler individual one on this measure. Worth knowing for anyone assuming group support must automatically do more than one-to-one support.
