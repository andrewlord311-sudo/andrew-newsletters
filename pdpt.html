🆘 A national helpline closes — what it means for the local safety net

Papyrus Prevention of Young Suicide, the charity behind HOPELINE247 — a UK-wide helpline for people under 35 having thoughts of suicide, reachable by phone, text or email with no referral needed — collapsed into administration on 8 September 2026, with immediate effect. All 124 staff were made redundant on the day; joint administrators from Opus Restructuring & Insolvency were appointed to wind the charity down. Civil Society's report cites a £1.59m deficit for the year to March 2025 and falling donation, legacy and investment income as the immediate cause, on top of "a reduction in reliable funding across the sector." UKCP responded on 10 September, calling it a charity that had "played a vital role in suicide prevention and in supporting children, young people, families and professionals across the UK for almost three decades" — its CEO Jon Levett adding that "suicide prevention cannot depend on organisations operating under financial pressure."

Why this belongs here, not just in the national press Locally, EPUT's CAMHS service in Colchester — based at the St Aubyn Centre — takes referrals only through a crisis or community mental health team; HOPELINE247 was one of the few routes into support here that needed none of that. Some of its functions "may still be transferred to new partners if possible," per Civil Society's report, but nothing has been confirmed yet. Worth knowing if it comes up with a client: the gap is real for now.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

On this newsletter's standing priority: screening and psychotherapy for postnatal depression A systematic review in European Psychiatry (Valentina, published 31 July 2026, searching the literature through August 2025) found that early screening followed by psychotherapeutic intervention significantly reduced depressive symptoms and improved maternal–infant bonding compared with standard care. The most consistent benefits came from CBT or relational approaches started within six months of birth, and telepsychotherapy performed comparably to in-person sessions — relevant to anywhere access to a specialist in person is patchy. The review also recommends validated screening tools such as the EPDS as standard practice across perinatal services, rather than an optional extra.
New this week: does working with the body change psychodynamic outcomes for depression? A meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology (Heider, Sulz & Franzen, published 26 March 2026) pooled 11 randomised controlled trials, 1,624 participants, on psychodynamic therapies that explicitly work with bodily experience — noticing physical sensations, divergences between what a client says and how they hold themselves, and connecting bodily experience to underlying dynamics. Against control conditions, these approaches showed a statistically significant benefit for depression (SMD −0.53), with the effect growing rather than fading by follow-up, and performed on a par with established therapies. A specific, technical answer to a specific question, not a headline result — but a useful one for anyone weighing how much embodied technique to bring into psychodynamic work.
