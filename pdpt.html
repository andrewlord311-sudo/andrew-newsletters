🏛️ The Milburn review wants therapy for NEET young people fast-tracked — BACP and UKCP agree on the need, split on the conditions

Alan Milburn's report From a Welfare State to a Working State, on young people not in education, employment or training, proposes moving away from a "diagnose-wait-treat" model towards earlier, needs-led mental health support delivered through a new Youth Opportunity Service — digital-first triage, with NHS Talking Therapies and brief in-person counselling (including for difficulties linked to possible or diagnosed ADHD or autism) for those it can't reach online. BACP's response welcomes the recognition of counselling's role and repeats its own long-standing call for sustainable government investment and for the existing qualified workforce — which its recent Commission report found has untapped capacity — to be put to use rather than overlooked. UKCP's response is warmer about the principle than the mechanism: it welcomes needs-led help that doesn't require a diagnosis, but warns that benefit sanctions for young people who don't engage, support made conditional on a "participation plan," an over-reliance on digital and AI-assisted tools in place of trained practitioners, and a Youth Opportunity Service left to vary community by community could all work against the young people it's meant to help. Both stress that none of this is government policy yet — it still needs parliamentary agreement.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

For social anxiety, short-term psychodynamic therapy and CBT may be closer than the field's pecking order suggests A new meta-analysis in BMC Psychology (Norén, Lindqvist, Lilliengren and Mechler) pooled 11 randomised controlled trials totalling 1,167 people with social anxiety disorder. Against inactive controls, short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy did clearly better, with gains that held — and in some cases kept improving — at follow-up. The more pointed question is how it stacks up against CBT, usually treated as the default choice for social anxiety: across the five trials that made that comparison directly, the pooled difference was essentially zero (g = 0.00, 95% CI −0.23 to 0.23). The authors are careful not to oversell that: the CBT comparison was a post-hoc subset of the full review, the trials varied in quality, and several were small, so this reads as "no evidence of a difference" rather than proof the two are equivalent. Taken with that caveat, it's still a genuinely new data point for a therapy that is routinely ranked below CBT for this condition, and the authors call for more head-to-head trials with active comparators.

BACP · BACP News · UKCP · UKCP News · BABCP. Beyond the Milburn review above, BACP's 2026 AGM, held 20 November, carried all eight motions and resolutions on the table — a Blue Light Card discount scheme for counsellors passed comfortably (1,408 for, 84 against), a new Articles of Association was adopted, and the closest-run item was a Coaching Division motion to introduce "Coach Therapist" and "Therapeutic Coach" descriptors (706 for, 440 against, 464 abstaining). The day before, BACP published its 2026 membership survey (2,487 responses): members trust BACP to represent the profession (63%) and see membership as important (69%) far more than they're satisfied with it (48%) or feel listened to by it (37%) — a real gap between standing and experience. And on 12 November, BACP's Dr Lisa Morrison gave evidence to the parliamentary committee on the Conversion Practices Bill, asking for a clear legal definition of "serious distress" so it isn't confused with the ordinary discomfort of therapy, and for the bill to cover unregistered as well as regulated therapists. UKCP's own governance business, a General Meeting on 27 November to vote on amended Articles of Association, is still ahead rather than reported. BABCP's news page turned up nothing new this week with an Essex angle or otherwise.
