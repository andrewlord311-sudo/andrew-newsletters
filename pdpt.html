⚖️ Watchdog finds Essex NHS board broke the rules awarding a talking-therapies contract

NHS England's Independent Patient Choice and Procurement Panel has found that Mid and South Essex Integrated Care Board broke the Provider Selection Regime in six separate ways when it awarded a contract for NHS Talking Therapies and psychological therapies for severe mental health problems to the private provider Vita Health in May 2025. Reporting from The Lowdown and a procurement-law analysis from Ward Hadaway set out what the panel found: bidders were given an unfair, non-level playing field to be assessed on, only 21 working days (plus a two-day extension) to respond given the size and complexity of the contract, and a budget that didn't cover the TUPE-transferred staffing costs bidders were required to take on. The panel's recommendation was blunt — re-run the flawed parts of the process, or abandon it and start again.

Worth knowing The complaint that triggered this review came from the incumbent providers, including Essex Partnership University NHS Foundation Trust (EPUT) — the same trust building the new Colchester mental health crisis unit covered in a previous issue. This particular contract covers Mid & South Essex, not the North East Essex patch that includes Colchester, so it doesn't directly change anyone's local referral here — but it's a live illustration of how fragile NHS talking-therapies commissioning can be across the county, and EPUT's own capacity is shaped by contracts like this one. Subsequent tender records show the ICB re-ran the procurement through 2026, with a fresh award decision reached in June.

New this week: does short-term psychodynamic therapy help with social anxiety? A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials in BMC Psychology pooled 11 trials (1,167 patients) of short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy (STPP) for social anxiety disorder. Against a waitlist — i.e. people who got no treatment yet — STPP produced a large improvement in social anxiety symptoms. But against other active, "bona fide" treatments (nine trials), there was no meaningful difference either way, and dropout rates were similar across the board. In plain terms: STPP looks like a genuinely effective option for social anxiety, holding its gains at follow-up, but this study gives no evidence that it works better than CBT or other established therapies — only that it's a reasonable alternative to doing nothing.
