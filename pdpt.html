🧭 Independent Commission calls for statutory regulation and a new therapy federation

The final report of the Commission for the Future of Counselling and Psychotherapy, set up by the Partnership of Counselling and Psychotherapy Bodies (PCPB — which includes BACP, UKCP and BABCP), landed in the past week and drew swift responses from both BACP and UKCP. Built on evidence from over 4,000 practitioners, employers, commissioners, researchers and people with lived experience, it estimates around 14 million people across the UK have mental health problems against 82,000 qualified counsellors and psychotherapists on accredited registers — and argues that using just 10% of the profession's spare capacity could give therapy to over 150,000 more people a year for roughly 0.56% of the UK mental health budget. Its six priorities: wider commissioning and funding, statutory regulation to strengthen public protection, workforce development, more research and evaluation, better public/political recognition of the profession, and a new Counselling and Psychotherapy Federation to speak with one voice. BACP's response called it "a shared platform for practical change" but wants any regulation to stay proportionate and not create new barriers to access; UKCP's response went further, backing statutory regulation outright and calling for psychotherapists to be included in NHS multidisciplinary teams.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

Can six sessions of psychotherapy around pregnancy and birth change a child's development two years on? A trial published in Frontiers in Psychology in March 2026 (Gomà, Arias-Pujol, Llairó, Prims, Ferrer, Sastre, Glover and Nanzer) tested "Parenthood-Centred Psychotherapy" — three sessions in pregnancy and three with mother and baby in the first six months after birth — against standard care, in 160 women with depression or anxiety symptoms recruited at a disadvantaged primary care centre in Barcelona. By 18 months, mothers who had the therapy scored lower on depression and anxiety, their children did better on three of five developmental measures (communication, gross motor skills, problem-solving), and on a combined risk measure only 1.8% of their children were rated high-risk versus 44.1% in the control group. The caveats matter: allocation wasn't fully randomised, the groups differed slightly at baseline, and the therapy needs specially trained practitioners — but it's a genuinely new data point for brief psychodynamic-informed work around the postnatal period, not just long-term therapy.
Does brief psychoanalytic therapy hold up against CBT for generalised anxiety disorder? A new randomised controlled trial in the Journal of Clinical Medicine (Çınaroğlu, Yılmazer, Ülker and Hızlı Sayar, a Beykoz University team) randomised 60 adults with diagnosed GAD to 12 weekly sessions of brief psychoanalytic psychotherapy, 12 weekly sessions of CBT, or a waitlist. Anxiety scores on the Beck Anxiety Inventory fell by roughly 14.5 points in the psychoanalytic arm and 16.3 points in the CBT arm — both far ahead of the waitlist, with no statistically significant difference between the two active treatments on any outcome, including therapeutic alliance. The authors' own framing is the useful bit: head-to-head trials of psychodynamic therapy against CBT for GAD specifically have been rare, so this — short-term only, 60 participants — is a small but real addition to that gap rather than a rehash of the social-anxiety meta-analysis covered here previously.
