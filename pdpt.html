🌳 The story: from Essex MA to the only therapy garden of its kind

Kate Beckwith graduated from the University of Essex's MA Psychodynamic Counselling and Psychotherapy with Distinction in 2023 — profiled by the university at the time — after an earlier career as a First Class Fine Art graduate running the university's Arts Education Team. She's since built Kate's Garden, a psychodynamic psychotherapy practice set in private woodland on the outskirts of Colchester that works with infants, children and young people outdoors — blending talking therapy with art, play, forest-school techniques and equine-assisted work. It's described as the only practice of its kind in the country. Beckwith has since taken the model to TEDxUniversity of Essex, arguing for a more holistic, dynamic model of child therapy than the traditional consulting room allows.

Worth knowing This is a good example of what an Essex-trained psychodynamic practitioner can build locally: Kate's Garden isn't a franchise or a directory listing, it's a genuinely novel service model that grew directly out of the local MA programme covered in the training section below.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

One genuinely new 2026 item this week, on an angle psychodynamic research has under-explored: the body itself as a route into depression treatment.

Does paying attention to the body help psychodynamic treatment for depression? A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology (Heider, Sulz & Franzen) pooled 11 randomised trials (1,624 patients) of psychodynamic treatments that take an explicitly "embodied" approach — using bodily sensations and the gap between what a patient says and how their body behaves as diagnostic and therapeutic material, rather than working purely through talk. Against control conditions, the effect was moderate straight after treatment (SMD −0.53) and grew larger by follow-up (SMD −1.23) — and it held up as equivalent to CBT and other established treatments head-to-head. The authors are careful to call this preliminary rather than conclusive, but it's a plain data point that the body-focused strand of psychodynamic practice isn't just theory — it moves the needle, and increasingly so after treatment ends.
