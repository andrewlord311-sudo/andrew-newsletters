🪑 New Chair takes over at the NHS trust behind Colchester's talking therapies, as CQC report lands

Essex Partnership University NHS Foundation Trust (EPUT) announced that Jackie Craissati took up post as its new Chair on 1 September 2026. Craissati is a Consultant Clinical and Forensic Psychologist with over 30 years in the NHS, previously Clinical Director for the Forensic and Prisons Directorate at Oxleas NHS Foundation Trust, and she continues to chair Dartford and Gravesham NHS Trust and the charity Crohn's and Colitis UK. Interim Chief Executive Trevor Smith called it "a critical time in the transformation of services" for the trust. EPUT is the organisation behind Therapy for You, the NHS Talking Therapies service covering Colchester and North East Essex.

Worth knowing The appointment lands the same week EPUT published the CQC's report following a Well-Led inspection of the trust. The trust's own announcement doesn't state the headline rating, but says it "welcomes the CQC's feedback as an opportunity to recognise areas for ongoing improvement" and points to recent developments including ward redesigns, the new mental health urgent care centres (the Kingswood Centre at Colchester Hospital among them), and a rollout of electronic patient records. Worth checking the CQC's own page for EPUT directly if the rating itself matters to you.

🔬 Research worth knowing about

Two genuinely new items this week — one psychodynamic, one CBT — both meta-analyses/trials that hadn't appeared in previous issues.

New this week: does "embodied" psychodynamic therapy help with depression? A systematic review and meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology (Heider, Sulz & Franzen, 2026) looked at psychodynamic treatments that work explicitly through the body — using bodily sensations diagnostically, noticing mismatches between what a patient says and what their body shows, and linking physical sensation to psychological pattern. Across 11 RCTs (1,624 patients), this "embodied" approach produced a meaningful improvement in depression symptoms versus control conditions, with gains that grew larger at 42-week follow-up. Against other established therapies like CBT, there was no meaningful difference. In plain terms: promising and durable, but — same caveat as last week's psychodynamic study — evidence it works, not evidence it works better than what's already offered.
New this week: NHS-funded trial of therapist-led online CBT for depression The INTERACT trial, published in The Lancet Primary Care (Bristol-led, NIHR-funded), tested a version of CBT that combines one-to-one therapist messaging with structured online worksheets, against usual GP care, in 451 adults with depression recruited from 67 GP practices. The combined approach produced depression scores roughly 4.4 points lower than usual care, with the improvement holding up at 12 months. The pitch isn't a new therapy — it's a new way of delivering CBT that needs less therapist time per patient while keeping a real person in the loop, aimed at the access problem as much as the treatment itself.
