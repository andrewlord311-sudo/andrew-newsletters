🧩 Sudoku variantsTwo grids built on whispers lines, and one where you have to invent the sets yourself

Gentle Set Thursday · 13 solves already

Diamond in the Middle

by StrandofIvy, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published 5 November 2026 at 17:10; the setter grades it level 2 of 5, an easy puzzle, and thirteen people had solved it by Friday morning
Five constraints, all in the portal's own English wording, and none of them exotic. Normal Sudoku: place the digits 1–9 in every row, column, and 3×3 box without repeats. Anti-Knight: cells separated by a chess knight's move cannot contain the same digit. German Whispers: adjacent digits along a green line must differ by at least 5. Kropki Dots: cells separated by a white dot contain consecutive digits. V and X Clues: a V between two adjacent cells means their digits sum to 5. An X means their digits sum to 10. Not all possible dots, Vs or Xs are necessarily given.
The whispers line is the engine, as it always is: a difference of five or more pushes every cell on the line to one extreme or the other, and bans 5 from the line outright, since nothing is five away from a 5 in both directions. What makes this one gentle is that the other four rules all pull in the same direction rather than fighting. A white dot wants a gap of exactly one, so a dot touching the line is nearly always a contradiction waiting to be read off. V and X are small-sum clues, which means they live at the low end — the same end the line is already forcing. And anti-knight quietly does the tidying: once the extremes are placed, it stops them repeating anywhere within a knight's move, which propagates a long way across a grid this constrained without you having to do any arithmetic at all.
Why it's here: whispers plus kropki is the pairing this newsletter's ratings like best, and this is the cheapest version of it on the board — a twenty-minute puzzle to start the weekend on. StrandofIvy has not appeared here before, and this is not a Cracking the Cryptic pick: it went up on the Rätselportal on Thursday evening and has never been near a video.

Medium Set at 2am Friday

Mystery Islands

by Killer Joe, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published 6 November 2026 at 02:52, a few hours before this issue went out; the setter grades it level 3 of 5, a medium puzzle
Normal sudoku, and then a rule that hands you part of the puzzle's own definition. The digits 1–9 split into three “mystery sets” of three digits each — and which digits go in which set is for you to work out. A circled digit gives the size of an orthogonally connected group of cells whose digits all belong to that digit's own mystery set. No group contains more than one circled cell, and not every group has a circle. Digits separated by a red diamond must not be in a 2:1 ratio.
This is the opposite of the grid above, and worth the switch. There are no lines and almost no arithmetic; instead the constraint is about regions you cannot see yet, defined by a partition you have not been told. A circled 4 means the four cells around it, connected orthogonally, all draw from the same three-digit set — so the circle is simultaneously telling you a shape and a fact about the partition, and neither is any use without the other. The way in is the clause that sounds like a footnote: no group contains more than one circled cell. That stops two circles sharing an island, which turns nearby circles into evidence that their digits sit in different sets, and from a handful of those you can start pinning the partition down. The red diamonds are the brake — they rule out 1/2, 2/4, 3/6 and 4/8 pairings exactly where you most want them.
Why it's here: the ratings favour lines, and this deliberately isn't one — five weeks of lines earns one grid that works a different muscle, where the deduction is set-theoretic rather than arithmetic. Hours old, no video, and the sort of invented constraint that only really exists on the portal.

Medium Three magic squares, position unknown

Three Square Trick

by Ali M, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published 5 November 2026 at 15:59; the setter grades it level 3 of 5, a medium puzzle, though the final deduction is the heaviest lift of the three here
Normal sudoku, a familiar stack of clues, and then one unusual global rule. Along a green line, adjacent digits must have a difference of at least 5. Digits within a cage sum to the total in that cage's upper-left corner. A white dot between two digits means they are consecutive; an X means they sum to 10; a V means they sum to 5. Not every possible dot, X or V is shown. And the global one: besides the nine standard 3×3 boxes, the grid contains three hidden 3×3 regions, each a magic square using 1–9 once, with every row, every column and both main diagonals summing to the same value. The hidden regions may overlap each other, but none sits in the same position as a standard box.
The thing to know before you start is that a 3×3 magic square on 1–9 is almost completely rigid. Its common sum has to be 15, because the nine digits total 45 and three rows share that equally; the centre cell is forced to 5; the even digits take the corners and the odd ones the edges. Every such square is the same square up to rotation and reflection — there are eight of them and no others. So “there is a hidden magic square here” is a far stronger statement than it looks: find one cell of one, know it holds 5, and you have its centre and a very short list of possible surroundings. The puzzle's difficulty is therefore not solving the magic squares but locating them, and the whispers line and cages are what narrow the candidate positions — a whispers line cannot pass through a magic square's centre, for instance, because that cell is a 5.
Why it's here: cages and a whispers line are both on the 👍 list, and the magic-square overlay is the rare kind of extra rule that adds a genuine theorem to the solve rather than another arithmetic chore. Ali M is new to this newsletter.

🌍 The wider puzzle worldA nonogram welded to a loop puzzle, and a Nikoli genre you can learn in ninety seconds

Monster 5/5 · one solver so far

A Walk Through the Frozen Forest

by Nell Gwyn, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published 5 November 2026 at 20:30; graded 5 of 5, a very difficult puzzle, by the setter. Solved once. Not a sudoku: there are no digits 1–9 and no boxes anywhere in it
Two separate pencil-puzzle genres share one grid and constrain each other. Two-Colour Nonogram: each cell is shaded blue for ice, shaded green for forest, or left unshaded, and the row and column clues give the run lengths in each colour. Summation U-Bahn: a single connected loop runs through cell centres — it may branch and may cross itself, and it has no dead ends. Each cell is scored by the shape the line makes in it: unvisited 0, a corner 1, a straight 2, a branch 3, a crossing 4. The clues give the sums of those scores along runs of visited cells.
Then the two halves are wired together, and this is the part that makes it a single puzzle rather than two: a green cell must hold a branch or else be unvisited; a blue cell can hold neither a branch nor a corner; an unshaded cell can hold neither a branch nor a crossing. Number clues sit on unshaded visited cells and give the length of the unshaded stretch of path running through them.
Read those three interaction rules again and you can see the whole puzzle in them. Blue can hold no corner, so ice is where the line goes dead straight — a blue run is a tramline. Green is the only place a branch can live, so every junction in the network is standing in a tree. And unshaded cells take no branch and no crossing, so bare ground carries only plain line. That means the shading is the wiring diagram: deduce a run of blue and you have deduced that the path through it cannot turn, which feeds straight back into the nonogram clues. The deductions cross from one genre to the other on nearly every move, which is why one person has finished it.
Why it's here: five weeks of wider-world picks have been archives and generators — somebody's back catalogue, or an endless supply of machine-made grids. This is a single composed puzzle set on Thursday night by a human being who thought a two-colour nonogram should also be a branching loop puzzle, and it exists nowhere else. Start with the 7×7 example: it teaches the scoring in about five minutes and is the difference between a hard evening and a wasted one.

Gentle New genre · rules from the inventor

Five Cells, from Nikoli itself

at nikoli.co.jp/en — the Japanese publisher that gave sudoku its modern form and named most of the pencil-puzzle genres anyone solves today. Rules in English, with a worked sample and its solution on the page
Four rules, quoted as Nikoli states them. Divide the grid into blocks of five cells. Blocks with fewer than five or more than five cells are not allowed. A number in a cell gives the count of block lines touching that cell — the outer frame's lines count toward the number. A block may contain up to five numbered cells. Lines must enclose a cell and may not pass into a block.
It is a pentomino-tiling puzzle wearing a very plain coat, and the clue type is the clever bit: a number counts walls, not cells. So a 0 means a cell is in the middle of its block with no boundary touching it on any side, a 4 means a cell is its block's lone outpost — impossible, since blocks have five cells, which is the kind of contradiction that cracks a corner open. A 3 on an edge cell is already mostly determined, because the frame is doing some of the counting for it. You work in walls and the answer arrives as shapes, which is a genuinely different sensation from counting cells, and after one puzzle the clue numbers stop looking arbitrary.
Why it's here: a deliberate ninety-second pick to sit beside the monster above. Nikoli lists over forty genres in English with the rules on each page, and Five Cells is one of the ones that never made it out of Japan into the apps — no daily, no app, no channel. Print the sample, or draw the grid out; it is pencil-and-paper rather than interactive, which this week is the point. If it clicks, the genre index is the best map of the unexplored parts of this hobby that exists.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekIBM's November challenge — this one wants a few lines of code, not a pencil

"The SumSum" — IBM Research, Ponder This, November 2026
Take a sequence a₀, a₁, …, aN−1. Delete the first element, then replace each of the rest by the partial sums of what remains. Repeat that remove-and-sum step until a single element is left: that value is the sequence's SumSum. For 1, 2, …, 10 the SumSum is 16,796. Your task: for N = 1010 and p = 1010 + 19, find the SumSum modulo p of the sequence ai = C(N, i), the binomial coefficients. Bonus: for N = 1011 and p = 1011 + 3, do the same for ai = 13i.
A change of pace after last week's pencil-and-paper proof: this is Ponder This, so brute force is deliberately hopeless and the puzzle is really asking you to find the closed form hiding in that repeated partial-summing. Two things are worth noticing before writing any code. The first is 16,796 — that is the tenth Catalan number, which is not a coincidence and is the single biggest hint on the page. The second is that N is 1010, so whatever you find has to be evaluated without ever touching the sequence itself; the modulus is prime, which is the organisers telling you which tools they expect. IBM posts the solution at the start of the following month, and solvers' names go up as they come in — there were already entries dated 1 and 2 November, so somebody solved this within a day of it going live.
