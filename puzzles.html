🧩 Sudoku variantsA gentle colour-and-sandwich hybrid, a 7×7 with leaves instead of boxes, and a genuinely nasty one

Gentle Posted 31 August

Coloured Sudoku #4

by Sudonym, Logic Masters Deutschland — rated level 1 (very easy) by solvers, with a 93% approval score
Normal sudoku rules, plus three layers that between them do most of the work for you. Cells sharing a colour hold the same digit, so one placement propagates across the grid. Numbers outside the grid are sandwich sums — the total of the digits sitting strictly between the 1 and the 9 in that row or column, which pins down how far apart the 1 and the 9 must be. And there is only one 9 across the grid's two long diagonals between them, which is the clue that unlocks the rest. Twenty minutes, not two hours.
Why it's here: a first-time-friendly way into two variants at once, from a setter who posts straight to the German portal and has never had a video made about them.

Medium Posted this morning

7×7 Clover Sudoku #5

by Todd, Logic Masters Deutschland — published 1 September, rated 3/5 for difficulty
Not a 9×9 at all. Place the digits 1 to 7 so each appears exactly once in every row and every column — and then the boxes are gone, replaced by four leaf-shaped regions, each of which also holds 1 to 7 exactly once. Cells outside the leaves are constrained only by their row and column. Losing the 3×3 boxes removes the crutch you lean on without noticing in ordinary sudoku, and the overlap between leaf and row is where the whole solve lives.
Why it's here: irregular-shape Latin squares almost never surface on the daily-app circuit, and this is #5 in a series the setter is building out quietly.

Tough Artisanal Sudoku

Pickle Jar — and the two warm-ups that come with it

by James Sinclair, Artisanal Sudoku Volume 243, published 31 August 2026 — the setter rates it 8/10
Six constraints on one grid. German whispers: neighbouring digits on a green line differ by at least 5, so no 5 ever sits on one. Quadruple circles: each digit printed in a circle at a four-cell junction must appear somewhere in those four cells. V and X pairs: cells joined by a V sum to 5, by an X to 10. Shaded cells are even. And no digit repeats along the diagonal. The volume is built as a ladder — Lost Thread (cages and quadruples, 3/10) and Short Track (arrows and white dots, 4/10) come first, and there is a gentler 5/10 version of Pickle Jar with extra clues if the full one bites.
Why it's here: a weekly three-puzzle set from an independent setter publishing direct to subscribers — no channel, no algorithm, and a different platform from the two picks above.

Simon Tatham's Portable Puzzle Collection

Maintained by hand since the late 1990s — free, no account, no ads, and it runs in the browser
Forty-five-odd single-player logic games, each generating fresh puzzles on demand at a size and difficulty you choose. The pick of the constraint-logic ones: Keen — fill an n×n grid so every digit from 1 to n appears once per row and column, with the grid carved into blocks carrying arithmetic clues. A block's digits must add or multiply to its number; two-cell blocks can instead be subtraction (one digit exceeds the other by the clue) or division (one divides by the other to give it). The twist that catches people: a block may repeat a digit, so long as the repeats aren't in the same row or column. Beyond it sit Loopy and Pearl for loop-drawing, Towers for skyscrapers, Palisade and Mosaic for region division, and Light Up, Magnets, Slant and Unequal after that.
Why it's here: it predates the entire daily-puzzle-app economy and has never been part of it — no streak, no leaderboard, no puzzle of the day. You just pick a genre and it makes you as many as you want, forever.

Monster World Puzzle Federation

The Grand Prix booklets — championship rounds, free and unlocked

WPF Sudoku GP 2026, round 8 (hosted by France) and Puzzle GP 2026, round 8 (hosted by India, set by Prasanna Seshadri)
The competition windows have closed, which is the good news: the Sudoku GP round 8 booklet is open — seven pages, with a full solutions booklet alongside it. Five classic sudoku of rising weight, then the variants: Anti-Knight (digits a knight's move apart must differ), Kropki (white circle = difference of 1, black = ratio of 2, and an unmarked edge means neither), Clone, and the odd one — 1-5-9 Column Indexer: if digit X sits in a grey cell in column Y of some row, then in that same row, digit Y sits in column X. Ninety minutes was the competition allowance; take the weekend instead. The Puzzle GP's own booklet is still password-locked, but its instruction booklet is free and is a small education in itself — Nurikabe, Nuribou, Nanro, irregular Heyawake, Yajilin, Castle Wall, Pentopia, Nori Chain and Number Rope, each with the exact rules and a worked example. (One round genre is word-based; skip it.)
Why it's here: this is what the people who win world championships were solving three weeks ago, published free as PDFs on a site with no promotion attached to it whatsoever.

Medium Dormant archive

Puzzles for Progress — 362 free puzzles, nobody looking after them

by Jacob Cohen — the count is his own, from October 2023; the last new post went up in June 2025
A one-person site with an archive far larger than its profile. Everything is free and, unusually for a hand-set archive, every puzzle comes three ways: a printable PDF, a penpa link and a puzz.link link, so you solve in the browser with proper tools rather than squinting at an image. A good entry point is his set of three from a Logic Masters India contest he co-wrote — a Star Battle, a Nurikabe and a Slitherlink, difficulty 1, 1 and 2 respectively. Worth knowing before you browse: the archive spans thirty-odd genres and a fair number of them are word puzzles, so stay in the logic categories.
Why it's here: a dormant archive is a feature, not a bug — 362 puzzles that aren't going anywhere, aren't expiring tonight, and won't turn up in anyone's feed.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekA one-line problem from a competition-training classic

"Tile Swap" — Futility Closet, from Arthur Engel's Problem-Solving Strategies (1998)
You're tiling a floor with 4×1 and 2×2 tiles when you accidentally break one. A tile of the other shape is available. Show that it's not possible to cover the floor by rearranging the tiles.
No grid, no numbers, no arithmetic — and no amount of shuffling tiles about will get you there, because the thing you're being asked for is a proof of impossibility. Everything turns on finding the right way to mark the floor before you start. It is one of the neatest arguments in the whole genre and takes about three lines once it lands. Futility Closet keeps the answer behind a click on the same page, so nothing is spoiled by opening the link and having a think first.
