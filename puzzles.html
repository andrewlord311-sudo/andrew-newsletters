🧩 Sudoku variantsA greater-than-by-two rule, a line that does arithmetic in two digits, and a grid that rotates while you solve it

Gentle New setter here

Fivetress Sudoku

by Bill Murphy, at Grandmaster Puzzles — published 14 October 2026, his 168th puzzle there, in the Genuinely Approachable Sudoku series
Normal sudoku, plus exactly one extra rule and nothing else: each grey cell's digit must be at least 2 greater than the digit in any orthogonally adjacent white cell. That is all of it. The grey cells are drawn with a greater-than shape rather than a flat fill, which is a small kindness — the constraint's direction is visible on the page instead of something you have to remember.
Worth noticing how much work "at least 2" does compared with a plain inequality. A grey cell beside a white 7 must be 9. And a grey cell can never hold a 1 or a 2 if it touches any white cell at all, because its white neighbours would have to be 0 or less — so the low digits in grey, and the high digits in white, are where it cracks open. The series name is accurate: the setter's reference solve was 2:58, and two solvers in the comments reported 3:33 and 3:27.
Why it's here: a setter with 168 puzzles behind him who has never once been in this newsletter, from a series explicitly built to be approachable rather than brutal — and a variant whose name you will not find in any daily app's rotation. The last three issues' gentle slot went to line constraints; this one is a comparison rule.

Medium Published this morning

Sudoku Variants Series (489) — Fibonacci lines

by Richard, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published at 06:00 this morning, 16 October 2026; setter-estimated 3 out of 5
Normal sudoku, plus a line that does arithmetic and can run out of room. Each Fibonacci line is read left to right, and from the third digit on, every digit is the sum of the two before it. So far, Fibonacci. The twist is what happens when a sum goes past 9: a two-digit sum is written across two consecutive cells in the same row, in reading order, and the digit following a multi-digit sum is again the sum of its two predecessors — the two predecessors being the numbers, not the cells.
The setter's own example makes the geometry clear: the sequence 5-2-7-9-16-7 cannot sit on the line starting at r6c2, because the 1 and the 6 of the 16 would not end up sharing a row. That is the real constraint — a line's arithmetic has to fit the line's shape, and a bend in the wrong place kills a sequence that is otherwise perfectly valid. A cell can hold half of a number.
Why it's here: this is episode 489 of a variant series that has been running on the portal for years — an institution with no video channel, no app and no front page, which is exactly the sort of thing that never reaches a daily-games feed. The constraint is also a genuine novelty: most line rules constrain digits, this one constrains numerals.

Monster Setter-rated 5/5, unsolved

Rotation Sequence

by Nelyn, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published last night, 15 October 2026; setter-rated 5 out of 5, and at the time of writing solved by nobody
This one is barely a sudoku and is all the better for it. The grid is divided into four concentric square rings, A on the outside to D on the inside, with the centre cell fixed. You are solving for a rotation sequence that uses each of the digits 1 to 9 exactly once; each number in the sequence rotates its own ring and every ring outside it, sliding cells along the ring's perimeter, with arrows giving the direction. After the whole sequence has been applied, the result must be a single consistent rotation of the original grid — by 0°, 90°, 180° or 270°.
And both grids have to be legal: the original and the rotated result must each satisfy every rule and every clue outside the grid, while the digits you actually enter are the original's. On top of that, normal sudoku, no repeats on the positive diagonal, purple region sum lines (box borders cut each line into segments of equal sum, with the diamond endpoints counting), a lockout condition on those same purple lines (the two endpoint diamonds differ by at least 4, and no digit on the line may fall strictly between them), white kropki dots for consecutive pairs, and arrows summing to their bulbs.
Why it's here: the sudoku section's three 👎 all went to puzzles whose difficulty came from clues outside the grid telling you about digits you could not see. There is nothing like that here — the difficulty is structural, a constraint on the grid as an object rather than on its cells. Set last night by someone who has never featured here, and still at zero solves, so you would be early.

🌍 The wider puzzle worldAbout 150 genres that one person invented, and the two worth starting with

The find this week is Erich's Puzzle Palace — an index of roughly 150 distinct puzzle types, grouped as maths, grid, path, mind, chop and chess puzzles, each with its own page of hand-made examples and solutions. No app, no account, no daily reset, no video. Several of the genres appear to exist nowhere else at all. Two caveats before you browse: a handful of the 150 are word and anagram puzzles, which are not your thing and are easy to skip by name; and the interactive versions the index links to at pedros.works have lapsed — that domain now redirects to a parked advertising page, so stick to the Palace's own pages, where the puzzles are plain images you can print.

Medium New well

Hexa Trex

from Erich's Puzzle Palace — a maths-and-path hybrid, with a page of examples and their solutions
A grid of hexagons holding digits and operators. Find a path that passes through every hexagon exactly once, such that the path traces out a true equation. Standard order of operations applies — multiplication and division resolve before addition and subtraction — which is the whole difficulty, because a path that reads plausibly left to right can still be false once precedence is applied.
It is a neat collision of two puzzle families you normally meet separately: the Hamiltonian-path requirement of a loop puzzle, and arithmetic as the thing being constrained. Every hexagon must be used, so there is no discarding an awkward operator — you have to route through it and make the sum come out. The Palace's page notes that a further set of these is published at nucleuslearning.com.
Why it's here: the maths puzzles this newsletter has run have all been read-and-think problems. This is a maths puzzle you solve by drawing, and it is a genre rather than a one-off — once you have the idea there is a page of them. Nothing in a daily-games app works like it.

Gentle 15 puzzles, with solutions

Full House

from Erich's Puzzle Palace — 15 puzzles in five sets of three, each with its solution linked
Draw a path that passes through every blank square exactly once. The movement rule is the clever part: the path may only change direction when it hits a dark square, a square already containing part of the path, or the edge of the grid. Otherwise it keeps going straight. Each puzzle has a unique solution.
So you are not routing freely — you are firing a ball around a room and choosing only where to place the bounces, because everything between obstacles is forced. It has a family resemblance to the slide-and-stop movement in a daily app or two, but inverted: there the constraint is which cells you must visit, here it is that momentum carries you until something stops you, and you must still cover every square. Fifteen of them with answers is enough to get properly fluent over a weekend.
Why it's here: a forced-momentum path genre, from the same one-person archive and sitting at the easy end, so it is the natural door into the Palace. Printable, solvable with a pencil on the sofa, and not on anyone's daily rotation.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekSet in 2016, answered on Monday — a geometry problem disguised as a walk

"An Asteroid Walk" — Tanya Khovanova's Math Blog, via Konstantin Knop
Seven astronauts land on a small spherical asteroid and set off from the same spot in different directions. All walk the same route: x kilometres forward, turn 90 degrees left, x kilometres, turn 90 degrees left again, and x kilometres more. Each uses a different x, one of 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 and 90. All but one of the seven finish in the same location. Which x belonged to the astronaut who ended up alone — and how big is the asteroid?
The trick is that none of this is happening on a plane. Three sides and two right angles on a flat surface take you somewhere unremarkable; on a sphere of the right size they can bring you home, and "the right size" is what the question is really asking for. The six who met up are telling you the circumference. Worth a go on paper before you click — and do resist, because the second link is the actual answer, posted on Monday 13 October 2026, nine years and seven months after the puzzle itself went up in March 2016.
