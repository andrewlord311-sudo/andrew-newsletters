Slingshot Sudoku

by clover!, published on Grandmaster Puzzles — 10 September 2026, part of the Genuinely Approachable Sudoku run
Normal sudoku — one digit 1 to 9 in each cell, none repeating in a row, column or box — plus one rule you will not have met before, because it was built for this puzzle. Slingshot: some cells carry a circle with an arrow. The circle marks an adjacent cell whose value is about to be flung in the direction the arrow points, and the digit in the slingshot cell itself tells you how many cells away that flung value lands. So each marker ties three things together at once — a neighbour's digit, its own digit as a distance, and whatever sits at the far end of the throw.
Why it's here: the setter rates it at two party hats, one for quicker solvers, so the difficulty is deliberately mild and the novelty does the work. A brand-new constraint with a gentle rating is a rare combination — usually a new rule arrives strapped to a monster.

Rail Slide — and the 8/10 sitting behind it

by James Sinclair, Artisanal Sudoku Volume 244 — published 7 September 2026, free to read
Three puzzles arrive together each week, ordered gently to brutally, and the middle one is the pick. Rail Slide, rated 5/10: normal sudoku, plus renban lines — a line holds a set of consecutive digits, no repeats, in any order — and quadruple circles, where a digit written inside a white circle must appear in at least one of the four cells around that circle. Renban tells you what a run of cells contains without telling you where; quadruples tell you a digit is nearby without telling you which cell. Between the two you spend the puzzle narrowing a neighbourhood rather than filling a cell.
Why it's here: if 5/10 goes down easily, the same page carries Pressure Packed at 8/10 — arrows, where the digits along an arrow sum to the digit in its circle, crossed with thermometers, where digits increase from the bulb. Two constraints that both talk about size, arguing.

Sudoku Adventure — a 6×6 variant every single day, past its 1,100th

run by Rangsk, hosted free on SudokuPad, with puzzles commissioned from setters worldwide
Not one puzzle but a standing habit. Every day the series puts out a 6×6 grid carrying one variant rule, deliberately small and deliberately approachable — the stated purpose is to introduce variant rules to newer solvers while giving experienced ones something to finish over a cup of tea. Because the grid is tiny, a rule you have never seen has nowhere to hide: you meet it, you use it, you are done. Each puzzle gets its own SudokuPad page and, if you want it, a video walking through the logic afterwards. The link below opens the series' 1,100th-adventure puzzle, set by Rangsk himself to mark the milestone.
Why it's here: a daily variant series with over a thousand puzzles behind it, built by invited setters rather than a channel's own roster — and one this newsletter has never mentioned in any issue. If nothing else it is a standing supply for the mornings when a full 9×9 is too much.

Bosnian Road — two of them, on a solver you've never opened

set by IHNN on IHNN's Puzzle Emporium, posted 9 July 2026 — playable on pzprxs, which checks your solution for you
Bosnian Road: draw one single loop, exactly one cell wide, through the grid. The loop may never touch itself — not even diagonally, which is the rule that does all the work. It never passes through a black cell, and a black cell may carry a number: that number says how many of the up-to-eight cells surrounding it the loop uses. Those cells needn't be consecutive, so a clue behaves rather like a minesweeper count wrapped around a corner. Start on the 6×6 to learn the shape of it, then the 11×11 is the real one.
Why it's here: the genre was named in 2009 after Bosnia and Herzegovina, home of its inventor, and turns up at world championships rather than on any app. The same post also carries Isowatari, Nibunnogo and Japanese Sums grids — genres so rarely set that the site exists largely to give them somewhere to live. Clues by Sam — work out who's lying, except nobody is

a daily browser deduction game, free, with paid packs if you want more — not a LinkedIn or Thinky title
A grid of suspects, each either criminal or innocent, and statements that get revealed as you go. Every statement is true — the criminals don't lie, which sounds like it should make it easy and instead makes it a pure constraint problem. Your job is to mark each character correctly. The game's own promise is the interesting part: you never have to guess, there is always a logical next move, and it won't let you make an illogical one. Puzzles get harder across the week, Monday to Sunday, in the crossword tradition.
Why it's here: it launched in 2025 and passed fifty thousand players a day by early this year, entirely outside the LinkedIn and Thinky orbit this newsletter keeps declining to recap. It is the closest thing to a logic-grid deduction puzzle in daily form, and there is no reading comprehension in it — the deduction is the whole game.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekOne line of text, one picture, no algebra required — and no answer on the page

"Three hexagons on a circle" — a geometry puzzle by Catriona Agg
Three regular hexagons sit on a circle. The smallest has area 6. What is the pink area?
Agg's puzzles are the ones that circulate endlessly without attribution — a handful of overlapping shapes, one number given, one shaded region to find, and an answer that turns out to be startlingly clean once you spot the right equality. No trigonometry, no coordinates, nothing you couldn't do on paper; the difficulty is entirely in noticing which two lengths are secretly the same. This one is from July. The diagram is on the linked page, and the solution is not — it sits behind its own separate link, alongside a second page on the techniques, so you can peek at the method without being handed the number.
