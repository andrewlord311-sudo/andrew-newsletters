🧩 Sudoku variantsAn orange line that forbids near neighbours, a grid where the circles describe themselves, and the Friday killer that turns Diabolical by Saturday

Gentle Yesterday's puzzle · par time 8:00

Pumpkin Pi

by Philip Newman, on GM Puzzles (The Art of Puzzles) — posted 26 November 2026, so it is a day old. It belongs to the site's GAS series, Genuinely Approachable Sudoku, which publishes no star rating and gives time standards instead: two party hats at 8:00, one at 15:00. Those are the only difficulty figures the post carries, and the Gentle badge is read off them rather than invented
Two rules, quoted as the post gives them. Standard sudoku: insert a number from 1 to 9 into each cell so that no number repeats in any row, column, or bold region. Dutch Whispers: numbers in cells directly connected on an orange line must differ by at least 4.
Dutch Whispers is the quieter sibling of German Whispers — same idea, different threshold, and the threshold is what makes it worth a weekend. A difference of at least 4 splits the digits into a low band and a high band with 5 as the hinge, and the arithmetic is unforgiving: 5 can only sit next to 1 or 9, so a 5 anywhere on a line is almost a solved cell in itself. More usefully, 4 can only reach 8 or 9, and 6 can only reach 1 or 2, which means the middle digits are the constrained ones and the extremes are the free ones — exactly backwards from most line constraints, where the extremes are where you start. The practical consequence is that a Dutch Whispers line of any length alternates low-high-low-high, and once you have fixed which end is which the whole line usually collapses. Long lines are therefore easier than short ones here, which is the opposite of the instinct you bring from thermos and renbans.
Why it's here: GM Puzzles' GAS slot publishes a fresh variant sudoku every single day from a rotating bench — Philip Newman, Bill Murphy and clover! between them covered Dutch Whispers, Outside, Quadro, Little Killer, Hundred, Battenburg, Kropki Pairs, small Arrow, Easy-as and XV Pairs in the last ten days alone. It is a daily feed of single-constraint puzzles with the rules written out properly, which is precisely the thing the ratings keep asking for, and it has nothing to do with anybody's video schedule. Newman has not been picked here before.

Tough — our read, not a published rating The find of the week · open for submission until 1 December

Friendly Circles (problem N/D1)

in MIT Technology Review's Puzzle Corner, November/December 2026 issue — a column that has run continuously since Allan Gottlieb launched it in 1966 and is now set by Michael S. Branicky, Edward Faulkner and Abe Kunin. The puzzle is a static grid in the issue PDF; print it or draw on screen, there is no app. The column carries no difficulty ratings at all, so the Tough badge is an editorial read of the constraint and nothing more
The rules, quoted in full. Normal sudoku rules apply (place the digits 1–9 once each into every row, column, and 3×3 box). A cell is “friendly” if its digit matches its row, column, or box number (boxes are numbered from left to right and top to bottom in normal reading order). Draw a circle in every friendly cell that doesn't already contain a circle. If a digit N appears on a circle, then it appears N times on circles.
Read that last sentence twice, because it is doing something unusual. This is a self-referential sudoku: the circles are not given to you as clues, they are an output of the solution, and the final rule is a condition on that output. So you cannot simply scan the marked cells — you have to reason about a set of cells you have not drawn yet. The machinery is lovely. “Friendly” is defined three ways at once, which means row 1 is friendly wherever it holds a 1, column 7 wherever it holds a 7, and box 5 — the middle box — wherever it holds a 5, and a single cell can qualify on more than one count. The counting rule then closes the loop: if a 1 ends up circled anywhere, it must be the only circled 1 in the grid; if a 9 is circled, there must be exactly nine circled 9s, which is a huge demand given there are only nine 9s on the board in total. That asymmetry is the whole puzzle. Low digits on circles are nearly free and high digits on circles are nearly impossible, so the work goes into proving which digits can appear on a circle at all before you worry about where. Note too that every grid has some friendly cells — the three definitions cover far too much ground to avoid — so “no circles at all” is not an escape route.
Why it's here: a 60-year-old print column from an engineering magazine is about as far from a recap of this week's community portals as it is possible to get, and the variant is one this newsletter has never been able to offer before: a sudoku whose constraint is about its own solution. The practical bonus is the deadline. Answers go to puzzlecorner@technologyreview.com by 1 December, and correct solvers get named in a later issue — there are four days left, which makes this the one puzzle all year you can actually enter rather than just solve. The same issue's other two problems are below in the maths section; back issues going back decades are free.

Tough Diabolical tomorrow Site's own grading · new grid at midnight GMT

The Daily Killer Sudoku, no. 6822

by Andrew Stuart, on SudokuWiki — today's grid, 27 November, graded Tough by the site. The grading runs on a fixed weekly curve, which is the useful part: Gentle on Monday and Tuesday, Moderate Wednesday and Thursday, Tough on Friday, Diabolical on Saturday and Sunday. The day turns over at midnight GMT. Some grades are reserved for the site's All-Access supporters, so if a difficulty will not open that is why rather than a broken link
Two rules, in the site's own words. Killer is based on sudoku, so you must place 1 to 9 in every cell so that each number is unique in every row, column and box. Cells are grouped into cages, and the clue on a cage is the sum of the solutions to those squares — so a clue of 3 on a two-cell cage means those cells hold 1 and 2, though not yet in which order.
Killer is the one well-liked family the ratings keep rewarding, and a pure killer with no second constraint lives or dies on cage arithmetic rather than pattern-spotting. The levers worth having ready: a cage's total and its size together pin down a candidate set, and the extremes are free information — a three-cell cage summing to 6 is {1,2,3} and a three-cell cage summing to 24 is {7,8,9}, with no choice about either. Then comes the rule that does most of the real work and has nothing to do with cages at all: every row, column and box sums to 45. So take a box, add up the cages wholly inside it, and the difference tells you the total of whatever cells are left over, even when no cage touches them. Chain that across two or three boxes at once and you get sums for odd-shaped leftover regions that the clues never mention, which is how a Tough killer cracks open. On a Diabolical one you will need it combined with ordinary candidate elimination rather than instead of it.
Why it's here: SudokuWiki is a site Andrew has almost certainly used — for the solver and the strategy write-ups, which are the best free reference there is on sudoku technique. What nobody remembers is that Stuart also publishes his own killer every day, and has for over 6,800 days. It is not a community portal, not a competition, not a video, and it is the only pick here with a built-in difficulty ramp: solve the Tough this morning and Saturday's Diabolical is waiting tomorrow on the same page.

🌍 The wider puzzle worldA university maths competition with a locked answer box, the world championship's own free archive, and fifty genres a day

Medium to Tough Eight problems · answers gated, nothing spoiled

MathsBombe 2026

run by the Department of Mathematics at the University of Manchester — “from the people behind the Alan Turing Cryptography Competition,” as the site puts it. Eight problems in the 2026 edition, each on its own page, plus archived editions back to 2016 (the site notes not every past edition is currently available). Aimed at school-age entrants, which tells you nothing useful about the difficulty — these are proper olympiad-flavoured problems
The format is the reason this is a find. Each problem has a “Check answer” box rather than a visible solution, and the box makes you answer a simple arithmetic question before it will even look at your submission. So you can work through all eight and know whether you are right, without any possibility of accidentally reading the method — which is exactly the thing that ruins most online maths puzzles. There is a solutions page for the 2026 set, so the answers do exist if you want them; it is a separate click, deliberately.
Problem 1, to show you the flavour, and not spoiled below: a circus performance includes a tightrope act with 25 performers. They all set off along the rope at the same instant and at the same speed, each having chosen a direction; whenever two meet they both reverse; and a performer who reaches an end of the rope steps off it. You are asked for the largest number of turns the performers can make altogether. If that smells like a physics problem about colliding particles, that is the right instinct and also the trap — the standard move is to notice what is really conserved when two identical things bounce off each other, at which point the whole thing becomes a counting argument rather than a simulation. The site records that someone first solved it at 4:03:32pm on 21 January, which is a nice measure of how long it takes once you see it.
Why it's here: a new lineage entirely. The maths puzzles this newsletter has run have come from American columns and quant-firm puzzle pages; this is a British university's own competition, written by mathematicians for a national schools audience, and it comes with ten years of archive attached. Nothing about it will have crossed any of the feeds — and the gated answer box means it survives being read on a phone on a Friday morning without the weekend's puzzle being wrecked.

Championship tier Free PDF · solutions booklet included

The Sudoku Grand Prix 2026, round 8

the World Puzzle Federation's own Grand Prix archive — the competition circuit that feeds the World Sudoku Championship. The 2026 Sudoku GP ran eight rounds, each set by a different country's authors: Türkiye in January, then Slovakia, Thailand, Switzerland, Ukraine, Czechia, Hungary and France for round 8 in August. Round 8's competition booklet is seven pages and the solutions booklet comes with it
What you are getting is a real competition paper rather than a single puzzle: a timed round's worth of variant sudoku, each puzzle carrying its own rules and point value, set by national-level authors and solved under the clock by the best solvers in the world a few months ago. The instruction booklet is a separate download on the same site and is worth opening first, because GP rounds routinely use variants that never appear on the community portals — the rules pages are where the genuinely unfamiliar constraints live.
One honest warning, checked this morning. On the puzzle side of the Grand Prix the equivalent file is password-protected — round 8 of the Puzzle GP downloads as a locked PDF and will not open, which is presumably deliberate. The Sudoku round 8 booklet and its solutions booklet are both open and download cleanly; those are the two links below, and they are the ones to use. The site's archive currently exposes only round 8 of each series rather than the whole season, so this is the round to take.
Why it's here: championship material is the one tier of puzzle this newsletter has never pulled from, and it is free, printable and sitting in the open. It is also the right shape for a weekend rather than a coffee break — seven pages, no app, no account, and a solutions booklet so nothing is left hanging. If the round goes well, the GP site's own archive is the way into previous seasons.

Gentle to Large Fresh grids today, in the browser

Nurikabe and Kakuro, daily

on PuzzleMadness — a British site publishing a fresh set of grids every day across roughly forty sudoku variants (Jigsaw, Killer, Skyscraper among them) plus a full logic-puzzle roster: Nurikabe, Hitori, Slitherlink, Fillomino, Kakuro and Nonograms, each in Small, Medium and Large sizes and a Mixed option. There is also a Giant tier — Giant Slitherlink, Giant Fillomino, Giant Minesweeper — for when a normal grid is not enough. Playable in the browser with no install; registration is offered for cloud-saving progress rather than required to play
Nurikabe, in the site's wording: each numbered cell belongs to a wall, and that wall must contain exactly that many squares. Walls are groups of connected squares, and different walls cannot touch horizontally or vertically — diagonal contact is allowed. The remaining squares form the maze, which must all connect to one another, and the maze cannot contain any 2×2 block of open squares. Today's grids are dated Friday 27 November 2026.
Nurikabe is the pick of the two and deserves the explanation, because its logic is unlike anything in the sudoku family: you are solving for two things at once that constrain each other from opposite directions. The walls are bounded above — each is exactly its number, no more — while the maze is bounded below, since it must stay in one piece. That tension is where every deduction comes from. Three openers worth having: any cell orthogonally between two different numbers must be maze, because a wall touching both would merge them; a numbered cell with a 1 is finished immediately, so all four of its neighbours are maze; and the no-2×2 rule is a positive tool rather than a restriction, because three maze cells in an L shape force the fourth to be wall. Late in a grid the connectivity rule does the heavy lifting — a maze cell about to be cut off from the rest tells you a neighbouring cell cannot possibly be wall. Kakuro, on the same site, is the other way to spend an hour: cage arithmetic in the same spirit as this morning's killer, so the sums you warmed up on transfer straight over.
Why it's here: one genuinely new platform, and a deliberate change of genre from last week's loop-and-track puzzles. Nurikabe in particular is a shading genre, which is a family this newsletter has barely touched — nothing on LinkedIn's lineup does it, Thinky's catalogue does not have it, and it asks a different kind of attention from anything else in this issue. Everything resets tomorrow morning, so it keeps for the whole weekend.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekThree evenly matched teams, one game already won — and a deadline of Monday

Problem N/D2, set by George Fischer '86 — MIT Technology Review Puzzle Corner, November/December 2026
Team A, Team B, and Team C are playing in a tournament. The three teams are evenly matched. To be the champion, a team needs to win three games in a row. Team A has won one game. What is the probability that Team A will win the tournament?
That is the whole statement, quoted as printed, and it is doing more than it admits. Everything you need is in two phrases. “Evenly matched” fixes every single game at even odds. “Three games in a row” is the sting: not three wins, three consecutive wins, so a loss does not merely cost a game, it resets your count to zero. And the obvious question the statement does not answer is the one to settle first — with three teams and presumably two playing at a time, who sits out, and who comes back in? Pin down that structure and the rest is a small, very tidy calculation about a process that can in principle run forever. Worth noticing that Team A's single win is not nothing and not much either; quantifying exactly how much it is worth is the puzzle.

Two more from the same issue, both good, neither spoiled here. N/D3 is Mousetrap, a card game Arthur Cayley introduced in 1878: deal a shuffled deck labelled 1 to n, count down from the top, move a card to the bottom if its number does not match the count, remove it and restart the count from 1 if it does. You win if every card goes. No general formula is known for how many permutations win. There are 6 winning permutations with four cards, 15 with five and 84 with six — compute, as elegantly as possible, the number for seven. And the Speed Department, for the back of an envelope: a ship takes five days to complete a journey downstream, and seven days to return against the river's current. How long would it take to complete a one-way journey if there were no current?
