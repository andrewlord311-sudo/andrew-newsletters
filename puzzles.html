🧩 Sudoku variantsA killer grid that bans the number 10, circles that have to beat their own neighbours, and a 12×12 with no straight edges

Gentle 89% from 26 solvers

Fourteen Ways to Avoid Ten

by gk122, on the Logic Masters Deutschland Rätselportal — posted 19 November 2026, so it is a day old. The setter rated it 2 on the site's five-point scale, which is its "easy puzzle" band; a solver in the comments reckoned three stars was fairer. Both numbers are on the page, and the Gentle badge takes the setter's own rather than mine
Three rules, and only three. Standard sudoku — each row, column and 3×3 box holds the digits 1 to 9 exactly once. Killer cages: the digits in each dotted cage sum to the small number printed in its top-left corner. Anti-XV: no two orthogonally adjacent cells may sum to 5 or 10.
The third rule is a negative constraint, and negative constraints are quietly the most powerful things in variant sudoku because they apply to the entire grid at once rather than to the handful of cells that happen to be marked. Work out what it actually forbids and the board shrinks fast: no 1 may sit beside a 4 or a 9, no 2 beside a 3 or an 8, no 3 beside a 2 or a 7, no 4 beside a 1 or a 6, and 5 may never touch 5. That last one is free — two 5s can never be orthogonally adjacent anywhere on the grid, in any row, column or box, before you look at a single clue. The interaction with the cages is where the puzzle lives, because a cage total that could normally be split several ways usually has most of those splittings killed off by adjacency. A two-cell cage summing to 10 is the joke in the title: the pair has to add to 10, and if those two cells touch, the anti-XV rule forbids exactly that. So the cage must be bent.
Why it's here: killer is the one well-liked constraint family this newsletter has not run in over a month — the last four issues went palindrome, whispers, arrow, region-sum and zipper, all line constraints. gk122 has never been picked here, and the German portal has had a fortnight off. Nothing to do with any video channel.
If you want another from the same board: Cross My Heart by TheMegaDud, posted the same week and rated 3 on the site's scale — arrows only, where the digits along each arrow sum to the digit in its circle, and the arrows in the middle box run straight through their crossing point rather than turning at it. 90% from 13 solvers.

Greatest circles

by Avelpuzzles, on the Logic Masters Deutschland Rätselportal — posted 19 November 2026. Marked 2 on the site's scale, its "easy" band, with commenters landing between two and three stars. At 94% from 25 ratings it is the best-received new sudoku on the portal this week, and a different setter again
Three rules. Standard sudoku — digits 1 to 9 once each per row, column and region. White dots: two cells separated by a white dot must differ by exactly 1. Circles: a digit in a circle must be greater than the sum of its orthogonally adjacent cells.
The circle rule is the one worth sitting with, because it is far harsher than it sounds. The smallest digits available are 1 and 2, so a circle with four orthogonal neighbours needs to beat a sum of at least 1+2+3+4 = 10 — impossible, since 9 is the largest digit there is. Every circle in the middle of the grid is therefore a contradiction, which means every circle must sit against an edge or in a corner, and even then it is squeezed: a circle on an edge has three neighbours and must beat at least 1+2+3 = 6, so it is 7, 8 or 9 and its neighbours are the three smallest digits going. A corner circle has only two neighbours and is the loosest case. So before you place anything you can read the geometry straight off the board — circles mean large digits ringed by tiny ones — and the white dots then do the fine work, because a difference of exactly 1 either chains those small neighbours together or rules the pairing out entirely.
Why it's here: the ratings have been consistent for two months that one clean idea beats four stacked ones, and this is about as clean as a non-standard constraint gets: a single inequality, stated in one line, that reshapes the whole grid before you write a digit. It is also a genuinely unusual rule rather than a stock one — not killer, not thermo, not a line.

Jigsaw Sudoku-12 no. 102

on Puzzlemix, Gareth Moore's puzzle site — a 12×12 grid, graded Hard by the site, published this week and listed as playable until 4 December. Puzzlemix has appeared in this newsletter once, back in October, and only for its pencil puzzles; its sudoku variants have never been picked here. It publishes a free puzzle most days across eleven different sudoku variants — Sudoku-X, Kropki, XV, Odd Pair, Consecutive, Toroidal, three flavours of Killer and two of Jigsaw
Two rules, in the site's own terms. Each number must appear once in every row and every column. The standard rectangular boxes are replaced by irregular, bold-lined shapes, and each shape must also contain every number once. On a 12×12 grid the twelve symbols are 1 to 9 plus A, B and C — letters standing in for 10, 11 and 12 so each one fits in a cell, not a word puzzle hiding in a grid.
Jigsaw is the oldest idea in variant sudoku and still one of the best, because it removes a crutch rather than adding a rule. In a normal grid the boxes are rectangles, so a box and a row always overlap in exactly three cells and the standard scanning patterns are the same everywhere on the board. Bend the regions and that regularity is gone: a region might share five cells with one row and a single cell with another, and every region behaves differently. What replaces it is the law of leftovers — take any band of regions and compare it against the rows and columns it covers, and the cells that spill outside must collectively hold exactly the digits the enclosed cells are missing. On a 12×12 that technique is not a flourish, it is the main road, and the site's own advice is the other half: watch for a region with only two cells left where a given number can go. A real weekend puzzle rather than a Friday-morning one.
Why it's here: three different platforms would have been three different flavours of the same community boards, and this is deliberately not that — a British puzzle site with its own generator, its own difficulty grading and a twelve-symbol grid, which is a size the setter-portal world almost never publishes. Nothing about it will have crossed your feed.

🌍 The wider puzzle worldOne man's 150 invented puzzle types, and twenty genres published fresh every day of the year

Medium The find of the week

Loop Count Puzzles

by Erich Friedman, on his own puzzle pages — eight puzzles, each with its solution, and a worked demonstration grid above them. They are static images: you print them or draw on screen, there is no app. The index they sit in is the real discovery, and it is extraordinary: roughly 150 different puzzle types, nearly all of them his own invention, sorted into maths, grid, path, mind, chop and chess sections, with easy / normal / hard entry points
Three rules, quoted as given. Draw a single closed loop that moves horizontally or vertically through every square exactly once. Each number in the grid must equal the sum of the lengths of the two loop segments passing through that square. Each puzzle has one unique solution.
That second rule is a genre of its own and I have not seen it anywhere else. Most loop puzzles clue the loop from outside it — Slitherlink counts edges around a cell, Masyu marks cells the loop must turn or run straight through. This one clues the loop from inside, using the shape of the loop itself: stand on a numbered square, look along the loop in both directions until it turns, and the two straight runs you are standing at the junction of must add up to the number under your feet. So every clue is simultaneously about direction and distance, which is why a 2 is so informative — the smallest two segments possible are 1 and 1, so a 2 means the loop turns immediately on both sides and you have found a corner. Large numbers mean long straights and pin down whole corridors of the grid. Since the loop must visit every square, it is a Hamiltonian problem underneath, and the usual parity and dead-end arguments all still apply.
Why it's here: this is as far from a recap as this newsletter gets. It is not a community portal, not a competition and not a daily — it is one person's private workshop, published as plain pages that have clearly been there for years, holding puzzle types that exist nowhere else because he made them up. The index lists interactive versions of many of the series on a companion site, pedros.works, if you would rather click than print. Once you are in there, Hamiltonian Mazes, Non-Crossing, Pentomino Puzzles and Loop Count alone are a month of weekends. Skip the anagram and rebus sections.

Gentle to Tough New grids every day, in the browser

Tracks and Fillomino, daily

on BrainBashers — a long-running British puzzle site that publishes a fresh set of grids in twenty-odd logic genres every single day, each in several sizes and difficulties, playable in the browser straight off the listing page. Today's board alone runs to Bridges, Light Up, Range, Slants, Tents, Neighbours, Hitori, Futoshiki, Battleships, Skyscrapers, CalcuDoku, Slitherlink, Nonogrids, ABC View and more
Tracks, in the site's wording: find the route of the track. There is only one continuous track, with one start and one end. The track does not cross itself. Each clue gives the number of track pieces in that row or column. Sizes run 4×4 up to 10×10, two fresh puzzles of each size a day, with a 12×12 weekly special.
Fillomino, likewise: divide the grid into groups. Each square contains a number giving the size of its group. Groups link only horizontally and vertically, and you may start with two or more numbers already in the same group. Today's are an 8×8 and a 12×12 at easy, a 16×16 at medium, and a 20×20 as the weekly special.
Tracks is the one to start with, because it is the rarer genre and the logic is unusually pure. The row and column counts are the whole puzzle: a row whose clue equals its width must be entirely track, a row clued 0 is entirely empty, and every piece you place forces its neighbours because a track that enters a cell must leave it. The standard lever is counting in both directions at once — a column that still needs three pieces with only three unfilled cells resolves instantly, and the no-crossing rule kills off most of what is left. Fillomino's trick is the opposite, and it is the thing beginners miss: two groups of the same size may not touch. So a lone 2 next to another lone 2 tells you those two cells are in different pairs, and the pairs must grow away from each other. That single observation solves most of an easy grid.
Why it's here: genre variety at a scale nothing else in this newsletter matches. Tracks in particular is a genuinely uncommon type — it is not on the German portal's usual roster, not on LinkedIn's daily lineup and not in Thinky's catalogue — and having twenty of these resetting every morning is a different proposition from a one-off puzzle you solve and finish. If the weekend goes well, the 20×20 Fillomino is the deep end.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekA billiard table with no bottom edge, and one blind shot

"Bouncing Billiards" — Zach Wissner-Gross, Fiddler on the Proof, 13 November 2026
A cue ball of radius 1 is centred at (0, 0). A red ball, also of radius 1, is centred at (0, 5). The table has a top wall at y = 10 and two side walls at x = 5 and x = −5 that extend infinitely downward. The table and its walls are frictionless, so the balls never slow down and bounce off the walls without losing energy. You are new to the game, so you are equally likely to hit the cue ball in any direction. What is the probability that the cue ball hits the red ball before it makes contact with the walls twice?
Then the extra credit, which is the better puzzle of the two: what is the probability that the cue ball hits the red ball at all, however many times it bounces off a wall first? Note what the geometry is doing. Two parallel side walls ten units apart, closed at the top and open forever downward, means any shot aimed below the horizontal is gone for good, while any shot aimed upward is trapped in a corridor and will keep coming back — so the two halves of the circle of directions behave completely differently, and that asymmetry is the puzzle rather than an aside. The standard tool for bouncing inside a corridor is to stop bouncing: reflect the table instead of the ball, so the path becomes a straight line through a row of mirrored copies of the table, each one holding its own mirrored red ball. The question then turns into which of those copies a straight line from the origin can hit — and since both balls have radius 1 rather than zero, a hit means passing within 2 of a target centre, which is what gives each one an angular width. The fact that the puzzle gives the balls a size at all is the hint.
