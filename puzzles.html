Sixth Sense

by Nelyn, Logic Masters Deutschland — published 3 September 2026, setter-rated 3/5
Normal sudoku, then four layers on top. Arrows: the digits along an arrow add up to the digit in its circled cell. Little killer: a number outside the grid gives the sum of every digit along the diagonal it points down. Red dots: two cells joined by one share a parity — both odd or both even, never one of each. And the odd one out — the four cells around a blue square hold only two different digits between them, which is a much tighter constraint than it first sounds and is where the grid cracks open.
Why it's here: Nelyn posts straight to the German portal, and this went up nine hours ago — no video, no thumbnail, no queue of people who've already solved it.

Medium Posted last night

Uni-parity

by Ollos, Logic Masters Deutschland — published 3 September 2026, setter-rated 3/5
Normal sudoku plus three constraints that pull in different directions. Uni-parity lines: a blue line contains only even digits, an orange line only odd ones — so every line immediately halves its own candidate pool. Disjoint groups: cells in the same relative position within their box must all differ, so the nine top-left-corner cells are themselves a full set of 1–9. Kropki dots: white means consecutive, black means one digit is double the other. The parity lines and the disjoint-groups rule interfere with each other constantly, which is the pleasure of it.
Why it's here: disjoint groups is a proper old-school constraint that almost never appears on the daily-app circuit, and pairing it with parity lines is not a combination you'll have been served this week.

Gentle LMI Puzzle Expo

Birthday Sudoku — five variants at once, kept deliberately easy

by Prasanna Seshadri (Puzzle No. 522) — published on his own blog and playable on Logic Masters India's Puzzle Expo
A stack of constraints that would normally add up to something brutal, set at a level the author says is meant for people newish to variant sudoku. Kropki: white circle means consecutive, black means one digit is double the other. XV: a V joins two cells summing to 5, an X two summing to 10. Odd/even: shaded squares hold even digits, shaded circles odd ones. Plus-minus: a clue number is the difference between the sum of its horizontal neighbours and the sum of its vertical neighbours. And the birthday joke that makes it — "33 to 34": any 3 may be taken as a 4 in value, whether that's in a plus-minus clue, a kropki relationship, an XV total, or even a 3 sitting in a cell marked even.
Why it's here: a different platform entirely — LMI's Penpa-based Expo rather than a sudoku-specific app — from a setter who writes rounds for the World Puzzle Federation Grand Prix rather than for a channel.

🌍 The wider puzzle worldA community platform hiding behind a competition site, and a numbered blog run by one person

Medium New here

LMI Puzzle Expo — and the birthday cake to start on

Logic Masters India's publishing platform, built on Penpa+ — free to solve, puzzles from setters worldwide, with times, ratings and comments
Everyone knows LMI for its timed contests; the Expo is the other thing they run — an open shelf where setters publish single puzzles and anyone can solve them in the browser, whenever, with no clock forced on you. Good first stop: a two-grid puzzle built as a birthday cake. Left grid, Choco Banana — shade some cells so that every orthogonally connected group of shaded cells forms a rectangle, and every connected group of unshaded cells does not. Right grid, Kurotto — shade some cells so each clue equals the total size of the connected shaded areas touching it edgewise; clued cells are never shaded. And the join: clues down the middle give the sum or the difference (sometimes both) of the number of shaded cells in that row across the two grids, so neither half solves alone. Speedset by the author and tested by Martin Ender.
Why it's here: two genres that don't exist on any daily app, on a platform Andrew is unlikely to have opened even if he knows LMI's contests — the archive behind it is large and nothing in it expires tonight.

Difficulty not stated One-person blog

djmathman's Puzzle 373 — a Tapa doodled and thrown online

from dj-puzzles.blogspot.com, posted 27 June 2026 — the puzzles are numbered, hand-set, and go straight out with a solving link attached
Tapa: shade some empty cells to make a single connected wall. A number in a cell tells you the lengths of the runs of shaded cells among its eight neighbours — and where a cell carries more than one number, those blocks must be separated by at least one unshaded neighbour. Numbered cells are never shaded themselves, and the wall may never contain a 2×2 block of shading anywhere in the grid. That last rule does most of the work: it's what turns a clue like "1 1" from a hint into a near-certainty.
Why it's here: the setter's own note is that he "doodled" it a few days earlier and it "got drowned in a sea of puzzles so quickly" on the site he posted it to — which is exactly the sort of thing no feed will ever put in front of you. He gives no difficulty rating, so this one is a genuine unknown. (Rules wording above from Grandmaster Puzzles' Tapa reference.)

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekNo arithmetic at all — just a fish, a tank, and three views of the same path

"The fin-tastic fish puzzle" — Alex Bellos's Monday puzzle, The Guardian, 31 August 2026
A fish swims in a tank two metres high, one metre across and one metre deep. Any path it swims casts three shadows: a front view, a side view and a top view. You are given the front and side views of a path — draw the top view of the shortest path that would produce them.
Set by maths educator George Watkins to mark the opening of the 1729 Maths School in Mill Hill, the first specialist maths secondary in the UK. There are two of these to work through and the diagrams are in the article; the whole thing lives or dies on whether you can hold the tank in your head, and Bellos notes that when he ran similar puzzles in 2015 and 2017, architects wrote in to say this is precisely what their training teaches. The solutions went up in a separate article at 5pm the same day, so the puzzle page itself is safe to open.
