Medium Published this morning

Furrowed

by tallcat, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published today, 18 September 2026, setter-rated 3/5
Normal sudoku, plus two line rules that pull in opposite directions. Renban: a pink line holds a set of consecutive digits, no repeats, in any order — so you learn what a run of cells contains long before you learn where anything goes. Between lines: a line strung between two filled circles must have every cell on it strictly between the two circled values. One rule fixes a set and hides the order; the other fixes a range and says nothing about the set. The puzzle is named for what that does to the grid.
Why it's here: renban has quietly become the best-rated constraint in this newsletter, and tallcat is a portal regular rather than anyone's weekly video pick — this went up at 5:31 this morning and had no solve count on it when it was chosen.

Medium One rule, all the way through

Shelves — Triple Cell Sum Lines Sudoku

by psams, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published today, 18 September 2026, setter-rated medium
Normal sudoku — the digits 1 to 9 once each in every row, column and 3×3 box — and then exactly one extra rule, stated in a single line: along a red line, in any three consecutive cells, one digit is the sum of the other two. Which of the three is the total isn't specified, and that is the whole puzzle. Every window of three you slide along the line has to work, so the constraint overlaps itself the length of the line: cells 1–2–3 constrain 2–3–4, which constrain 3–4–5. Nothing else is bolted on.
Why it's here: a deliberate contrast with last week's 👎. One constraint, no invented terminology, and all the difficulty coming from how the rule interacts with itself rather than from having to learn something new before you can start.

Tough Two grids, each inside the other

Grandfather Paradox — a loop puzzle that swallows its own tail

by Bobuardo da Vinci, on the Rätselportal — published today, 18 September 2026, setter-rated 4/5 (hard). Solved in Penpa in the browser.
Two grids, side by side, and here is the conceit: the left grid sits somewhere inside the right one, shrunk by a factor of three — and the right grid sits somewhere inside the left one, likewise shrunk, each occupying a 3×6 rectangle. A placed grid may be rotated but never reflected, and your line may only cross into it at marked openings. So where each grid is inside the other is part of what you're solving, and neither half can be finished alone.
The line itself is standard loop drawing — segments through cells forming one connected path, no dead ends, no branches. The clues come in two flavours. Yajilin (left): shade every cell left empty once the line is drawn and the other grid is placed; shaded cells may never be orthogonally adjacent; a numbered arrow counts the shaded cells in the direction it points. Reductionism (right): a numbered arrow instead counts how many distinct cell contents lie in that direction, empty cells included.
Why it's here: Reductionism is a genre you can go years without meeting, but it is welded here to Yajilin, which is properly established — so the unfamiliar half is scaffolded by the familiar one rather than left to stand on its own. That, and a self-containing fractal grid is simply a good joke to spend a Saturday on.

Free daily New to this newsletter

Fillomino, fresh every morning

PuzzleMadness — a free daily Fillomino, played and checked in the browser, with a dated archive going back by year
Fillomino: divide the whole grid into blocks of cells joined edge to edge. A number in a cell tells you how many cells are in its block — so every cell of a block of four carries a 4, whether or not it was printed there to begin with. The clues are partial: you are completing blocks around the numbers given, not inventing a tidy partition from nothing. The rule that makes it bite is the genre's standard one — two blocks of the same size may never touch edge to edge, which is what turns "this could be a 3" into "this cannot be a 3", and is where the whole deduction lives.
Why it's here: Fillomino is on every serious pencil-puzzle list — it is in the sources this newsletter is meant to be mining — and yet it has never once appeared in an issue. This is the cheapest possible way in: no account, no app, a new one every day, and today's is already up.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekFour sentences, no diagram, no arithmetic — and the answer is behind a click, not on the page

"Self-Study" — from Eureka, October 1962
(a) At least two of these statements, apart from this one, are true. (b) At least two of these statements, apart from this one, are false. (c) At least one of these statements is false. (d) x of these statements are true.

Given that, if you knew the value of x, you could determine uniquely which statements are true and which are false — determine the value of x.
Worth reading that last sentence twice, because it is doing something unusual. You are not told what x is, and you are not being asked to find a consistent assignment and stop. You are told that knowing x would be sufficient to pin down every statement's truth value — and it's that sufficiency, used as a clue in its own right, that rules out all but one candidate. It is the same trick as the birthday puzzles that go round every few years, in a much smaller room. Pure constraint logic, nothing to compute, and it comes from the journal of the Cambridge University Mathematical Society over sixty years ago.
