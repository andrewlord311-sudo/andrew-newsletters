Rossini (No Inverse) Sudoku

by clover!, in the Genuinely Approachable Sudoku series at GM Puzzles — published 1 October 2026
Standard sudoku, plus one outside-clue rule: wherever an arrow appears outside the grid, the first three digits in that row or column must increase in the direction the arrow points. An arrow pointing left means the three nearest digits ascend as you read leftwards, towards the arrow. That is the whole constraint — three cells, one direction, nothing about the other six.
The variant's name carries the catch. In plain Rossini, an absent arrow tells you something: it means those three digits are not in increasing order either way. Here the "no inverse" tag switches that off — a missing arrow means nothing at all, so you get no negative constraint to lean on and every deduction has to come from an arrow that is actually printed.
Why it's here: it is one established rule applied cleanly, which is what this section's ratings have consistently preferred over invented stacks. The series publishes target times rather than a video — 6:30 for strong solvers, 12:00 for the rest of us — and clover! has not appeared here since early September, with a different genre then.

Mind Field — minesweeper, inverted

by Ryanbaker, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published early this morning, 2 October 2026, setter-rated 4 out of 5
Normal sudoku, and then one rule doing an unusual amount of work. Any cell may contain a hidden mine. A cell whose digit equals the number of mines in the 3×3 area centred on it is not caged — so the cages mark out precisely the cells where the digit and the local mine count disagree. You are given the cages and asked to reconstruct both the digits and the minefield underneath them.
It is the inversion that makes it bite. A minesweeper clue normally tells you a count; here an absence of a cage is the clue, and a cage only tells you that some mismatch exists without saying in which direction. Worth reading the setter's own wording on the puzzle page before you start — one rule this compressed is easier to mis-parse than to solve.
Why it's here: a genuine hybrid of two separate genres rather than another line constraint, from a setter with no video channel behind him, posted a few hours ago. This is the kind of thing the portal turns up that a weekly roster never gets round to.

Monster Find the grid first

Some Deconstructed Sums

by sanabas, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published early this morning, 2 October 2026, setter-rated 4 out of 5
This one does not hand you a sudoku. Deconstruction: you have to find nine non-overlapping 3×3 regions which, read in reading order, form a valid grid — the boxes are yours to locate before any digit is safe.
And the grid you are building is not a sudoku either. Somedoku: row N contains exactly N different digits, and column N contains exactly N different digits — so the first row holds one digit repeated, and the counts tighten as you go down and across. On top of that: X-sums (an outside clue gives the sum of the first X cells from that side, where X is the digit nearest the clue), region sum lines (each box's worth of a blue line adds to the same total), German whispers (adjacent digits on a green line differ by at least 5) and black kropki dots (the two digits are in a 1:2 ratio). Empty cells are ignored when the line rules are evaluated.
Why it's here: this is the weekend puzzle — six interacting rules and a grid that has to be deduced into existence before the rules can even be applied. Deconstruction puzzles are rare and somedoku rarer still, and the setter has no connection to any of the channels. Pour the coffee first.

🌍 The wider puzzle worldA contest that closes on Sunday, and 850 Fillominos nobody has told you about

Closes Sunday evening Seven invented variants

The September Sudoku Contest — seven variants, fifty minutes, still open

by Riad Khanmagomedov, hosted by Logic Masters India — open since 27 September, entries close at 01:59 IST on 5 October, which is about 21:30 on Sunday evening UK time. Tested by Prasanna Seshadri and Seungjae Kwak.
Seven sudoku variants, 500 points, 50 minutes from the moment you start — you choose when that is, anywhere inside the window, and there are 10 bonus points per minute saved if everything is correct. Free; you need an LMI login to solve, and it runs in Penpa in the browser, so there is nothing to install and nothing to print.
The seven are Duplex, Masonry, Maze, Archery, Bastion, Shield and Sudoku with Ropes — and no, you haven't met them, because most of them are this setter's own. Masonry: classic sudoku, but the two-digit numbers read left to right in ten horizontal bricks must strictly decrease as you move row by row down and across. Shield: classic sudoku where the digit sums in all the circles, all the diamonds and all the octagons come out equal. Duplex and Bastion aren't sudoku at all in the usual sense — Duplex fills a grid with 1–8 where each outlined area uses every digit exactly twice. The instruction booklet gives all seven with a worked example each, and it is worth downloading even if you never enter.
Why it's here: a timed competition set from a Russian setter whose genres exist nowhere else, on a platform that is not a daily-games app, and it is live right now — the first pick in this newsletter with an actual deadline. If you want the puzzles without the clock, the booklets stay downloadable afterwards.

Bottomless Free, no login

Janko — 850 playable Fillominos, and three hundred other genres

janko.at, a German puzzle archive of thousands of hand-made logic puzzles across 300-odd genres — free, no registration, solvable in the browser on a phone
Last week's Nikoli pick was the door rather than the room: rules and one worked example. This is the room. The Fillomino index alone runs to over 850 numbered puzzles, sorted by size from 5×5 up to 21×21 and marked from easy to hard, each one playable interactively with the solution behind a click. The rule, in the site's own English: enter a number in every cell; cells holding the same number form regions, and each region must contain exactly as many cells as that number says. No clues outside, no arithmetic — just regions arguing with each other over where they stop.
The interface is German by default, but the rules can be shown in English (German, English and Japanese are all available), and the grids themselves need no language at all. Nurikabe and Slitherlink have archives of the same depth, and there are roughly three hundred further genres indexed beside them.
Why it's here: it is thirty years of hand-set pencil puzzles sitting in plain sight with no app, no streak counter and no daily reset — and because the Fillomino daily that took a 👍 a fortnight ago gives you one puzzle a day, where this gives you eight hundred and fifty whenever you want them.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekJane Street's October puzzle, out yesterday — you have until the end of the month

"It's a Metric, Too" — Jane Street, October 2026
Cut an 11-by-11 grid into "states": connected groups of squares, each symmetric under rotation or reflection. Moving between two neighbouring squares costs the size of the state you are leaving — or, crossing a border, the smaller of the two states. The numbers printed in the grid tell you the cheapest cost of reaching any state capitol from there.

What are the shapes of the states?
That is the shape of it rather than the exact wording — the puzzle page gives the real definitions, including what makes a square a state's capitol and the precise cost rule, and they are worth reading slowly because the whole thing turns on them. The answer you submit is a single number derived from the finished map, so you will know when you have it. Jane Street publishes one puzzle a month and the solution only after the month closes, so there is no way to peek and a full four weeks to chip at it — the September one, "Hint Singles", and everything before it are sitting solved in the archive if you want to see the house style first.
