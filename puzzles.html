Gentle Published last night

A Delicate Balance

by UncleLogic, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published last night, 8 October 2026, author-estimated easy
Normal sudoku, plus one line constraint you almost certainly have not met: along each orange see-saw line, the sums of adjacent pairs alternate between under 10 and over 10. Take any two neighbouring cells on the line, add them; the next overlapping pair tips the other way. The setter adds the detail that makes it work: no adjacent pair on a see-saw line may sum to exactly 10, so there is no sitting on the fence.
It is a lovely little mechanism — a parity-ish rule that is not about parity at all, but about which side of 10 each pair falls. Rated easy by the setter, which makes it the right place to learn the constraint before anyone builds something cruel out of it.
Why it's here: a constraint type that does not exist in any daily app's rotation, from a setter who has never appeared in this newsletter, posted about seven hours ago. No outside clues anywhere in it.

Medium Published this morning

Region Sum Lies

by marty_sears, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published at 01:52 this morning, 9 October 2026; community-rated medium, 100% approval from its first 14 solvers
The title is the joke and the rule. Underneath are region sum lines — box borders cut each blue line into segments, and every segment of a given line has the same sum, which you are not told. Over the top: every row, column and box contains exactly one liar cell, whose real value is not the digit it shows. The nine displayed liar digits are all different, and their nine real values are all different too, so between them they cover 1 to 9 twice over. No blue line may contain more than one liar. A black dot between two cells means one of them is double the other.
So a line's segment sums are computed from values that may not be the values you can see, and the no-two-liars-per-line rule is what gives you a grip on where the lies can hide. The answer-entry flourish is worth a look too: you record each row's liar in a blue arrow and its true value in the green circle beside it, so the solved grid carries its own confession.
Why it's here: region sum lines on their own have only ever earned a 🙂 here, twice. This is what happens when a real setter stacks something strange on top of them — and it is seven hours old, from the portal rather than anybody's video roster.

Monster Setter-rated 10/10

Audible

by James Sinclair, in Artisanal Sudoku Volume 248 — published 5 October 2026
Normal sudoku with four familiar constraints and no new ones, which is the point: arrows (the digits along an arrow sum to the digit in its circle, repeats allowed where the other rules permit), renban lines (each purple line holds a non-repeating set of consecutive digits in any order), a shaded square marks an even digit and a shaded circle an odd one, and a white dot between two cells means they are consecutive. Nothing there needs explaining. The difficulty is entirely in the interaction.
The setter publishes rough difficulty estimates and gives this one two of them — 10/10 and 6/10 — for its two versions, without saying which is which; the second link below is the version with extra clues, so that is the one to start on if the first refuses to move. His note on the volume is that it holds two puzzles that could each have been the headline, and one that may be the toughest thing he has set in a year.
Why it's here: a one-man weekly newsletter rather than a channel, and the only sudoku in this issue whose constraints are all standard — a fair test of whether the difficulty you enjoy comes from novel rules or from depth.

🌍 The wider puzzle worldA championship round from the federation itself, and the genre that invented lying clues

Tough New well

WPF Puzzle Grand Prix 2026, Round 8 — India

set by Prasanna Seshadri for the World Puzzle Federation — the round ran 21–26 August 2026, and the booklets stay up for anyone
Twenty-four puzzles, 1,096 points, 90 minutes if you want the real thing. The genre list is a tour of everything pencil puzzles do that sudoku does not: Nurikabe and Nuribou (shade cells so each number sits in a white region of exactly its own size), Nanro, Heyawake in an irregular variant, Yajilin, Castle Wall, Pentopia, Nori Chain and Number Rope. The last four puzzles are hybrids worth the download on their own — Nurikabe spliced with Nuribou at 95 points, Nanro with Heyawake at 120, a Castle Wall that is also a Yajilin, and a Pentopia crossed with Nori Chain.
Two of the twenty-four are Scrabble puzzles, which place given words into a grid — skip those two, they are word puzzles and this newsletter does not do those. Everything else is shading, loops and numbers. The instruction booklet gives every genre's full rules with a worked example, so no prior knowledge is needed; print it and the puzzle file and you have a weekend.
Why it's here: this is the World Puzzle Federation's own competition archive, which this newsletter has never opened in nine issues — championship-tier material that has never had a video made about it. The timing is not an accident either: Seshadri's federation, Logic Masters India, hosts the World Sudoku & Puzzle Championship in Kolkata from this Sunday, 11 October, and this round was India's calling card for it.

Medium Learn a genre

Usowan — the liar's shading puzzle

Nikoli, the Japanese publisher that named modern sudoku and invented a great many of these genres
Shade some cells, under four rules. Numbered cells are never shaded. A number says how many of its orthogonal neighbours are shaded — except that in each region bordered by bold lines, exactly one number is wrong. Shaded cells may never touch each other horizontally or vertically, and the unshaded cells must all stay connected as one group.
That "exactly one wrong number per region" is doing something quite different from a normal false-clue gimmick: it is a counting constraint. Each bold region must contain precisely one liar, so finding a region where two numbers cannot both be true tells you something, and so does a region where every number looks satisfiable. If you have just solved Region Sum Lies upstairs, this is the same idea with the sudoku stripped away — the lie is the whole mechanism rather than a layer on top.
Why it's here: Usowan exists nowhere in a daily-games app, and Nikoli's page gives the rules with a sample, a mid-solve position and the solution, so the genre takes about five minutes to pick up. Fair warning that Nikoli is the one well here that has been used before — Shakashaka, a fortnight ago, which earned the wider world a 👍; there are 50 genres on their index and this is a different corner of it.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekIBM's October challenge — a new well, and nobody has published the answer

"Ultimate Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe" — IBM Research, Ponder This, October 2026
Ultimate tic-tac-toe is played on a 9×9 board of nine small boards; number its cells 0 to 80. Find a game that runs the full 81 moves and fills the board, in which each of the last nine moves wins one small board — none of them tied, and none of them won any earlier — and the eighty-first move wins the game itself.
A combinatorial construction problem rather than a trick question: you are not asked how many such games exist, you are asked to produce one, as a sequence of 81 numbers. The page states the move-numbering convention and shows a worked example game that ends with O taking a diagonal of small boards, so there is no ambiguity about what counts as an answer — and a bonus for anyone who builds the 5×5 version, 25 small boards of 25 cells each, moves numbered 0 to 624. Solutions go to IBM by email. The challenge went up on 1 October and the page is already listing the names of solvers who have cracked it, but IBM does not publish the solution until the month is out, so nothing below the line spoils it.
