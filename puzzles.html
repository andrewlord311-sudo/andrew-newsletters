🧩 Sudoku variantsFog that lifts as you solve, a rule that lets a cell's value lie about its digit, and a renban tangle with a mercy option

Gentle Set at 1:20 this morning

One Knight's Gambit

by meggen033, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published at 01:20 this morning, 23 October 2026; setter-estimated 2 out of 5, no community rating yet
Normal sudoku, three familiar constraints and one piece of stagecraft. Cells a chess knight's move apart cannot contain the same digit. Digits in a cage do not repeat and sum to the clue in its top left. Digits along each thermometer increase from the bulb end. And then: the grid is partially covered with fog, and correctly placed digits clear the fog around that cell. The setter states plainly that no guessing is required.
Fog changes the feel of a solve more than its logic. You cannot survey the grid and plan, because most of it is not visible yet; you work from what the uncovered region forces, and each correct digit pays you in new information. The anti-knight rule is the right partner for it — it reaches diagonally outward past the box walls, so a digit you place in the clear can rule out cells you have not even seen. Set at an estimated 2 out of 5, which for a fog puzzle means the uncovering carries you.
Why it's here: meggen033 is the single most prolific setter on variantsudoku.com's index — 136 hand-set puzzles there, more than anyone — and has never once appeared in this newsletter. Nothing in a daily-games app hides its own grid from you, and the last four gentle slots here went to a comparison rule and three line constraints, not to a mechanic.

Medium Published this morning

Caesium

by Nicolas Duhail, setting as NicoD, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published at 05:30 this morning, 23 October 2026; setter-estimated 3 out of 5
Normal sudoku, plus the rule the puzzle is named for: any cell orthogonally adjacent to a cell containing the digit 5 also has a value of 5. Then three constraints that all read values rather than digits — values in a cage sum to the number in its top left corner, and digits may not repeat in a cage but values may; values separated by an X sum to 10, and not all Xs are given; white dots join cells with consecutive values, and not all dots are given.
That digit-versus-value distinction is the whole puzzle. A cell you have filled with a 2 can be worth 5 to every cage, X and dot that touches it, purely because something next to it is a 5 — so the same cell tells the truth to the sudoku and a lie to the arithmetic, and the two accounts have to be kept separately in your head. The 5s radiate: place one and its four neighbours become 5s by value, which can cascade into cages you thought you had finished. Note the asymmetry in the cage rule, too — repeated values are fine inside a cage, repeated digits are not, which makes "this cage sums to 20" a much looser statement than usual.
Why it's here: modifier-cell puzzles — where a cell's arithmetic value comes apart from its digit — are a genre this newsletter has never run, and they do not exist in any daily app's rotation, because the interface has to carry two numbers per cell. A new setter here as well, and at 3 out of 5 it is the week's middle slot rather than a monster.

Tough Volume 250

Scowl

by James Sinclair, in Artisanal Sudoku Volume 250 — published 19 October 2026; difficulty given as 8 out of 10, with a second estimate of 6
Normal sudoku, all clues standard, and three constraints stacked on one grid. Cages: the digits inside each cage sum to the small number in its top left corner, and digits cannot repeat within a cage. Renban lines: each purple line holds a non-repeating set of consecutive digits, in any order. Cells with a shaded square are even.
Renban and killer pull in opposite directions and that is the point. A cage fixes a total but says nothing about the spread; a renban fixes the spread — some run of consecutive digits — but says nothing about where in the grid the run sits. Where a line crosses a cage, the two facts multiply, because a consecutive set of known length has a known minimum and maximum sum. The shaded-even cells are the lever: parity is what turns "some consecutive run" into a specific one, since a run of n consecutive digits contains a forced number of evens. The milestone volume comes with a kindness, too — there is an extra-clues version of the same grid if the original stalls, which is a more honest mercy than a hint.
Why it's here: a different platform from the morning's two — a setter publishing straight to his own subscribers, no channel and no portal — and the three puzzles in a volume are hand-graded, which is why the week's tough slot can be a confident 8 rather than a shrug. Last week's top slot went to a puzzle nobody had solved at all; this one has been test-solved and has a lifeline.

Medium New well · dated puzzles

Slitherlink

at PuzzleMix — numbered and dated, the newest being Slitherlink 2649 on 21 October 2026, with 2647, 2644 and 2643 behind it on the 16th, 10th and 8th
The classic Nikoli loop genre, in the site's own words: "draw one single loop by joining the dots with horizontal and vertical lines"; "the loop cannot touch or cross itself at any point"; and "wherever a number appears in the grid, there must be that many horizontal and vertical line segments adjacent to it".
It is the purest deduction in the pencil-puzzle canon and it works nothing like a sudoku. There are no digits to place — every clue is about edges, and the engine of the solve is the parity of the loop: any line you draw must eventually come back, so a region's boundary has to be crossed an even number of times. That single fact resolves clues that look locally ambiguous, and it is why a 0 and a 3 sitting diagonally adjacent are worth more than either alone. Numbered into the 2,600s, and nothing resets at midnight.
Why it's here: a constraint-logic genre with no number placement at all, which is the main gap in what this newsletter has covered — and a back catalogue rather than a daily, so a good evening can be four puzzles instead of one.

Gentle New well · shading genre

Nurikabe

at PuzzleMix — the same free browser play, rules page with a worked explanation; a WPF Puzzle Grand Prix staple, and one of the genres in this year's India round
A shading puzzle with four rules, as the site states them: shade some squares so that each number sits in an unshaded region of exactly that size; each unshaded region contains exactly one number, and unshaded regions cannot touch orthogonally; shaded squares cannot form a solid 2×2 block; and all shaded squares form one single connected area. One logical solution, no guessing.
The two global rules are what make it. "All the shading is connected" and "no 2×2 of shading" fight each other constantly — the sea has to reach everywhere without ever pooling — and most of the real deductions come from that tension rather than from the numbers. The numbers mostly tell you where the islands cannot grow: two clues facing each other across a gap force shading between them, because their islands may not touch.
Why it's here: it is the gentlest way into the shading family, and worth knowing because it turns up in championship sets — it was one of the genres in the WPF Puzzle Grand Prix's India round this August, alongside Yajilin and Castle Wall. A free numbered archive is a better place to learn it than a competition booklet.

Result Kolkata, 11–18 October

Kolkata, settled — and a British first

19th World Sudoku Championship and 33rd World Puzzle Championship, hosted by Logic Masters India — reported by the World Puzzle Federation on 13 and 18 October 2026
The puzzle championship went to Ken Endo of Japan — his sixth world title. Freddie Hand of the UK, last year's champion, was second, and Christian König of Germany third, his second bronze after Beijing in 2024. Japan won the team event, Germany took silver, and Great Britain took bronze — the WPF's report calls it Britain's first team bronze in WPC history, from Freddie Hand, James McGowan, Neil Zussman and Peter Roder. Behind the podium: Takuma Muto fourth, Selene Luo fifth, Kevin Sun sixth, Valentin Miakinen seventh.
The sudoku championship went to Ming Letian of China, from a field of 153 competitors, with Hu Yuxuan second and Kota Morinishi third; the defending champion Tantan Dai finished fourth. China won the team title as well, and the United States took silver by a single point.
Why it's here: last week's issue said the WPC was being solved as it landed, so here is the answer. Worth the names more than the medals — Endo, Hand, König, Ming and Morinishi all set as well as solve, and a setter whose puzzles you enjoy is the most reliable source of future ones. The championship sets themselves are closed-door, but this newsletter has linked the instruction booklets twice, and those puzzles are solvable at home.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekJane Street's October problem — still open, so there is nothing to spoil

"It's a Metric, Too" — Jane Street's puzzle for this month
An 11-by-11 region is to be divided into states: connected, symmetric groups of squares. A state's capitol is the one square left fixed by every non-trivial symmetry of that state — when exactly one such square exists. Travel costs: stepping between orthogonally adjacent squares inside a state costs that state's size, and crossing between two different states costs the smaller of the two sizes. Each numbered cell in the grid tells you the lowest possible travel cost from that cell to any capitol. Find the division into states.
The pleasure here is that the metric is the puzzle. Distance is not geometric at all — it is priced by the sizes of the regions you are standing in, so a big state is expensive to walk across and a small one is cheap, and a low number on a cell is evidence about the shape of its neighbourhood rather than its position. The symmetry condition does the rest of the work: it sharply limits which blobs are legal states, and "exactly one fixed square" quietly rules out whole families of them. Submission is a single number — label every cell with its state's size, sum each row, then add up the squares of those row sums. The puzzle is live and the page asks solvers to check back before the end of the month, so for once the answer is not a click away. There is nothing to resist.
