🧩 Sudoku variantsA whisper-and-dot warm-up, a killer on the diagonal, and three kinds of line stacked on one grid

Gentle Set yesterday morning

Sinusoid Rhythm

by GiAnMMV, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published 29 October 2026 at 10:15; rated 86% by solvers, 69 solves, and marked a very easy puzzle
Normal sudoku and two constraints, both in the portal's own English wording. German whispers: every two consecutive digits along a green line must have a difference of at least 5. Kropki: two digits separated by a white dot are consecutive; two digits separated by a black dot have a 1:2 ratio.
These two rules are natural opponents, which is why the pairing is a classic and why it stays gentle. A whispers line wants gaps of five or more, so it drives digits to the extremes and all but bans the middle — a 5 can never sit on one at all, since nothing is five away from it in both directions. A white dot wants a gap of exactly one. Put them near each other and the contradiction does your work for you: the dot tells you two cells are neighbours in value, the line tells you its cells cannot be, and the digits that survive both are few. The black dots then pin the small end, because a 1:2 ratio has very few homes once the high digits are committed to the line.
Why it's here: whispers is the single constraint this newsletter's ratings like best, and a 69-solve, 86%-rated grid published yesterday is the cheapest possible way in — this is a twenty-minute puzzle, not an evening. GiAnMMV has not appeared here before.

San José

by ICHTUES, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published 29 October 2026 at 08:15; the portal grades it a difficult puzzle, level 4 of 5, rated 91% across its first 22 solves
Two constraints only, stated plainly on the page. Normal sudoku rules apply. The positive diagonal must contain all of the digits 1 to 9. In cages, digits must sum to the small clue in the top left corner of the cage, where one is given, and digits cannot repeat within a cage.
Note the clause doing the damage: where one is given. A cage without a total still forbids repeats, so it is a pure no-repeat region sitting on top of the boxes, and those are the cages that reshape the grid rather than the ones you can add up. The positive diagonal — bottom-left to top-right — then cuts across every box in its path and turns nine scattered cells into a tenth region that must hold all nine digits. Where an uncaged cage crosses the diagonal is where this puzzle actually begins.
Why it's here: a 91% rating from the first day's solvers is the portal's strongest signal, and it is the kind of grid where the rule list is short and the solve is not — two lines of rules, level 4 of 5.

Handlebars

by damo_89, a guest setter in James Sinclair's Artisanal Sudoku Volume 251 — published 26 October 2026; graded 8 out of 10, with a second estimate of 6
Normal sudoku, all clues standard, and three different kinds of line on one grid. Arrows: the digits along an arrow sum to the digit in its connected circle, and may repeat if other rules allow. Renban lines: the digits on a purple line form a non-repeating set of consecutive digits, in any order. Entropic lines: every three adjacent cells on an orange line contain one low digit (1–3), one middle digit (4–6) and one high digit (7–9).
Three lines, three completely different kinds of statement, and that is the whole pleasure of it. An arrow is arithmetic: it fixes a total and nothing else. A renban is about spread: it fixes that the digits form a consecutive run, but says nothing about which run or in what order. An entropic line is about neither — it is a statement about which third of the digit range each cell falls in, repeating every three cells regardless of size. So a cell where two lines cross is being described twice in incompatible vocabularies, and converting between them is the solve. The useful bridge is that a run of consecutive digits of known length has a known minimum and maximum sum, which is what lets a renban talk to an arrow; and entropy is what tells you roughly where that run sits before you know exactly which digits it holds.
Why it's here: arrows, renban and region-style lines are exactly the constraints that have drawn 👍 here all autumn, and this is all three at once rather than one of them. The volume is hand-graded by its editor rather than crowd-rated, which is why the week's hard slot can be a confident 8 — and if it bites, there is a version of the same grid with extra clues, which is a more honest lifeline than a hint. Not a portal and not a channel: a setter publishing straight to subscribers.

🌍 The wider puzzle worldThree hundred genres in an Austrian archive, and forty-one puzzles that never run out

Medium New well · ~300 genres

Heyawake, and the Janko archive behind it

at janko.at — a long-running Austrian puzzle archive with over 300 genres, each numbered, credited to its setter, and solvable in the browser; rules given in German, English and Japanese side by side
Heyawake (へやわけ) is a shading puzzle, and the site states the rules in English beside the German and the Japanese. Blacken some cells of the grid. A number in a region indicates how many cells in this region must be blackened; in regions without a number any amount of cells may be blackened, maybe none. Black cells may not be orthogonally adjacent, diagonally is allowed. Stripes of adjacent white cells may not span across more than one region border horizontally or vertically. All white cells must form a single orthogonally-connected area. Start with Nr. 810, set by Iwa Daigeki, taken from the Bachelor Seal Puzzle blog with the author's permission; the genre index lists 810 of them sorted by date, size or difficulty, graded from easy in eight steps up to hard.
What makes Heyawake worth the half-hour it costs to learn is that the region walls and the shading rules are at cross purposes. A region's number is local, but the two rules that bite are not — the white cells all have to stay connected, and a straight white stripe may cross only one region border, so it can touch two regions but never three. That stripe rule is the strange one, and it is where the puzzle lives: a long clear corridor becomes evidence about regions it merely passes through, so a deduction in one corner is made out of geometry on the other side of the grid. Nothing in a sudoku works like that.
Why it's here: the last four wider-world picks have all been archives of dated puzzles that run out. This one is the opposite problem — the index alone holds 810 Heyawake, and the site carries the same depth across Fillomino, Slitherlink, Masyu, Kakuro, Nurikabe and roughly three hundred more, including variant sub-genres with their own folders. Every puzzle is credited, dated and interactive, the ones with a clean logical path are colour-highlighted on the index, and none of it is behind an account.

Gentle 41 puzzles · nothing runs out

Simon Tatham's Portable Puzzle Collection

41 one-player logic puzzles, re-implementations of existing genres, MIT-licensed and playable in a browser tab — each has a JavaScript build at js/<name>.html, no account, no install, no daily limit
The names are Tatham's own rather than the Japanese ones, which is the only barrier to entry. The translations worth knowing: Loopy is Slitherlink, Towers is Skyscrapers, Keen is Calcudoku, Unequal is Futoshiki, Bridges is Hashiwokakero, Filling is Fillomino, Pattern is a nonogram, Light Up is Akari, and Solo is sudoku, including killer and jigsaw variants. Also in there: Galaxies, Palisade, Pearl, Range, Signpost, Singles, Slant, Tents, Tracks, Undead and Unruly.
Each puzzle is generated rather than curated, from a random seed you can read off and type back in — so a grid is reproducible and shareable, but there is no queue to exhaust and no midnight reset to wait for. That is a genuinely different relationship with a puzzle from everything else in this newsletter: the archives above hand you someone's composition, and this hands you an endless supply of the genre so you can drill the technique until the deductions are automatic. For a genre you have read the rules of but never actually solved — Tracks, Range and Palisade are the usual gaps — it is the fastest way in.
Why it's here: no daily app, no channel, no setter roster; just forty-one pencil-puzzle genres you can open in a tab and never run out of. It is also the best companion to the Janko archive above — learn a genre here on generated grids, then go and solve a composed one there.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekPeter Winkler, by way of Futility Closet — the answer is one click away, so don't click

"An Orderly Vacation" — from Peter Winkler's Mathematical Puzzles (2021)
A certain island contains a network of roads. Each road segment — between one intersection and the next — has its own name. If d is the average number of road segments that meet at an intersection, show that it is possible to take a drive on the island that traverses at least d road segments and hits them in alphabetical order.
This is a proof, not a number, which makes it a different animal from the last few weeks' puzzles — there is nothing to submit and no grid to fill, just a claim you either see your way to or you don't. Two things are worth noticing before you start. The first is that d is an average, and averages are the standard way into this kind of argument: somewhere there must be an intersection at least as good as average, and often that is the whole proof. The second is that the alphabetical condition sounds like a crippling restriction and is secretly a gift — it imposes an order on the segments, and an order is exactly the structure a greedy argument needs. Winkler's book is the modern successor to Kordemsky in this newsletter's rotation, and Futility Closet keeps the answer behind a "Click for Answer" toggle, so the page is safe to open and read.
