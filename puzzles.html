🧩 Sudoku variantsThree platforms, three setters new to this newsletter — gentle, monster, and one in between

Gentle Published yesterday

Symmetease — Region Sum Lines Sudoku

by Philip Newman, in the Genuinely Approachable Sudoku series at GM Puzzles — published 24 September 2026. His 159th puzzle for the series.
Standard sudoku, plus one line rule and nothing else: along each blue line, the digits in every box the line passes through add up to the same total. The line tells you nothing about individual cells — only that each box's worth of it carries equal weight. Where a line crosses a box boundary is therefore the whole game: a four-cell segment in one box has to match a two-cell segment in the next, which pins the small segment high and the long one low before you have placed anything at all.
Why it's here: region sum lines are the same family as the sum-line puzzle that took a 👍 last week, and this section's ratings have been unambiguous that established line constraints beat novelty. The series is a moderated daily with published target times rather than anyone's weekly video roster — this one is set at seven minutes for strong solvers, thirteen for the rest of us, and the editor got it in 2:36.

Medium Published overnight

Floor 47 — Numbered Rooms and Skyscrapers, on the same clues

by damo_89, on Logic Masters Deutschland's Rätselportal — published overnight, 25 September 2026, rated 3–4 stars after test-solving
Standard sudoku, with two established outside-clue genres sharing one set of numbers. Skyscrapers: a clue counts how many digits are visible from that side, where a digit is visible only if it is taller than everything before it — read the row as a skyline and count the buildings you can actually see. Numbered rooms: a clue is a digit that sits in the Nth cell from that side, where N is whatever digit is in the first cell — so the nearest cell tells you where to look, and the clue tells you what you'll find there.
The trick is that each clue digit appears twice outside the grid — once doing the skyscraper job and once doing the numbered-room job. The same number means two entirely different things depending on which hat it is wearing, and half the solve is working out which pairs up with which.
Why it's here: both genres are championship staples rather than inventions, which is what the 👎 on an invented constraint two weeks ago was asking for — the novelty here is in the construction, not in a rule you have to learn from scratch. There's a cosmetic-lite version too if the styling gets in the way.

9 out of 10 The setter's own favourite kind

Hodgepodge

by James Sinclair, in Artisanal Sudoku Volume 246 — published 21 September 2026, setter-rated 9/10
Four established constraints at once, all of them ones you already know. Arrows: the digits along an arrow add up to the digit in the circle it comes from. Renban: a purple line holds a set of consecutive digits, no repeats, in any order. Quadruple circles: a digit printed in a white circle on a grid intersection appears in one of the four cells around it. X: two cells either side of an X add to 10. Nothing invented, nothing to look up — the 9/10 comes from having to hold all four in your head simultaneously.
Why it's here: the setter's own note on this volume is that he gives a puzzle a name implying he doesn't like it — Hodgepodge being the example he picks — and that those are often his favourites. It's the week's genuine weekend puzzle: a one-man newsletter, hand-set, no video, no channel. If 9/10 is more than a Saturday deserves, the alternative version below carries extra clues and he rates that one 6/10.

New genre Straight from Nikoli

Shakashaka — the one where the white space has to come out rectangular

Nikoli, the Japanese publisher that gave modern sudoku its name and invented a good half of the pencil-puzzle canon — rules page with a worked sample
You place black triangles in the white squares — four orientations, each filling half a square diagonally — and you may not place anything in the black squares. The constraint that does all the work: whatever white space is left over, once the triangles are in, must form rectangles and squares. Not roughly rectangular. Every remaining white region, exactly a rectangle or a square, at any angle. A number in a black square counts the black triangles orthogonally adjacent to it, and those numbers are the only arithmetic in the puzzle.
It is an unusually satisfying rule to deduce against, because the shape requirement propagates: a single triangle forces its neighbours into alignment to keep the leftover white honest, and the chains run a long way. Nikoli's page states the rules and walks a sample grid through to its solution, so you can learn it and solve one without leaving the page.
Why it's here: Shakashaka has never appeared in this newsletter, it isn't on any daily-games lineup, and it is nowhere near a sudoku — the well it comes from is the one that dug most of the others. Worth knowing that when last week's Fillomino daily took a 👍, the reason was a new genre rather than a new puzzle. Fair warning: this is rules plus one worked example on the publisher's own page, not a daily — it's the door, not the room.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekThree cups, a random swap, and a number for an answer — no self-reference this time

"Can You Swap the Cups?" — The Fiddler, by Zach Wissner-Gross, 18 September 2026
My friend has three cups, labelled "A", "B" and "C" in a row. She randomly picks two different cups and swaps their positions. Then she does this again and again, picking a random pair each time, until all three cups are back in their original order.

On average, how many swaps would you expect until the cups return to their original order?
Last week's puzzle turned on a statement about itself and got a 🙂 rather than a 👍, so this one is the opposite kind: no paradox, no trick reading, just a question with a number at the end of it. What makes it better than it looks is that the obvious approach — enumerate the arrangements, notice there are only six, set up the expectation — is exactly right, and the answer still isn't the one most people guess. There are only three cups and three possible swaps, which means the whole state space fits in your head on a walk, and it is a small enough room that you can be confident you have actually finished rather than merely stopped. The Fiddler is a weekly column; solutions to each puzzle go up in the following week's edition, so this one's is either live now or will be within the day.
