Gentle Artisanal Sudoku

Artisanal Sudoku, Volume 241 — entropic lines and white dots

by James Sinclair — a new hand-crafted variant sudoku every Monday morning
Normal sudoku rules, plus two constraints. An entropic line: any three consecutive cells along the line must contain one low digit (1–3), one middle digit (4–6) and one high digit (7–9) — in any order. A white dot between two cells: those two digits are consecutive. Sinclair pitches the newsletter at solvers who want the variants without the brutality — each issue ships beginner-friendly guides to the rulesets it uses, and this volume includes a walkthrough of cages. Every puzzle comes with a SudokuPad link, so it plays in the browser.
Why you won't have seen it: a one-person subscription newsletter with 240-odd back issues, entirely outside the YouTube-channel ecosystem.

Medium janko.at

X-Sums Sudoku, on janko.at

from the Janko brothers' Austrian puzzle archive — solvable in-browser, free, no account
Normal sudoku rules, plus clues printed outside the grid. The clue gives the sum of the first X cells reading inwards — where X is the digit in the very first cell of that row or column. So if a row starts with a 5, the clue is the total of those first five cells; if it starts with a 2, it's just the first two. The reason it bites: the clue's meaning depends on a digit you haven't placed yet, so each one is really a menu of possibilities that collapses as the grid fills.
Why you won't have seen it: janko.at is a German-language archive of tens of thousands of logic puzzles going back decades, with its own in-browser solver. It has roughly two dozen sudoku variant families indexed — Rossini, Randsummen, Wolkenkratzer, Kropki, Konsekutiv, Ziegelmauer — and almost none of it circulates in English-speaking puzzle circles. Difficulty here is my read of the genre, not a rating the site publishes.

Monster World Puzzle Federation

The Sudoku Grand Prix 2026 archive — eight rounds, free

World Puzzle Federation, set by a different national team each round
The WPF's Sudoku GP runs eight 90-minute rounds a year, each authored by a different country's puzzle association, and every round is a mix of classic sudoku and variants — the variant list is published in advance in each round's instruction booklet. Round 8 closed on 12 August, which means the whole 2026 season is now sitting in the archive as free PDFs with their booklets. This is the material national teams train on, so treat the difficulty badge seriously; but the booklets explain every variant from scratch, which makes them an unusually good way to meet a genre you don't know.
Why you won't have seen it: championship-tier sudoku that is distributed as competition PDFs rather than as clickable dailies, so it never surfaces in the usual feeds.

Tough Live this weekend

WPF Puzzle GP 2026, Round 8 — hosted by India, open now

World Puzzle Federation — the published schedule puts Round 8 at 21–26 August
The Puzzle GP is the Sudoku GP's non-sudoku sibling: pencil-puzzle genres — loops, shading, region division, object placement — set by a different national team each round, 90 minutes on the clock, free to enter and open to anyone who registers. India set this one, and the window covers exactly this weekend. You pick your own start time inside the window, so it fits round a Saturday morning. The instruction booklet goes up ahead of the round and defines every genre in it, so unfamiliar puzzle types aren't a barrier — that's the format's whole design.
Why you won't have seen it: the competition circuit is a parallel puzzle world to the daily-app one, and this round is live for six days only.

Medium puzz.link

puzz.link — the pencil-puzzle plumbing

an open-source browser solver covering something like a hundred genres
Not a puzzle site so much as the infrastructure the puzzle world runs on: an open-source in-browser editor and solver where a whole puzzle is encoded in the URL, which is how constructors hand each other work. The genre list is the good bit — Slitherlink (one continuous non-crossing loop; each number says how many of its four edges the loop uses), Nurikabe (shade one connected wall that contains no 2×2 block and isolates each numbered island at exactly its stated size), Heyawake, Masyu, Hashiwokakero, Fillomino and dozens more, each with rules and playable examples. Thomas Snyder of Grandmaster Puzzles has written it up as the digital-solving option he recommends.
Why you won't have seen it: it has no feed, no daily and no marketing — you find it because a setter sends you a link.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekIBM Research's Ponder This — monthly since 1998, and nothing like a Facebook maths post

"The Wheel of Buttons" — Ponder This, August 2026
A game show has a wheel carrying N×N buttons, equally spaced and perfectly symmetric. Each button is either on or off — and nothing on the wheel tells you which. Pressing a button toggles it. At the start the states are random, subject to one condition: not every button is on. Can you get them all on?
The catch is the symmetry: the wheel can be rotated, so you never quite know which button you are pressing relative to where you started. Posed by Sanandan Swaminathan and posted on 31 July; the full statement — including exactly what you're allowed to observe between presses, which is the crux — is on IBM's page. Answers go to the Haifa lab by email and correct original solutions get their author named when the month closes, so nothing is spoiled by looking now.
