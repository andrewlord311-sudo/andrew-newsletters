Gentle Grandmaster Puzzles · approachable series

Almost Palindrome Sudoku

by clover!, on Grandmaster Puzzles — posted 3 November 2026, their 195th puzzle for the site's Genuinely Approachable Sudoku team. The site's own target times are 7:30 for two party hats and 14:00 for one, which is where the Gentle badge comes from rather than from me guessing
Two rules, in GM Puzzles' own wording. Insert a number from 1 to 9 into each cell so that no number repeats in any row, column, or bold region. The numbers along each grey line form a palindrome — except that each line contains a single pair of non-matching numbers, and working out which pair that is, is part of the puzzle.
A plain palindrome line is one of the most generous constraints in the game: read it from either end and it says the same thing, so the first cell equals the last, the second equals the second-last, and a line of five collapses to three unknowns. This takes that and puts exactly one lie in it. The effect is lovely, because you can no longer simply mirror the line — you have to mirror it and then find the one place the mirror fails, which means a pairing you have already pencilled in is evidence rather than proof. Count the cells first: an odd-length line has a middle cell that is its own partner and so can never be the mismatched pair, which is usually where the first real deduction lives.
Why it's here: palindromes are a line constraint, and lines are what the 👍 ratings have rewarded for two months running. It is also a deliberate change of setter — the only previous GM Puzzles pick here was Bill Murphy's Fivetress in October, which got a 👎, and clover! sets a completely different kind of grid. Nowhere near Cracking the Cryptic's channel.

Atascadero

by James Sinclair, in Artisanal Sudoku Volume 253 — published Monday 9 November 2026. A Substack with over ten thousand subscribers that puts out brand-new hand-set variant sudoku every Monday morning; the puzzles in the free edition are free to play
Three rules, quoted as the setter gives them. Normal sudoku rules apply (fill each row, column and 3×3 box with the digits 1–9 once each), and all clues are standard. German whispers: along green lines, digits differ from their neighbors by at least 5. Digits in cells separated by a black dot have a 1:2 ratio.
The pairing is the point. A whispers line forces every digit on it to one extreme or the other and bans 5 from the line entirely, since nothing on 1–9 sits five away from a 5 in either direction — so the line runs high, low, high, low, and you can often fix its pattern before placing a single digit. A black dot is a doubling clue, and the two interact sharply: the dot's possible pairs are 1–2, 2–4, 3–6 and 4–8, every one of which pairs a low digit with another fairly low one. Put a black dot on or beside a whispers line and the high end of the line is immediately in trouble, because an 8 can only be doubled from 4 and a 6 only from 3. That is the whole engine, and at a stated 5 out of 10 it never gets nastier than that.
If you want more: the same free issue has Creative Accounting, which Sinclair rates 7/10 — cages, arrows, renban lines and shaded even cells all at once, and a genuinely tough weekend puzzle rather than a Friday-morning one.
Why it's here: whispers is the best-rated constraint in this newsletter's history, and this is an entirely new source for it. Sinclair does post to the German portal, but these Monday puzzles are set for the newsletter and appear there first — no video, no channel.

Arrow Sudoku (no. 510)

by akash.doulani, on Logic Masters India's Puzzle Expo — published 10 November 2026. 43 people have solved it, 28 have rated it, and it averages 4.66 out of 5; the site publishes the mean time of a puzzle's ten fastest solvers, which here is 8:03. Not flagged beginner-friendly
Three rules, in the setter's own wording on the site. Place a digit from 1 to 9 into each empty cell in the grid so that each digit appears exactly once in each row, column and 3×3 outlined box. The digit in each circled cell is the sum of digits along the path of its arrow. Digits can repeat within an arrow shape.
That third clause is the one to read twice, because it is the permissive version of the rule and it changes the arithmetic completely. With repeats allowed, a two-cell arrow summing to a circled 6 can be 1+5, 2+4, 4+2, 5+1 or 3+3 — so arrows stop being tidy partition puzzles and become counting problems, and you lean on the row, column and box constraints to kill the repeats instead of the arrow rule doing it for you. The standard openings still work: a circle can never be 1, any arrow of three or more cells forces its circle high, and a long arrow lying inside a single box is heavily constrained because its cells cannot repeat there even though the arrow rule would permit it. Sort the arrows by length before you start.
Why it's here: arrows have twice scored 👍 here, and this is a platform that has never appeared in this newsletter — Logic Masters India's community board, with 1,585 puzzles on it carrying solve counts, ratings and solve times. Different continent, different setters, no overlap at all with the German portal that supplied the last six issues. The puzzle also sits on the setter's own blog, which is where he numbers it 510.

Gentle ★4.8 — best-rated recent non-sudoku

Touching Pentominos

by professorX, on Logic Masters India's Puzzle Expo — published 7 November 2026, an 11×11 grid. Flagged by the site as both LMI-exclusive and beginner-friendly: 39 solvers, rated 4.8 out of 5 from 23 ratings, the best-rated non-sudoku on the board in the last three weeks. Not a sudoku — there are no digits in it at all
Four rules, quoted from the puzzle's page. Shade some cells so that the shaded cells form the shapes of twelve different pentominoes. Each pentomino shape is used exactly once, but can be rotated or reflected. Pentominoes cannot touch along edges, but can touch at corners. If a corner is marked with a dot, then two pentominoes touch at that corner. If a corner is not marked with a dot, then two pentominoes do not touch at that corner.
The clue type is the clever part, and it is worth being precise about it: the dots do not describe cells, they describe corners — the points where four cells meet. A dot there means two different pentominoes are diagonally adjacent across that point. And because the rule is stated in both directions, every unmarked corner is a clue too, which is unusual: a bare corner is a positive statement that no two pieces meet there. That turns the whole grid into a constraint map before you shade a single cell. The other half of the puzzle is inventory — there are exactly twelve pentominoes and you get each one once, so it is as much about which pieces are left as about where they go. The X piece, the plus-shape, has only one orientation and is usually the one to hunt for first.
Why it's here: this is the find of the week. The site flags it exclusive, which means it is published nowhere else — not on the German portal, not on a channel, not in an app, so it cannot have crossed your path. It is also the opposite of a grind despite the 11×11 board: beginner-friendly, and the ten fastest solvers averaged a minute and a half, so it is an evening's curiosity rather than a project.

The November Newcomers Contest

set by Hemant Malani for Logic Masters India — a sudoku contest running 13 to 19 November 2026. It opened yesterday evening and closes Thursday, so the whole window is this weekend plus a few days
This is the one thing in this issue with a deadline, which is why it is here rather than in an archive note. LMI runs monthly contests all year, but the Newcomers series is the one set explicitly for people who have never competed before — a gentler puzzle set and a field of other first-timers, rather than the championship-tier material in their Sudoku Mahabharat and Puzzle Ramayan series. Why it's here: every wider-world pick for the last two months has been something to solve alone — an archive pull, a daily, somebody's back catalogue. LMI hosted this year's World Sudoku and Puzzle Championship in Kolkata in October, so the organising is not amateur. Their calendar runs to 509 events and the archives stay open afterwards.

➗ Maths puzzle of the weekA calculator with no multiply key — how few presses do you need?

"Fastest way to simulate × using +, − and 1/x" — asked by Pranay on Puzzling Stack Exchange, 4 November 2026
A calculator has ten number keys (0, …, 9) and three operation keys — addition (+), subtraction (−) and reciprocal (1/x) — and the equals (=) key. But it's missing the keys for multiplication (×) and division (÷). What's the fewest number of operations in which you can compute the product of any two positive integers? By "number of operations" is meant the number of times you press any of the three operation keys.
Two clarifications from the question itself, both of which matter. You may store any value you have computed so far and recall any stored value at any time — so memory is free, and only the operation keys are counted. And after a commenter found a loophole, the asker added the restriction that the operation keys and the equals key may be pressed only a fixed number of times, independent of the two numbers — which rules out the cheap answer of adding a to itself b times, and is what makes the puzzle hard. The reciprocal key is the whole game: it is the only non-linear thing on the keyboard, so any multiplication you build has to route through it. A reasonable first target is to find any method with a fixed press count at all, then count the presses and start shaving. One answer has been posted and the question is on 16 votes, so there is a confirmed figure to beat — and Stack Exchange hides answers behind spoiler blocks, so the page will not give it away before you are ready.
