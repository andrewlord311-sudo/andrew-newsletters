Try this weekend

Two browser tabs and one real application. Reading the grand staff, a RAW developer, and a synth you write rather than click.

Da Capo

Grand-staff reading drills that know what you are bad at · browser, nothing installed

What it is. Sight-reading practice in a web page. Timed note-recognition on the grand staff, then intervals, chords, rhythms and continuous sight-reading; a weakness heatmap that shows which notes and keys you actually hesitate on; a daily plan and a practice log; Hanon-style scale and exercise work; ear training; and 31 public-domain pieces to read, or your own MusicXML dropped in. A MIDI keyboard is recommended but optional — the computer keyboard and an on-screen piano both work — and there are wait and rhythm modes for playing along.

Why it is for you. You read scores for pleasure and you are trying to get back to classical piano, and the thing that stands between those two is fluency on the grand staff rather than any shortage of repertoire. This is the drill for exactly that, and the heatmap is the part worth having: it turns “I'm slow at reading” into “I'm slow on ledger lines and in four flats”, which is a practisable fact. It takes your own MusicXML, so the pieces you are studying can become the drill rather than someone else's exercises. And it is the right shape for the hour after dinner: open a tab, do five minutes, close it. The children's version of the same argument is obvious, but note the honest limit below before you hand it to the iPad.

To try it. Go to playdacapo.com. That is the entire instruction — nothing to install, no account. Use Chrome or Edge on the Mac if you want to plug a MIDI keyboard in, because Web MIDI exists nowhere else. Two minutes to the first drill; your practice history lives in the browser's own storage (IndexedDB) and can be exported to a file. Signing in is optional and only exists to sync between your own devices.

Risk. MIT, stated in the repository, with third-party pieces under their own licences (VexFlow MIT, the Bravura music font under the SIL Open Font Licence, Verovio LGPL-3.0-or-later) — a clean set, and worth knowing if you ever lift code out of it. The shape is the weak point: 10 stars, 2 forks, 8 open issues, created 24 September and pushed two days ago. That is one person's project, two months old, with essentially nobody else in the code; the saving grace is that nothing is installed, so the cost of it being abandoned is a closed tab. It calls itself version 0.2.0 and says so about its own edges: the thresholds for judging loudness, pedal and key-hold time are provisional, and several translations are still awaiting native-speaker review. The iPad is not promised. The project never addresses touch input, and Web MIDI is desktop Chrome or Edge only — so it will probably open on the iPad with the on-screen piano and may well be fiddly; treat tablet use as untested. On your data it is as good as this gets: practice stays in your browser, nothing is sent to a model, and the site counts visits without cookies. There is an Apple app built from the same code but it is not on the App Store.

CalibRaw

A RAW developer you own instead of rent · Mac (Apple Silicon or Intel), downloadable build

What it is. A free, open-source RAW photo editor: sliders, tone curves, colour grading, a colour mixer, masks (brush, gradient, shape), lens correction, HDR merge, focus stacking, LUT import, presets, tiled export and JPEG XL output. RAW decoding goes through LibRaw and Rawler, so the usual alphabet is covered — .arw, .cr2, .cr3, .nef, .raf, .dng, .orf — alongside JPEG, PNG, HEIC and TIFF. The AI tools (subject, sky, depth and object masks, remove, denoise) run on your own machine. Editing is non-destructive: adjustments live in small .calibraw JSON sidecars next to the photo. There is also a headless command-line mode and an “Edit Replay” that exports your editing session as a 30fps MP4.

Why it is for you. This is squarely in the category you said you wanted: software that is normally expensive, and specifically normally a subscription. A large personal photo library plus a dislike of renting your own tools is the exact case this answers, and the two design decisions that matter for a library you intend to keep for decades are both the right way round — sidecar files mean your originals are never touched and your edits are plain readable JSON rather than a proprietary catalogue, and the AI masks run locally so culling and tidying a shoot does not become an upload. The headless CLI is the part that will interest you a week later: a batch of edits applied from a script is the kind of job your Python-on-the-Mac habits already fit. Keep this on the Mac, where Metal does the work; the Windows machine is the one short of memory.

To try it. There are prebuilt downloads — a macOS .zip for Apple Silicon or Intel, with Flathub, AppImage and .deb on Linux, an installer or portable build on Windows, and Play Store, F-Droid or .apk on Android. No curl | sh, no sudo, no account and no subscription. Building from source is Cargo and Rust 1.92+ if you would rather. An evening: open one shoot, try a sky mask and an export, and watch what the first AI tool downloads. GPU work needs Metal on the Mac (Vulkan or Direct3D 12 elsewhere) — OpenGL-only drivers are not supported.

Risk. GPL-3.0-or-later, which is stricter than most things in this newsletter — fine for using it, something to read properly before you reuse any of its code in your own projects. 344 stars, 27 forks, 20 open issues, created 13 July and pushed yesterday, so four months old and actively worked on; the fork count is on the low side for the stars, which usually means plenty of users and few contributors, i.e. one maintainer carrying it. There is no beta or early-access label anywhere, which on a four-month-old photo editor is a mild warning rather than a reassurance: keep it off anything irreplaceable until you have seen it round-trip a shoot you do not care about. On data the project is explicit and credible: nothing leaves your device, the only network access is downloading the AI models or the ONNX runtime, and that happens once, only after you agree, and is verified by SHA-256 before it runs offline. It does not say how big those models are, so expect an unknown download the first time you touch a mask. Two practical notes: the macOS build arrives as a plain .zip, so expect Gatekeeper to want convincing the first time; and no model, lens-profile or raw-decoder coverage claim should be taken on trust for an unusual camera — try your own files early.

rondocode

Write the synth and the pattern as text, hear it immediately · browser, built for a phone

What it is. A live-coding environment for sound in a browser tab. You write synths — oscillators, filters, envelopes — and patterns in a terse mini-notation, in either JavaScript or rondo, a small language that compiles to JavaScript, and a from-scratch AudioWorklet engine plays them as you type. Sampling is optional. It can bounce a render to a file offline, and there is an optional MCP bridge so a coding agent can drive the session, off unless you set it up. The author's own framing is that it is built to be played on a phone.

Why it is for you. Generative music, Web Audio and writing music as code are three separate interests of yours that this collapses into one tab, and it arrives without the thing that usually stops you: no DAW to open, no plug-in to install, nothing to configure before the first sound. For a composer who writes MIDI from Python and has played with Penrose tiling as a score, a pattern language with a real DSP engine underneath is a sketchpad rather than a toy — a place to try a process, hear it in ten seconds and throw it away. The phone-first design is the practical bit: this is a thing to poke at away from the desk. And if it sticks, the MCP bridge is an obvious experiment for the Claude Code habit.

To try it. Open rondocode.com, load an example, change a number, hear it change. Nothing to install and no account or key mentioned anywhere. Ten minutes to a noise you made, an evening if you start reading the rondo syntax properly. Running your own copy is pnpm install and pnpm dev, which you do not need in order to try it.

Risk. MIT. 74 stars, 9 forks, 13 open issues, created 21 July and last pushed on 11 November — four months old, one author, and eight days since the last commit, which is fine but not the daily activity of this issue's larger picks. Safari and the iPad are a known soft spot rather than a claim: the project ships an iOS and Safari audio diagnostics page at /diag, which tells you Safari audio has given trouble, and nothing anywhere promises tested tablet support — so if the iPad is the device you had in mind, test it with that page before you invest an evening. Two limitations the author states plainly, and they are the honest kind: an offline bounce is not bit-identical to what you just heard, because peaks above 0.89 get scaled down, and renders using sing() are not reproducible across machines. No accounts, keys or telemetry are mentioned; the optional agent bridge is off by default, which is the right default. It is a sketchpad, not a DAW — nothing here replaces Reaper, and it does not pretend to.

For your projects

For the video pipeline — still the only area the ratings have never disagreed with. Something to use, and one pattern to steal.

explainroo

Narrated explainer videos rendered from a script and a scene file · Mac, command line

What it is. A tool that turns a script plus a JavaScript scene file into a finished narrated video — usually with a coding agent writing both. The voice is Kokoro running on your own machine, the word timing comes from Whisper, and the picture is a canvas renderer drawn through headless Chrome; ffmpeg assembles it. Out the other end: an MP4, .srt and .vtt subtitles, a cover image, YouTube chapters when the script names them, optional GIF and transparent MOV, a web player via explainroo export, and ready-made packages for YouTube, Shorts, TikTok, Reels, LinkedIn or X.

Why it is for you. Your pipeline already records HTML scenes with Playwright and joins frames with ffmpeg, so this is not a new idea to you — it is your idea with the two pieces you have not built: a voice that costs nothing and needs no account, and word-level timing, which is what makes captions and on-screen text land on the syllable instead of approximately. Use it for the teaching videos and the hymn channel, where narration plus captions is most of the work and currently the slowest part. Steal one pattern from it even if you never run it: the README carries a table of exactly what leaves your machine for each command, so local and non-local work are separated by command rather than by trust — which is the documentation your own pipeline has no equivalent of, and the thing that makes a tool safe to hand to an agent. It also means the expensive options stay genuinely optional: ElevenLabs or Cartesia for voices, OpenRouter for AI images, each on your own key, each doing nothing until you ask.

To try it. npx --yes explainroo doctor --fetch, which checks your machine and downloads the voice and timing models — about 400 MB, once, from Hugging Face. It wants Node 20.11 or newer, ffmpeg and Chrome or Chromium, and no graphics card, which suits the Mac. No account or API key for the default voice. If you want certain non-English voices, espeak-ng is a brew install (the README says so outright rather than hiding it); Japanese and Chinese additionally want Python. The optional agent plugin for Claude Code is a separate step. An evening for a first two-minute video, most of it spent on the script.

Risk. MIT, with the usual honest caveat that the components keep their own licences (Kokoro, Whisper via Transformers.js, Rough.js, Lucide, MathJax, Playwright, ffmpeg, and fonts under the SIL Open Font Licence) — worth reading before you publish commercially, and not a problem for a channel. The numbers want flagging under this newsletter's own rule: 1,383 stars in eight weeks is fast, and fast star growth is the one number that cannot be told apart from bought attention. Here the supporting shape holds up — 137 forks is about one in ten, which is what real use looks like rather than a bookmarking spree, only 5 open issues, and it was pushed yesterday — but it is two months old, so treat it as promising rather than proven. It calls itself version 0.6.1, and is clear about its unfinished edges: share does nothing yet because the hosted service is not running, there is no presenter feature, and the optional AI clips can get text and diagrams wrong and can cost more than the quoted price. The author develops on Linux; tests run on macOS and Windows too, but Apple Silicon is never named, so do not be surprised if the first run needs a nudge. On data it is as clean as this issue gets: no telemetry, nothing about your usage sent anywhere, voice and timing and rendering all local, and network access limited to those one-off model downloads plus whichever outside service you explicitly invoke. One Linux-only wart documented in the README: the ONNX runtime may try to fetch CUDA files it never uses.

A game

In a browser, on a tablet, with a virtual joystick — and the same seed gives the same universe every time.

Deep Space

Fly to any planet in 1,024 star systems and walk about on it · browser, touch or keyboard

What it is. A procedurally generated space game in a web page, built on Three.js with a WebGL 2 renderer and no loading screens: you fly from interstellar space down to a planet, land, and get out and walk, skydive and fight on seed-generated terrain. The galaxy is a fixed, curated spiral of 1,024 systems, and the same seed always produces the same world. There is a 3D galaxy map, a ship you can walk around inside, six surface weapons, outposts with patrolling robots, and a black hole to find. Quality settles itself from the frame rate it measures rather than from what hardware you have, and mobile automatically caps its render resolution.

Why it is for you. The iPad is for recreation and this is the kind of thing it is actually good for: open a link, fly somewhere, stop. Touch is properly thought about rather than bolted on — one finger steers, a pinch is the throttle, two fingers orbit a planet you have selected, and walking around on the surface uses a virtual joystick — so it is a real tablet game and not a desktop game that tolerates a tablet. There is a second reason, which is Three.js: you build with it, and a seamless space-to-surface transition with no loading screen is a genuinely hard thing to pull off in a browser. Worth half an hour as a player and ten minutes as someone who will wonder how the terrain streaming works.

To try it. Open dp.eray.top and fly. Nothing to install, no account, no login. On the Mac the mouse steers, W and S are thrust and brake, L auto-lands and M is the galaxy map; on foot it is WASD, 1–6 for weapons, F to interact. On the iPad use the touch controls above. Ten seconds to playing.

Risk. Mixed licensing, and this is the thing to understand before you fork it: the code is MIT, but the 42 music tracks are not — 27 were generated with Google DeepMind's Lyria and 15 with an early Suno, and the project states they fall outside the MIT licence and are for non-commercial use only, telling anyone who wants to redistribute or go commercial to replace the audio. The game runs with the audio folder deleted. For playing it, none of that matters; for borrowing from it, it matters a lot. The shape is small: 25 stars, 3 forks, no open issues, created 17 July and pushed yesterday. One person's project, played in their browser and hosted on their own domain — so the honest risk is that the link stops working one day, not that it harms you. The documentation is mainly in Simplified Chinese with an English README alongside. No accounts, analytics or telemetry are documented, and the only network traffic described is the music loading on demand from the project's own assets — but note that this is a personal site rather than a repository you inspect, so “no telemetry” here is the absence of a claim rather than a promise. It needs WebGL 2 and states no minimum hardware; if the iPad struggles the automatic quality tiers are supposed to handle it.
