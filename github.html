Try this weekend

A real application for the Mac, and two browser tabs. Harmony you can see, and a chip you can learn without owning it.

Orfeo

MIDI in, harmony out · a desktop app for the Mac (and the Windows PC)

What it is. A cross-platform app that takes a MIDI file apart and shows you what is in it. Notes fall onto a keyboard that lights up, coloured by track and by hand; tempo changes without changing pitch; there is a loop region, a metronome, a count-in and a mixer. Around that sit the parts that make it more than a player: a Chord Explorer, a Scale Explorer with a circle of fifths, a chord prompter, and real-time chord detection that names the harmony as the music goes past. It reads MIDI, MusicXML, Guitar Pro, Capella and karaoke files, has a note editor that saves edits as a new _ORFEO_vN.mid rather than touching your original, and exports to MP3 or WAV. Three sound engines, including SpessaSynth with soundfonts.

Why it is for you. You study scores for pleasure — Mozart's quartets in score, Brahms and Bach avoiding cadence, pandiatonicism — and the gap between reading a score and hearing what the harmony is doing is exactly what real-time chord detection closes. Drop in a MIDI of a movement you are working through and you get a running first-pass harmonic analysis against the sound, which is a different and faster thing from working it out at the piano. The MusicXML import matters for the same reason: your own material, not someone's exercise set. And the second use is the one you will probably get more hours out of — a slow-it-down, loop-a-bar, play-along tool for getting back to classical piano, with your own MIDI keyboard if you want it and the mouse if you do not. This is also one of the very few picks that is genuinely useful on the Windows PC, which is where your music work actually happens.

To try it. Download a build from the project's releases — a .dmg for Apple Silicon or Intel on the Mac, an installer, a portable build or the Microsoft Store on Windows. No curl | sh, no sudo, no account, no subscription. Twenty minutes: open it, drop in a MIDI file, turn on chord detection, and try the scale explorer on something you know well. Extra soundfonts are an optional download if the default General MIDI sound annoys you. Building from source instead is Node 20 LTS and npm install.

Risk. GPL-3.0-or-later, stated in the repository, with GSAP bundled under a separate additional-permission grant — worth knowing only if you ever lift code out of it. 788 stars, 61 forks, 3 open issues, created 13 June 2026 and pushed yesterday: five months old, active, and a healthy fork-to-star ratio, which is the number I trust here. Three open issues against 788 stars is unusually tidy — either very well kept or issues get closed quickly, and I cannot tell which from the outside. The honest limits are the ones it states itself: chord transcription is beta, so treat a detected chord as a first pass and not an authority — which is the one caveat that matters for the use I am recommending it for; the Native sound engine is beta and Windows-only; the Linux builds are experimental. No iPad and no browser version — this is a desktop application, and nothing in the project claims otherwise. On your data it is as clean as these get: the project says no account and no telemetry, MIDI is processed entirely on your device, and the internet is used only to fetch soundfonts, check for updates or open a link.

ESP32 Labs

A chip's whole bench guide as a web app, simulators included · browser, so the iPad counts

What it is. Thirty-two hands-on lessons on the ESP32-S3, rebuilt as a React app you read in a tab: a live pinout, a 3D model of the board, interactive simulators for the lessons, a simulated serial monitor that answers commands you type, a reader that walks the companion firmware's source, a bench log, and a readable companion to the chip's datasheet. In eleven languages. The important part for you is that the lessons and the simulators work with no board plugged in — the project says so explicitly; only the source-code reader needs the sibling firmware checkout to have anything in it.

Why it is for you. “I want to teach electronics” has been on your list for a long time and your electronics notes are still stubs, and the reason is the usual one: teaching electronics appears to need a bench, parts and a soldering iron before anyone learns anything. This is the version that does not. It is a browser tab, so it works on the iPad — which is where you actually sit with ideas — and the simulators mean you can read a lesson, poke the thing, and find out whether you want to teach from it, before buying a single component. It is also a good model to steal from if you ever build your own teaching material: lessons with a simulator beside them is precisely the shape your Nuggets games already take, applied to a subject you know and the children do not.

To try it. Open ionloft.com/esp-labs/. That is all — nothing installed, no account, and your progress and last-opened lesson live in the browser's own storage. One correction worth having: the README's hosted address (a Vercel one) returns a payment-required error as of this morning — I checked both — so use the project's own site above, which is live. Running it locally is Node 24.14+, pnpm 11 and two repositories cloned side by side, which you do not need in order to read the lessons. Half an hour to know whether it is a teaching resource for you.

Risk. MIT, stated in the repository; third-party libraries and the separate firmware repository carry their own licences. 1,012 stars, 153 forks, 3 open issues, created 21 October 2026 and pushed on 23 November. Two cautions on that: it is 36 days old, only just past this newsletter's 30-day line, and a thousand stars in five weeks is fast — though 153 forks is in proportion to it, which is the reassuring half. Unfinished in places, and it says so: two of the Wi-Fi lessons and the entire Sensors track are planned rather than written, and Espressif's reference text is included only when the reference file is present at build time, otherwise citations link out to the official PDFs. It is not affiliated with Espressif, and the English source text is used even in the translated interfaces. The hands-on half assumes specific hardware — an ESP32-S3 DevKitC-1, a 1.9″ TFT, buttons, and extra modules for the RFID and sound lessons — and flashing firmware needs a real board, so budget for a shopping list if you go past reading. On data: no account, progress stays in your browser, and the project says the Vercel analytics and speed-insights scripts load only in Vercel builds, not in local or plain ones. The project does not say whether the “Internet & AI” Wi-Fi lesson needs an API key, so assume it might.

Rawl

Western scores in twelve colours · browser, drop a MIDI file on it

What it is. A piano-roll viewer for harmony. You drop a MIDI file onto a web page and get it back as a piano roll where the twelve pitch classes are twelve colours keyed to scale degree, so a modulation is a change of palette and a borrowed chord is a wrong-coloured note. Alongside that sit curated corpora — classical, rock and pop, jazz, and a large body of NES game music — with the author's own written analyses, and an argument about teaching harmony by having students analyse pieces rather than learn rules first. The NES section is organised by harmonic “language”: common practice, blues, chiptune cadences, modes, pentatonic, hijaz.

Why it is for you. Half your music notes are about how harmony moves — cadence avoided in Brahms and Bach, pandiatonicism, what holds a form together — and this is the cheapest way to see that in a piece rather than read about it. Colour-by-scale-degree is a genuinely good idea for the specific question you keep asking of a score: where does the ground shift, and how was the shift prepared. Ten seconds to find out whether it suits how you think, because there is nothing to install. There is a teaching use too — a child can see that a modulation happened long before they can name it — and a caveat about the framing, below.

To try it. Go to rawl.rocks and drag a MIDI file in. No install, no account. Try something you know in score first, so you are judging the tool and not the piece. The built-in corpora are a quicker start than your own files if you want to see what the author thinks the colours are for.

Risk. The licence is the weak spot and you should know exactly how. GitHub reports GPL-3.0 for the repository, which satisfies this newsletter's licence rule; but the README itself only says the project uses “the same licence as” the upstream project it is forked from (Chip Player JS) without naming it. So the licence is inherited rather than restated — fine for using a hosted web page, worth a proper check before you reuse any of the code. 84 stars, 10 forks, 29 open issues, created in 2022 and pushed yesterday: a long-running one-person research project, not a product, and the open-issue count against the star count tells you so honestly. Two limits it states plainly: it cannot save your files, and it cannot save your annotations — so it is a lens, not a workspace, and anything you work out you write down yourself. A list of intended features (optical music recognition, colouring MusicXML noteheads, live MIDI) is aspiration rather than function. Last, a taste warning rather than a risk: the project is polemical about notation being gatekeeping, and you are someone who reads notation for pleasure — take the colours and leave the manifesto. No accounts, keys or telemetry are mentioned.

For your projects

The video pipeline, and the one thing in it that still costs money.

MLX-Audio

Speech models that run on your own Mac · for the video pipeline — to use

What it is. Text-to-speech, speech-to-text and speech-to-speech running locally on Apple Silicon through Apple's MLX framework, with a long menu of models to choose between and voice cloning in several of them. Three ways in: a command-line tool, a Python API, and — the one that matters here — a local server with OpenAI-compatible endpoints (/v1/audio/speech, /v1/audio/transcriptions). There is an optional web UI if you would rather click.

Why it is for you. Your pipeline records HTML scenes with Playwright and joins them with ffmpeg, and the piece that has never been solved locally is the voice: the good options want a paid account, which is the thing you say you dislike most about otherwise-good software. Use this for the teaching videos and the hymn channel. The specific reason to use it rather than admire it is the OpenAI-compatible endpoint: if any part of your pipeline already knows how to ask a hosted service for speech, it can ask localhost in the same shape, so this is a configuration change rather than a rewrite. The transcription half is worth as much as the speaking half, because word-level timings from your own narration are what make captions land on the syllable. And since it is MLX, it runs on the machine you actually have rather than needing the RAM-starved PC or a GPU.

To try it. pip install -U mlx-audio on the Mac (Python 3.11+), then one generate command with a small model and listen to it. No curl | sh, no account, no key. ffmpeg — via Homebrew — is needed only if you want MP3 or similar; plain WAV works without it. An evening, most of which is downloading models and deciding which voice you can bear to listen to for ten minutes.

Risk. The code is MIT, stated in the repository. The models are not, and the README does not say what they are licensed under — they come down from Hugging Face at first use, from several different originators. That is the one thing to check before a voice generated here narrates anything you publish; the tool being free is not the same as the voice being yours to use. Sizes range from a few hundred megabytes for the small speech models to around 17 GB of memory for the largest, which your Mac may or may not enjoy — start small. Apple Silicon and macOS only, so nothing here helps the Windows PC. 8,338 stars, 808 forks, 93 open issues, created November 2024, pushed yesterday: two years old with real activity and a fork count in proportion to its stars, which is the healthiest shape of anything in this issue. It is company-adjacent — the README banners a commercial lab and a product — but neither is required to run it, and nothing in it asks for an account. Two stated caveats: one model's voice cloning is not yet available upstream so it quietly uses a default voice instead, and some models are English-only. Nothing was installed or run to write this.

A game

Your day job, as a cartoon, in a browser tab. This one needs no explaining to you at all.

Series of Tubes

Run an ISP: subsea cable, IXPs, 95th-percentile billing and the occasional backhoe · browser

What it is. A free cartoon browser game about how the internet actually works. You start with one router in one city and try to grow into a transit-free Tier 1 network. You win customers (home broadband, enterprises, hosting, DDoS protection, AI data centres), decide how to interconnect — IP transit, internet exchanges, peering, CDN caches — and build the physical layer: leased waves across some forty subsea cable systems, dark fibre, router generations, link aggregation. Then you do traffic engineering and survive what the world does to you: backhoe cuts, route leaks, DDoS, sharks. There is an optional multiplayer mode where players peer with and sell transit to each other.

Why it is for you. This is optical networking as a toy, which is not a thing that usually exists. You spend your working life on the layer this game is a cartoon of, and the pleasure on offer is specific: finding out which parts it gets right, and laughing at the parts it does not. 95th-percentile billing as a game mechanic is a genuinely funny idea and a sign the author knows the business. There is a second use if you want one — it is the clearest answer you will find to “what does Daddy do”, and the kind of thing your teaching work is made of — but mainly it is half an hour of recreation aimed almost personally at you.

To try it. Go to seriesoftub.es. Nothing installed, no account, no cookies; your progress sits in the browser's local storage. Eight languages. Multiplayer is opt-in and the game works fine without it, so start solo.

Risk. MIT, stated in the repository. 263 stars, 58 forks, 58 open issues, created 23 October 2026 and pushed this morning: that is 34 days old, barely past this newsletter's 30-day line, and one author working fast — 58 open issues against 263 stars is what a month-old game looks like, not a warning in itself, but do not expect stability. Do not use it as a reference: the project says outright that it simplifies and gets some things wrong for the sake of the joke, and points at a book for the accurate version — which, given your field, is the one thing worth knowing before you get irritated. The iPad is untested: nothing in the project addresses touch or tablets at all, so it may well want a mouse. On data: no accounts and no cookies in solo play; the optional multiplayer sends game data to relay servers, and the project says those relays never keep IP addresses and cannot see your strategy or money, with deals sealed end to end — which is a good answer, and still a reason to stay solo if you would rather not find out.
