Four works kept out of circulation by circumstance rather than by quality — 1834, 1919, 1933, 1948.

String Quartet in E flat major, H. 277 (1834)

Fanny Hensel (1805–1847)

You said Mendelssohn is growing on you. This is the argument his sister was having with him, in writing, in the same year. It began as an abandoned piano sonata from five years earlier; over the summer of 1834 she rescored the first two movements for quartet and wrote two new ones — which is why the piece behaves so oddly at the start. It opens with a slow Adagio ma non troppo as the first movement, not an introduction to one, and it never gives you the confident sonata-allegro the genre is asking for. Felix read it and told her, more or less, that it lacked form. She wrote back that she knew, and that the fault was a want of the ability to sustain ideas properly — then went on writing exactly like this anyway.

The thing to listen for, as a composer rather than as a sympathiser: her transitions. She modulates by simply arriving, without the preparatory bar or two that Felix would always supply, and the effect is not clumsiness but a kind of impatience — she has heard the new key already and assumes you have too. The Romanze third movement is the one people excerpt, and it is the most beautiful, but the finale is where the argument about form gets settled in her favour.

Sonata for Viola and Piano (1919)

Rebecca Clarke (1886–1979)

Written for a competition funded by Elizabeth Sprague Coolidge, judged blind, and it tied for first place out of seventy-two entries with a suite by Ernest Bloch. The patron broke the tie in Bloch's favour. When the names were revealed and the judges found that the other finalist was a thirty-three-year-old English violist, a rumour went round that "Rebecca Clarke" was a pseudonym for Bloch, or that she had not written it herself. She spent the rest of a long life largely not composing.

None of which would matter if the piece were not superb. It is the closest thing in the repertoire to what you like about Brahms — that seamlessness, the difficulty of locating yourself — arrived at by a completely different route. Brahms gets it by developing variation, one cell grown continuously. Clarke gets it by pentatonic and modal material that refuses to cadence, so the harmony keeps sliding on rather than closing, and you lose your place because there is no place to hold. The opening Impetuoso announces the whole thing on the viola alone. The middle movement is a scherzo that behaves like a nocturne with the lights off. And the finale, unusually, brings the first movement's material back and does cadence, which after twenty minutes of deferral lands like a door closing.

Concerto for String Orchestra (1948)

Grażyna Bacewicz (1909–1969)

If you love Bartók, this is the piece to put on next, and it is short enough that there is no excuse. Bacewicz was a first-rate violinist as well as a composer — she led the Polish Radio orchestra — and the string writing has the specificity that only comes from someone who knows exactly what is easy and what is merely difficult-sounding. Three movements, neo-Baroque bones: a driving Allegro with a genuine Baroque motor rhythm, a slow movement, and a finale that is essentially a moto perpetuo. It is commonly called her masterpiece.

Where it differs from the Bartók you know is instructive. The Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta is built symmetrically, arch upon arch, and you can feel the architecture from inside. Bacewicz is writing in Poland in 1948, when the state was about to start telling composers what music should sound like, and the Baroque frame is doing double duty — it is a real formal choice and it is also cover. The folk inflection is there but it never becomes ethnography. What you get is Bartók's rhythmic bite with Handel's proportions, which is an odd hybrid and it works.

Symphony No. 4 in C major (1933)

Franz Schmidt (1874–1939)

The big one, and the one most likely to actually land, because it sits exactly where you live: the continuing Romantic line, tonal, Viennese, written by a man who played cello under Mahler in the Vienna Court Opera orchestra for years and absorbed him at close range. Schmidt wrote it in 1933 after his daughter Emma died following childbirth. He called it a requiem for her, and the form is the point: one continuous forty-five-minute movement in which the four traditional movements are nested, opening with an unaccompanied trumpet solo and closing with the same solo, so the whole symphony is enclosed by a single line.

That structural device is the reason to spend the time. It is not a cyclic theme in the Franck sense, brought back for recognition. It is a frame that makes the intervening forty minutes read as one long parenthesis, and it solves a problem you must have run into — how to justify a very long single span without either sectionalising it or exhausting the listener. The slow movement, a cello threnody at the centre, is the emotional core and it does not need the biography to work. His reputation was badly damaged after his death by an unfinished commission he accepted for the Nazi regime in his last months, and the piece went into the shade with it; the music predates all of that by six years.

A composer to explore

One, with a route in — which piece first, then what.

Why now — Jan Dismas Zelenka (1679–1745) You are fond of Bach's instrumental output and you find him hard when he goes too cerebral; you are also curious about reviving very old music by lesser-known composers. Zelenka is the exact intersection. A Bohemian working in Dresden, one of the strangest harmonic minds of the eighteenth century, and — this is the part that should interest you — a composer Bach knew personally and admired. Bach's son Wilhelm Friedemann studied in Dresden; Bach requested copies of Zelenka's music. He was passed over for the Kapellmeister post he had effectively been doing the work of, his manuscripts were locked away in the Dresden court library after his death and not allowed to circulate, and he was essentially unavailable for two hundred years. He is the best answer going to your question about how the eras ran into each other, because he sounds like a Baroque composer who has already heard something later and cannot un-hear it.

Route in: the six Trio Sonatas, ZWV 181 (c. 1720–22)

Start here

Two oboes, bassoon and continuo — or violin substituting for an oboe in two of them — and they are notorious among wind players as some of the hardest music of the century to actually get through. Start with No. 3 in B flat or No. 6 in C minor. What you will hear immediately is that the bassoon is not a continuo instrument here; it is a third contrapuntal voice with its own material, which almost nobody was doing. And the harmony goes places that have no business being there in 1721: chromatic side-steps mid-phrase, suspensions that resolve onto another suspension, cadences deferred past the point of comfort. It is contrapuntally as dense as late Bach but it is never dry, because the material is fundamentally dance music and it keeps moving. Then: Hipocondrie à 7 Concertanti, ZWV 187 (1723) — seven minutes, a French overture whose title means roughly what you think it means, and the harmonic swerves are the joke. After that: the Lamentationes Jeremiae Prophetae, ZWV 53, where the strangeness turns serious.

Something to study

For the composer's ear, not the listener's.

This week — Schubert's third key, and why it is not a detour You have said Schubert speaks to you more than Beethoven does. There is a technical reason for that, and it is not temperament — it is that he systematically breaks the one structural rule the sonata was built on, and he does it often enough that it is a method rather than a liberty.

Schubert, String Quintet in C major, D. 956 — first movement exposition

The exercise

The classical exposition is a two-key argument: tonic, then dominant (or relative major), and the whole drama of the recapitulation is the resolution of that single opposition. Schubert routinely uses three. In the D. 956 first movement the exposition runs roughly 154 bars and moves C major → E flat major → G major. The famous second subject, the cello duet, arrives in E flat — a flat mediant, not the dominant — and only afterwards does the music slide to G to close the exposition properly.

Here is the part worth a pencil. The lazy reading is that E flat is a beautiful digression and G is the "real" second key arriving late. Test that and it collapses. Time the sections: the E flat music is where the lyrical weight of the movement sits, and the G major passage is transitional in character — it is doing the job of a closing group, not of a second subject. So the structural second key is E flat, and the dominant has been demoted to a formality on the way out. Then go to the recapitulation and ask the question that makes this useful: what does he do with three keys when he only has one tonic to resolve them into? Schubert's answer is not to transpose everything to C. Track where each of the three lands the second time and you will find the movement's actual argument.

Two follow-ons once you have done it. First, the same procedure, more compressed, in the first movement of the Great C major symphony. Second, the composer's question underneath all of it: a three-key exposition weakens the tonic–dominant polarity that holds a long movement together, so Schubert needs something else to carry the span. Watch how much more work the rhythmic surface and the sheer length of phrase are doing instead. That is the trade he made, and it is the direct ancestor of what Bruckner and Mahler do with very long forms.

An evening with one score · free on IMSLP; Aaron Grant's dissertation on Schubert's three-key expositions is the thorough treatment if you want to go further

The Proms are in their final fortnight. Two nights worth clearing, out of the whole week.

When What
Tue 1 Sep
7.30pm
Royal Albert Hall		 BBC Symphony Orchestra / Jakub Hrůša, with Lukáš Vondráček (piano). Vítězslava Kaprálová, Suita rustica, plus Rachmaninov and Janáček. This is the one. Kaprálová died at twenty-five in 1940 and wrote this at about twenty-two — Czech folk material handled with a rhythmic hardness that is much closer to Bartók than to Dvořák, and it is almost never programmed.
