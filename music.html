Listen this week

Three. The first is twelve minutes and you have almost certainly never played it.

Gesänge der Frühe, Op. 133 (1853)

Robert Schumann (1810–1856)

Your Prince of Music wrote these five short pieces in October 1853. They are the last thing he completed for solo piano, and four months later he walked into the Rhine. That sequence is why they spent a century being filed as evidence rather than heard as music — the standard nineteenth-century verdict was that they were the work of a mind already going, and they were left out of the repertoire accordingly. They are not that. They are the strangest and most forward-facing thing in his piano output, and if you are obsessed with this family you have a gap here that is twelve minutes wide.

What to listen for, as a composer rather than a mourner: the first piece opens with a chorale that keeps refusing to cadence where the voice-leading says it should, and the whole set works by holding a texture steady while the harmony slides underneath it. The second is rhythmically unplaceable — you will lose the downbeat, deliberately. This is Schumann solving the same problem Brahms would spend his life on, which is how to keep a piece continuous without letting the listener find the joins, and solving it by a completely different route: not seamless construction but a refusal to confirm. The score is free, it is five pages of ideas, and you could read it at the keyboard this afternoon.

Das klagende Lied — the 1880 version, in three parts

Gustav Mahler (1860–1911)

Mahler finished this in 1880, aged twenty, before the First Symphony and before anything else he let stand. It is a cantata on two grim German folk tales — a man murders his brother, a minstrel carves a flute from one of the bones, the flute sings the crime at the wedding feast and the hall comes down. What makes it worth your evening is not the story but the fact that the voice is already complete. The offstage band, the marches that curdle, the bird-calls, the way a major-key wedding is made to sound like a threat: none of that was learned later. It is all here before he had an orchestra of his own to try it on.

The version matters, and this is the part most Mahler listeners have never sorted out. He later cut the entire first part, Waldmärchen, and the piece was premiered in 1901 without it; that two-part shape is what was published and what most recordings give you. The complete 1880 score was not conducted until Kent Nagano did it in Manchester in October 1997. So if you know the work at all, you probably know the version its composer edited in middle age — and the interesting question for you is what the older Mahler thought the younger one had got wrong. Hear Waldmärchen first, on its own, then ask whether cutting it was judgement or embarrassment. Monday's Radio 3 concert is the original 1880 version, so the full comparison is free this week.

String Quartet No. 3, H. 183 (1929)

Bohuslav Martinů (1890–1959)

You like the Czech line and you love Bartók, and Martinů is the obvious door between them that nobody opens for you. This one was written in Paris in 1929 and dedicated to the Roth Quartet, who gave the premiere. It is the most experimental of his quartets and it is short — three movements, barely a quarter of an hour — which makes it an unusually cheap way to find out whether you want the other six.

The thing to notice is how differently he solves the problem Bartók was solving at the same moment. Bartók built his rhythmic language out of transcribed village recordings; Martinů was in Paris, writing motor rhythms that come from jazz and from Stravinsky, and the folk material in him surfaces as melodic shape rather than as metre. Put this against a Bartók quartet of the late twenties and you have two composers from neighbouring countries, the same age within a decade, going at the same inheritance from opposite ends. The Pavel Haas Quartet's recording is the one to use, and they are also on Radio 3 three times next week.

A composer to explore

One, with a route in — which piece first, then what.

Why now — Mieczysław Weinberg (1919–1996) This section has gone to Zelenka, Suk, Holmboe and Stenhammar. Weinberg is the one that answers your own complaint most directly: you say there are many twentieth-century composers you have not explored, and here is a tonal composer with twenty-two symphonies and seventeen string quartets who was, for forty years, Shostakovich's closest friend and musical confidant. Born in Warsaw, he walked out of Poland in 1939 when the Germans came, lost his family at Trawniki, and spent the rest of his life in the Soviet Union writing an enormous, largely unplayed body of work. Shostakovich intervened personally to get him out of prison in 1953. If you want the single largest unexplored catalogue in the idiom you already like, this is it — and unlike most such recommendations, the recordings now exist.

Route in: Piano Quintet in F minor, Op. 18 (1944)

Start here

Start with the Quintet because it is the piece where the relationship is legible. It is in five movements, like Shostakovich's Piano Quintet of 1940, and it is openly written in that shadow — but it is wilder, longer-limbed and much less careful about good manners than its model. Written in 1944, when he was twenty-five. If you have ever wondered what the Shostakovich chamber idiom sounds like without the self-censorship that came from being the most watched composer in the country, this is the answer, and it will tell you inside two movements whether the rest is for you.

Then: the String Quartet No. 6, Op. 35 — banned before it could be played and left unperformed for over fifty years, and the place where the quartets stop being apprentice work. After that: the Symphony No. 21, Kaddish, his late memorial for the Warsaw ghetto, which is where the whole life arrives somewhere. Save that one for a night you are not doing anything else.

Quintet about 45 minutes · Shostakovich's Op. 57 Quintet alongside it makes the comparison in one sitting

Something to study

For the composer's ear, not the listener's. Two free scores and a broadcast to hang them on.

This week — the borrowing Schubert did not hide On Wednesday, Steven Osborne plays Schubert's C minor Sonata, D958, on Radio 3. Its first movement opens with a theme taken almost note for note from Beethoven's 32 Variations in C minor, WoO 80 — same key, same descending bass, same defiant character. Schubert wrote it in 1828, the last year of his life, the year after Beethoven's funeral, at which he had been a torchbearer. This is not a scholarly conjecture you have to take on trust: put the two openings side by side and you can see it in one glance.

Schubert D958 against Beethoven's WoO 80 — what he did with what he took

The exercise

The interesting question is not whether he borrowed. It is what a borrowing is for. Beethoven's theme is a closed eight-bar unit built to be varied — it exists to be a fixed object that thirty-two variations can stand on, so it is deliberately foursquare and harmonically self-contained. Schubert takes that same material and needs it to do the opposite job: launch a sonata-form movement, which means it has to be unstable enough to go somewhere. Open both scores and mark, bar by bar, every change he makes to it. They are small and there are not many, and every one of them is aimed at the same thing.

Then the harder half. Beethoven's set proves the theme by exhausting it — thirty-two ways of being that harmony. Schubert's movement proves it by leaving it, and his second subject goes somewhere no variation set could follow. So you have one piece of material treated by two composers with opposite theories of what material is for: Beethoven's is a foundation, Schubert's is a departure point. For someone who owns the Mozart quartets in score and is chasing how one era ran into the next, this is a clean and unusually well-documented specimen of exactly that handover — the older man's method inherited and then pointedly declined by the younger.

Both scores are free. Read them on Wednesday afternoon, then have Osborne play you the sonata at 7.30 the same evening.

An afternoon with two scores · D958 on Radio 3, Wednesday 7.30pm · both scores free on IMSLP
