Listen this week

Four, in chronological order, and together they are a cross-section of the same fault line: 1773, 1821, 1849, 1880.

Symphony for strings in B minor, Wq 182/5 (H 661)

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach (1714–1788)

One of six string symphonies written in 1773 for Baron Gottfried van Swieten — the same Viennese patron who, a decade later, pushed Mozart towards his father’s counterpoint. Three movements, eleven minutes, and the opening Allegretto behaves like no Baroque first movement and no Classical one either: it stops mid-phrase, changes its mind about the key, and restarts somewhere you did not expect.

Why it should suit you: you wrote that Bach becomes hard going when he turns cerebral. This is his son doing the exact opposite — all nerve and gesture, no fugal piety — and it is the single clearest piece of evidence for how the Baroque actually ended. Not a gradual softening into Haydn, but a deliberate lurch. Eleven minutes is nothing, and the score is free, so you can see on paper how little material he needs to destabilise a movement.

Three movements (Allegretto · Larghetto · Presto) · about 11 minutes · 1773 · Arte dei Suonatori

Symphony in D major, Op. 24

Jan Václav Hugo Voříšek (1791–1825)

Written in 1820–21 in Vienna by a Bohemian who was Beethoven’s acquaintance, Schubert’s contemporary, and dead at 34. Four movements, and the third is marked Scherzo rather than Minuet — in 1821 that is still a statement. Be warned that the catalogue is a mess: the same symphony is published as Op. 23 and as Op. 24 depending on whose edition you pick up.

Why it should suit you: you have now rated Kraus, Burgmüller and Taneyev highly, which says you trust this seam — the first-rate symphony by the man history filed second. Voříšek is the Vienna version of it, and the interest for a composer is that you can hear him deciding, bar by bar, whether to be Beethoven or Schubert: Beethoven’s rhythmic insistence in the outer movements, Schubert’s way with a tune in the Andante, and no settled answer. The join between two eras, audible inside one piece.

Four movements · about 28 minutes · 1820–21 · Prague Philharmonia, Jiří Bělohlávek

Nonet in E flat major, Op. 38

Louise Farrenc (1804–1875)

1849, for string quartet plus wind quintet — nine players, so every line is exposed and there is nowhere to hide a doubling. Farrenc was professor of piano at the Paris Conservatoire for thirty years and the only woman to hold a chair there in that century; she wrote three symphonies and a pile of chamber music and then vanished from the repertoire almost entirely.

Why it should suit you: this is the Paris of exactly the years you are obsessed with — the Nonet is contemporary with Schumann’s late chamber music, and it is what the Schumanns’ generation sounded like when it was French and not German. For a composer the attraction is the scoring problem: nine instruments is the awkward size, too big for chamber balance and too small to fake an orchestra, and she solves it by treating the winds as soloists and the strings as the frame. Listen to the Scherzo for how little she needs to make nine players sound like more.

Four movements · about 32 minutes · 1849 · for string quartet and wind quintet

Symphony in E major

Hans Rott (1858–1884)

Rott was Mahler’s fellow student in Vienna. He entered the first movement of this symphony for a composition prize in 1878 and the jury — Bruckner excepted — was openly derisive; he finished the whole thing in 1880, failed to get Brahms or Hans Richter to perform it, was committed to a mental hospital in 1881 and died of tuberculosis in 1884, aged 25. The symphony was not performed until 1989, after the scholar Paul Banks — prompted by Mahler’s praise of it — went looking and found the score in the Austrian National Library.

Why it should suit you: Mahler called Rott the founder of the new symphony as he understood it, and this is the only substantial piece of Rott there is, so it is the nearest thing available to hearing where Mahler came from rather than where he went. You will recognise the scherzo immediately and it will be uncomfortable — the gestures you think of as Mahler’s are here first, written by someone who never heard a note of Mahler. It is an hour, which is a real ask; if you only have twenty minutes, the scherzo is the movement that does the damage. The Berlin Philharmonic recording under Paavo Järvi is the one to use.

Four movements · about 60 minutes · 1878–80, premiered 1989 · Berliner Philharmoniker, Paavo Järvi

A composer to explore

Seventy string quartets and quintets written in the gap between Beethoven’s late quartets and Schumann’s, by a man almost nobody programmes — and you are the reader who owns quartet scores and reads them.

George Onslow (1784–1853)

Born in Clermont-Ferrand to an English father and a French mother, which is why he is in neither country’s canon. He wrote 36 string quartets and 34 string quintets — a chamber output on the scale of Haydn’s — was known across Europe in his lifetime as “the French Beethoven”, and is now represented in concert by roughly one piece.

Why you in particular. Your two live questions are how the eras collided and how Mozart was so far ahead; Onslow is the control experiment for the first. He was writing quartets continuously from 1806 to 1846, straight through the period when the language changed, with no agenda about it and no interest in being a pioneer — so his output is a continuous record of what a very competent professional thought the form was for, decade by decade. Put him next to the Mozart quartets you are already studying and you get a baseline: this is the level the craft had reached, which is what makes the distance to Mozart measurable rather than mystical.

The route in. Start with the String Quintet No. 15 in C minor, Op. 38, known as ‘The Bullet’ — in 1829 Onslow was accidentally shot in the face at a boar hunt and left partly deaf in one ear, and he wrote the quintet about it, marking the later movements for the fever, the convalescence and the recovery. Reviewers have compared it to Beethoven’s Op. 132, which does the same thing with its Heiliger Dankgesang — written four years earlier, and worth the comparison precisely because Onslow almost certainly had it in mind. Then a quartet, to hear him without a programme: No. 7 in G minor, Op. 9 No. 1, from 1812. After that, Editions Silvertrust has sensible notes on which of the seventy are actually worth your time.

New music

One 21st-century tonal composer a week. This one is the answer to a question you did not ask: what the Estonian choral sound does once you take Pärt’s system away from it.

Tõnu Kõrvits (b. 1969)

Born in Tallinn, trained at the Estonian Academy of Music and Theatre, where he has taught since 2001 and been professor of composition since 2017. He writes mostly for choir and for strings, in a language that is plainly tonal and modal, and the thing everyone says about him is that he treats the orchestra as a melodic instrument — the harmony moves slowly and the interest is almost always in a line.

Why you in particular. You like Pärt, and the easy assumption is that Estonian means tintinnabuli and stillness. Kõrvits is from the same tradition and works nothing like that: no system, no process, no grid — just long melodies over harmony that shifts a step at a time, scored with real care about register. He is also the rare living composer whose craft is legible on first hearing, which makes him useful to you rather than merely pleasant: you can hear the decisions. And he is a melodist, which is what you said you prefer about Handel over Bach, four hundred years earlier in the same argument.

The route in. Hymns to the Nordic Lights first — five short movements, around twelve minutes in total, Estonian National Symphony Orchestra; the quickest test of whether the ear suits you. Then Elegies of Thule, from the same album, whose movement titles come from Emily Brontë. Then Moorland Elegies (2015), the full Brontë cycle for choir and strings and the piece he is actually known for, here with the Estonian Philharmonic Chamber Choir and Tallinn Chamber Orchestra under Risto Joost — about an hour, and the one that will tell you whether he is a composer you want to follow.

Something to study

You own the complete Mozart quartets in score and you want to know how he was so far ahead of everyone. There is a way to answer that which does not depend on your judgement or mine: look at which one Beethoven picked.

Mozart, String Quartet No. 18 in A major, K. 464 — against Beethoven, Op. 18 No. 5

The anecdote first, because it is the whole method. Carl Czerny recorded that Beethoven, going through the score of Mozart’s six quartets dedicated to Haydn, turned to K. 464 first. That is a slightly odd choice — posterity has always found K. 465, the ‘Dissonance’, the more startling piece — and it is the choice of the one person in history whose opinion on this settles the matter.

The thing to actually do this week. Open K. 464 in your own score and put Beethoven’s Quartet in A major, Op. 18 No. 5 next to it. Then notice that Beethoven has taken the same key, the same unusual movement order — Menuetto second, not third — and the same decision to make the slow movement a set of variations rather than a song. That is not homage at a distance; that is a young composer reproducing another man’s structural plan almost wholesale, which he did for nobody else.

Then the part that answers your question. Work through K. 464’s Andante — the variation movement — and watch what the texture does: Mozart keeps the theme recognisable while handing the real argument to whichever instrument you are not listening to, so the four parts swap function without the surface changing. Beethoven got the plan and the key; what he did not get, and the reason the Mozart still sounds further ahead, is that ability to change what a line is for without changing what it sounds like. Stephen Rumph’s article ‘What Beethoven Learned from K464’ is the scholarly version of this argument and is free to read.

One bonus for a score reader. IMSLP now carries the British Library’s scan of Mozart’s holograph of K. 464 — 24 pages in his own hand, alongside the 1785 Artaria parts and the Neue Mozart-Ausgabe. Comparing the manuscript with the printed edition is an hour very well spent on a quartet you already know.
