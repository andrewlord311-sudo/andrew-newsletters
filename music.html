Listen this week

Three, and deliberately weighted differently from last week: the Haydn and the Vasks landed, the sixteenth-century organ loft only half did, so this runs Russian nationalism, Bartók before he was Bartók, and a piece of English string counterpoint you can hear on the radio on Monday night.

Tamara, symphonic poem after Lermontov

Mily Balakirev (1837–1910)

Balakirev is the one of the Five everybody names and nobody plays. He was their leader, their teacher and their bully — Donald Macleod spends Monday and Wednesday of next week on exactly that, including his two abrasive years running the St Petersburg Conservatoire in open opposition to the Russian Music Society. Tamara is the piece that justifies the reputation, and it took him roughly fifteen years to finish.

Why it should suit you: this is the Russian orchestral sound you already like in Tchaikovsky and the Russian school, but caught at the point where it is still being invented rather than inherited. The material is modal and repetitive by design — an orientalist set-piece built from short cells that get recoloured rather than developed — so the interest sits almost entirely in the scoring. For a composer that is the useful way to hear it: watch what he does to a four-bar idea with nothing but instrumentation, because that is the whole technique on display. The full score is free on IMSLP, which makes it an easy one to follow with your eyes.

About 21 minutes · completed 1882 · score free on IMSLP · good companion to Composer of the Week, Mon–Fri 4pm

Piano Quintet in C major, Sz. 23

Béla Bartók (1903–04)

You have written down two separate things that this piece answers at once: that you love Bartók, and that you are curious how the Classical, Romantic and later eras ran headlong into each other. Here is a composer you know inside out, aged twenty-two, writing music that sounds almost nothing like himself. By Bartók's own autobiographical account he was working under the strong influence of Brahms and of Dohnányi's youthful works, and had only just thrown himself at the Strauss scores — and all three are audible. Reviewers reach for “the rich palette of Richard Strauss and mature Brahms”, which is a fair description and a very strange sentence to write about Bartók.

The reason to spend forty minutes on an apprentice work is that you can hear the seams you cannot hear in Brahms. Everything that later becomes Bartók — the metrical bite, the folk inflection, the symmetry games — is either absent or trying to get out from under a late-Romantic overcoat. If Brahms terrifies you because the joins are invisible, this is the same idiom with the joins showing, written by someone who had not yet learned to hide them. He revised it in 1920–21, which is its own tell.

Four movements, about 40 minutes — Andante/Allegro molto, Vivace (Scherzando) from 12:26, Adagio from 21:36, then Poco a poco più vivace · premiered Vienna, November 1904, with the composer at the piano · the Kelemen/Frang/Kokas/Power/Altstaedt/Lonquich recording took both the Gramophone and BBC Music Magazine chamber awards for 2020

Concerto for Double String Orchestra

Michael Tippett (completed June 1939)

A direct follow-on from last week, and the reason to take the idea further than the organ loft. Lugge's double voluntary set two registrations against each other on one instrument; Tippett splits a string orchestra into two matched, complete string groups and writes for the gap between them. Same compositional problem — keep two bodies of sound independently audible — at a scale where you can hear every decision.

It is also squarely in the 20th-century line you say you have not finished exploring: entirely tonal, rhythmically springy, English without being pastoral, and built on the kind of sprung counterpoint Tippett took from Tudor music rather than from Germany. The slow movement is the test — nine minutes of Adagio cantabile where the two orchestras have to share one long line without either disappearing. Hear it on record first, because the Bournemouth Symphony play it on Radio 3 on Monday night and it is much more interesting the second time.

Three movements, about 23 minutes — Allegro con brio, Adagio cantabile from about 6:20, Allegro molto from about 15:55 · first performed at Morley College, London, April 1940 · on Radio 3, Monday 2 November, 7.30pm

A composer to explore

Someone whose entire output sits in the Romantic line you follow, who died at thirty-two, and who is essentially unperformed in Britain.

Mieczysław Karłowicz (1876–1909)

A Polish late-Romantic who wrote six symphonic poems, a symphony and a violin concerto, and then was killed by an avalanche in the Tatra Mountains at thirty-two while skiing alone. Reviewers place him squarely between Tchaikovsky and Rachmaninov; MusicWeb's verdict is “a contemplative Polish Tchaikovsky”, which is both accurate and the reason he should be on your list — you have said you enjoy Tchaikovsky, Dvořák and the Russian school, and this is that sound shifted a few hundred miles west and given a mountain to look at.

The thing that makes him worth a composer's attention rather than just a listener's is how programmatic he is without being illustrative. His solo walking in the Tatras is the acknowledged source of the music, and PWM calls Eternal Songs “the fullest manifestation of the composer's pantheistic credo” — but the scores work as long-breathed orchestral paragraphs, not as scene-painting. The orchestration is thick and slow-moving and he gets away with it; working out exactly how is the interesting part.

The route in. Start with the Violin Concerto in A, Op. 8 (1902) — it is the most immediately tuneful thing he wrote and the easiest way to decide whether you like him at all. If you do, go to Eternal Songs, Op. 10, written October 1904 to February 1906: three linked movements, Song of Everlasting Yearning (about 11 minutes), Song of Love and Death (about 12), Song of Eternal Being (about 5½), premiered by the Berlin Philharmonic under Grzegorz Fitelberg on 21 March 1907. After that the Lithuanian Rhapsody, Op. 11. Tortelier and the BBC Philharmonic recorded the symphonic poems for Chandos; Tasmin Little and Edward Gardner did the concerto.

New music

One living tonal composer a week. This one writes piano pieces that are explicitly homages to Haydn, Bartók, Beethoven and Mendelssohn — and she was born in Essex.

Cheryl Frances-Hoad (born 1980)

Born in Essex, started composing at eight, won the BBC Young Composer competition at fifteen, trained at the Yehudi Menuhin School, Gonville & Caius in Cambridge and King's College London. She has three British Composer / Ivor Novello Awards — for Psalm 1 and Stolen Rhythm in 2010, when she became the first young composer to win two in one year, and for Scenes from the Wild in 2022. She is well regarded and still under-broadcast, which is exactly the gap this section is for.

Why you in particular. Her music is described as having a “tongue-in-cheek, surprisingly tonal playfulness”, and is repeatedly inspired by “the music of bygone masters”. Her set Homages is a sequence of lyric piano pieces paying homage to Bartók, Beethoven, Mendelssohn, Haydn and Janáček — which is to say she has spent years doing in public precisely what you are doing privately with the Mozart quartets: taking apart composers you admire and writing back at them. The title piece, Stolen Rhythm, was written for the Haydn bicentenary and premiered by Matthew Schellhorn at Robinson College, Cambridge in 2012; another of the set, In the Dew, a Homage to Janáček, is on the current ABRSM Grade 8 piano syllabus.

The route in. Start with Homages: VII. Bar(tik)tok — a couple of minutes, and the joke in the title is also the compositional method, so you will know within thirty seconds whether she is for you. Then Homages: I. Contemplation for the other end of her range. If both land, go to Quark Dances (2013–14), a five-movement orchestral piece written after a visit to CERN and doubling as a ballet finale, played by the Rambert Orchestra under Paul Hoskins. All of it is on Stolen Rhythm (Champs Hill CHRCD119, 2017), with Ivana Gavrić at the piano.

Something to study

A free score, a single movement, and one question to take to it — chosen to sit directly against the Mozart quartets you are already working through.

Borodin, String Quartet No. 2 — the Notturno

Borodin is the member of the Five whom Donald Macleod picks up on Tuesday, and the framing there is the useful one: a working research chemist, torn between science and composition, who was also an amateur cellist. The Second Quartet of 1881 is what that biography sounds like. The Notturno is its third movement — A major, Andante, 180 bars — and the famous thing about it is the part-writing decision at the very start: the theme is given first to the cello, the composer's own instrument, and only then passes up to the first violin.

The question to take to the page. Putting your best tune in the cello is the awkward choice, because the melody is now underneath the instruments that have to accompany it. Mozart, whose quartets you have in score, almost never has this problem — he keeps the tune on top and works the interest into the inner parts. Borodin takes the opposite risk, so the thing worth tracing bar by bar is how he keeps the upper three out of the cello's way: where he thins the texture, how high he lets the viola sit, what register he leaves empty. Then watch the handover to the violin, and ask what changes in the accompaniment the moment the melody is no longer the bottom voice.

It is a good week for it: an amateur's instinct about his own instrument, set against Mozart's professional caution, in eight minutes of music you already half know. The IMSLP page has the complete score as a 48-page scan, plus the 1894 Belaieff edition and a later Eulenburg study print.
