Week of 26 September 2026

The thread running through this week is small containers holding large things: a symphony that folds four movements into one unbroken span, a 2011 quartet piece grown out of about eight bars of Haydn, and fourteen piano miniatures from 1908 in which Bartók worked out most of what he would spend the next forty years doing. Radio 3 also gives five afternoons to Cyril Scott, who was called the English Debussy by people who meant it, and the study item this week is a single page of score with a joke on it.

Listen this week

Three, none of them anybody you have had from me before, and all three are short. Start with the Shaw — it is the one you can check against a score you already own.

Entr'acte (2011)

Caroline Shaw (b. 1982)

You own the complete string quartets in score and you are working through them, which makes this the most directly useful ten minutes in the issue. Shaw wrote Entr'acte after hearing the Brentano Quartet play Haydn's Op. 77 No. 2, and specifically after the moment in the minuet where the trio drops into D flat. That is the whole seed: not a theme, not a motif, a modulation. Go and read that minuet first — it is in the book on your shelf — then play this.

Her own account of what she was chasing is worth having verbatim, because it is a composer's description rather than a critic's: she loves “the way some music (like the minuets of Op. 77) suddenly takes you to the other side of Alice's looking glass, in a kind of absurd, subtle, technicolor transition.” The piece is built as a minuet and trio and then pushes the form past where Haydn would stop. What to listen for as a composer: she treats that one harmonic sidestep as a structural event and spends the piece re-approaching it from different distances, so the form is generated by a single transition rather than by themes. It is also the answer to your complaint that the 21st century is flying off in all directions and you never get time — here is a direction that runs straight back into the repertoire you are already studying.

Symphony No. 1 in one movement, Op. 9 (1935–36)

Samuel Barber (1910–1981)

This is the one with the fact attached. Barber wrote out an analysis of Sibelius's Seventh Symphony on the same pages as the sketches for this piece — so the debt is not a critic's inference, it is physically there in the manuscript. He was twenty-five, he had been given the American Prix de Rome, and he wrote it between August 1935 and its completion at Roquebrune. It is squarely in the continuing Romantic line you say you like and it is almost never programmed here.

The reason it is worth twenty minutes to you rather than to a general listener: it is the cleanest demonstration you will find of the one-movement symphony as a real formal solution rather than a gesture. Four connected sections do the work of four movements — an Allegro non troppo, a faster triple-time stretch standing in for the scherzo, an Andante tranquillo, and then a passacaglia finale — and all of it runs off the three themes stated at the start, with the first of them cut down in diminution to make the scherzo and then laid out as the passacaglia bass at the end. So the same idea is by turns melody, figuration and ground. That is the technical thing to take away: what makes a single span hold together over twenty minutes is not transition-writing, it is having material that survives changing function.

Sea Sketches for string orchestra (1944)

Grace Williams (1906–1977)

Vaughan Williams's pupil at the Royal College, and the most substantial Welsh composer of her generation, which is a sentence that gets her filed rather than played. You like the 20th-century tonal line and you like string writing; this sits in the same room as the Tallis Fantasia and Elgar's Introduction and Allegro without imitating either, and it is a genuine gap rather than a charity case.

Five short movements — High Wind, Sailing Song, Channel Sirens, Breakers, Calm Sea in Summer — which tells you it is a suite of characters rather than an argument, and that is the interest. Because she has only strings, every change of character has to come from texture and division of the band: the two fast movements are largely about attack and register while Channel Sirens is about sustain and harmonics. Listen to it as a catalogue of what a string orchestra can be made to sound like when you cannot reach for a wind colour to change the subject — useful the next time you are scoring for strings alone and run out of ideas at bar forty. She dedicated it to her parents; it waited three years for its first performance, in 1947, from the BBC Welsh Orchestra under Mansel Thomas.

A composer to explore

One, with a route in — which piece first, then what.

Why now — Cyril Scott (1879–1970) Radio 3 gives him five afternoons starting Monday, which is more than he has had in a generation, and the reason to take it seriously is the company he kept. He was sent to the Hoch Conservatory in Frankfurt at twelve, where he fell in with Percy Grainger; his First Symphony was played in Darmstadt and Fritz Kreisler brought his Piano Quartet to London; he went to France, met Debussy and Ravel, and Debussy publicly backed him as a way forward. Stravinsky and Strauss both rated him. Then he lived until 1970, by which time he was considered so old-fashioned that he fell out with the BBC over it, and his last public appearance was at ninety in 1969. He is exactly the unexplored 20th-century tonal composer you keep saying must be out there, and this section has so far gone to Zelenka, Suk, Holmboe, Stenhammar, Myaskovsky, Weinberg and Rubinstein — none of whom had Debussy telling people to watch them.

Route in: Lotus Land, Op. 47 No. 1 (1905)

Start here — three minutes

Start with Lotus Land because it takes three minutes and settles the only question that matters, which is whether his harmonic ear is worth your time. It was his hit — the piece everyone played, and Kreisler made an arrangement of it — and it is parallel fourths and fifths and unresolved added notes in 1905, which is early enough that the usual explanation of “he heard Debussy” does not comfortably cover it. Radio 3 plays it twice this week, in two different pianists' hands.

Then: the Piano Quartet in E minor, Op. 16 — the Kreisler piece, on Monday's programme in the Primrose Piano Quartet's recording. This is where you find out whether he can build as well as colour, and it is the fairest test of him. After that: the Piano Concerto No. 1 (1913–14), Howard Shelley with the BBC Philharmonic under Martyn Brabbins, whose Chandos series is single-handedly why any of this is available — Tuesday's episode has the first movement. Then, if he has you: the Symphony No. 3, whose movements are named after the Muses, on Thursday. My honest expectation: you will like the harmony more than the forms, and you will find the piano writing better than the orchestration. Tell me if I am wrong — that is what the buttons are for.

Something to study

One free score, one page, and a composer telling you in his own words that he was making a joke. Elisabeth Brauss plays the whole set live from Wigmore Hall on Monday at 1pm, so you can read it in the morning and hear it in the afternoon.

This week — the page where Bartók threw out the key signature The 14 Bagatelles, Op. 6 date from 1908, the year his language changed, and they are usually described as containing in miniature nearly everything he went on to do. The first one is barely a minute and a half long and it is notated with a different key signature in each hand — four sharps in the right, four flats in the left. The score is free on IMSLP.

Bagatelle No. 1 — a notational joke that turned into a method

The exercise

First, read it as printed and try to hear two keys at once. You will fail, and that is the point. The right hand is in C sharp minor and the left is in C Phrygian, and if you actually listen rather than looking, the piece does not split into two independent layers at all — it coheres, because the two hands are drawing on transformations of the same mode. Scholars who have gone through it carefully make exactly this argument: the dual signature divides the page but not the sound. So the notation is describing something that is not there.

Second, take Bartók at his word about why. He wrote that “after carrying the key signature principle ad absurdum in the first piece, I dropped its use in all the other Bagatelles and in most of my following works as well.” Read that sentence twice, because it is doing more than it looks. He is not saying he invented polytonality. He is saying he wrote down a reductio — if a key signature is a promise about what notes will occur, then here is a page where the promise is made twice and kept neither time — and that having proved it absurd once, he stopped using the device for the rest of his life. One piece, written to retire a convention.

Why this is worth your afternoon and not just a footnote: you write music, and notation is the part of the job where you quietly accept other people's assumptions. What the first Bagatelle argues is that the key signature is not neutral information — it tells a player what to expect before a note has sounded, and once your harmony no longer works by functional expectation, that instruction is worse than useless. Then go through Nos. 2 to 14 with that in mind: no signatures, accidentals as they fall, and a set of pieces that are still perfectly readable. The lesson transfers directly. Every convention in your own scores is either carrying information or carrying a habit, and the way to tell is to write the piece that makes it absurd.
