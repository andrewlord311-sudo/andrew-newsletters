Listen this week

Three, all of them on the radio in the next five days — which is deliberate: it costs you nothing and the dates do the nagging. The Clara is under ten minutes and doubles as this week's homework.

String Quartet in F minor, Op. 5

Carl Nielsen (1865–1931)

Nielsen wrote four string quartets and almost nobody programmes them, which is strange for a composer whose symphonies are standard repertoire. This is the early one — 1890, written in his twenties, before the symphonist arrived — and it sits in exactly the territory you have said you want more of: twentieth-century-adjacent, entirely tonal, and in the line that runs out of Brahms and Dvořák rather than away from it.

Why it should suit you specifically: you study quartets as scores, not just as listening, and this one is a good test case because the argument is already Nielsen's own even while the manners are borrowed. The obstinacy you know from the symphonies — a line that decides on a direction and simply refuses to be talked out of it — is audible here inside a conventional four-movement frame. For a composer that is the interesting condition: inherited form, personal will, and the seams still visible. Brahms, whose seamlessness frightens you, is the opposite case, and hearing them a week apart is instructive.

The NOVO Quartet play it live from Wigmore Hall on Monday at 1pm, after Mendelssohn's A minor Quartet, Op. 13 — the one Mendelssohn wrote at eighteen. Mendelssohn is on your list as "growing on me"; Op. 13 is the piece most likely to finish that argument. Two quartets, actually live, free.

Four movements, around half an hour · live on Radio 3 Monday 19 October, 1pm, then on BBC Sounds

Variations on a theme by Robert Schumann, Op. 20

Clara Schumann (1853)

Nine minutes, and the most loaded nine minutes in this issue. Clara wrote these seven variations in May and June 1853 as a birthday present for Robert, on the Albumblatt from his Bunte Blätter, Op. 99. Within a year he had collapsed; Brahms then wrote his own variations on the same theme and gave them to Clara as a present of consolation, and at Brahms's request Breitkopf & Härtel published the two sets together, in one volume, in November 1854.

You say you are obsessed with that family and that you like Clara's music. This is the piece where the obsession has something concrete to work on, because the documentary record and the notes point at the same thing. Both sets are in F sharp minor. So is Robert's First Piano Sonata, Op. 11 — and Giorgi Gigashvili plays the Sonata and these Variations in the same recital on Monday at 7.30pm, which is a programming decision rather than a coincidence. He follows them with Fanny and Felix Mendelssohn's Songs without Words, so the first half of that concert is two musical families in parallel.

Then read it. The score is free (see Something to study), and IMSLP has Clara's own 1853 manuscript as well as the first edition.

Fantasie in F minor for piano duet, D940

Schubert (1828)

If Schubert speaks to you more than Beethoven does, this is one of the two or three pieces that explains why, and it is routinely missed because four-hands music is filed under domestic. It is from 1828, the last year, and it is a single continuous span in four linked sections that comes back to where it began — the F minor opening theme returning at the end with the fugue abandoned mid-argument.

The composer's reason to hear it this week: two players at one keyboard is a constraint, not a convenience, and Schubert uses the four hands for a density of inner-voice writing he could not get from two. Listen for how much of the pathos is carried by the second player's left hand — the bass is doing harmonic work that a solo texture would have to thin out. Pavel Kolesnikov and Samson Tsoy play it on Wednesday at 1pm, in a programme that continues with a live BBC Philharmonic concert of Brahms's Tragic Overture and Schubert's Ninth — so Wednesday lunchtime is late Schubert twice, small and large.

One continuous span, about twenty minutes · Radio 3 Wednesday 21 October, 1pm, then on BBC Sounds

A composer to explore

Five symphonies, written across forty years by an English composer who stayed tonal on purpose, and which essentially did not exist on record until this decade. One of them is on the radio on Friday.

Ruth Gipps, 1921–1999

Why now. On Friday at 1pm the BBC Philharmonic under Rumon Gamba broadcast her Symphony No. 5, Op. 64, of 1982 — the same orchestra and conductor who made the world première recording of it for Chandos. That is the whole case for her in one line: a British symphonist of the generation after Vaughan Williams, five symphonies, and the recordings only arrived in the 2020s. You wrote that you believe there are a lot of twentieth-century composers you have not explored. This is the clearest example I have found of one who was not available to be explored.

The route in. Step one is Friday, free: the Fifth, on the radio, with no commitment. Step two, if it lands, is the First Symphony, Op. 22, written in 1942 when she was twenty-one and premiered in 1945 by the City of Birmingham Orchestra under George Weldon — recorded for the first time on Chandos Volume 3 (CHAN 20284). Step three is Volume 2 (CHAN 20161), which has the Third Symphony and the Oboe Concerto; she was a professional oboist as well as a pianist, so the concerto is the piece written from inside the instrument. Volume 4 (CHAN 20319) pairs the Fifth with the Violin Concerto and Leviathan.

Why she should interest you in particular. She is in the line you have actually declared for — Elgar, Walton, Vaughan Williams, the twentieth century that did not go atonal — and she stayed in it while the post-war fashion went elsewhere, which is a position a composer can have an opinion about. Whether that reads as integrity or as insulation is the question to hold while listening, and it is a better question than "is she neglected".

The honest caveat. I have not heard all five, and a composer the record industry reached seventy years late is not automatically a composer the repertoire was wrong about. What I can tell you is that the entry point costs you nothing on Friday lunchtime, and that if it works there are four more symphonies and two concertos now actually available.

New music

One 21st-century tonal composer a week. This one is a working orchestrator as well as a composer, which is why she is here: the interest is in the scoring, and you will hear it immediately.

Dani Howard, born 1993

Who she is. British, born and brought up in Hong Kong, where from the age of nine she had a nine-year mentorship with Richard Bamping, principal cello of the Hong Kong Philharmonic. First-class degree from the Royal College of Music. She won a Royal Philharmonic Society Award in 2022 for the Trombone Concerto, was Resident Artist with the National Youth Orchestra of Great Britain in 2024, and in August this year the BBC Proms premiered Signal, a brass concerto — so she is not a rediscovery, she is someone currently mid-career whom you have most likely simply not crossed.

Why you in particular. She says her fascination with orchestral colour started when she heard Strauss's Don Quixote, and she works professionally as an orchestrator. The Times credits her with "a brilliant command of the orchestra"; Gramophone calls the sound world "luminous and effervescent" and another reviewer "technicolour orchestration". Orchestration and compositional technique are the two things your own note puts under "broader interests", and this is music where that is the main event rather than a by-product. The idiom is consonant and concerto-driven — Radio 3's Essential Classics has played the Trombone Concerto's first movement, which tells you roughly where it sits. Nothing here is minimalist and none of it is film music.

The route in. Everything below is on one disc — her debut orchestral album on Rubicon (RCD1125, 2024), the Royal Liverpool Philharmonic under Michael Seal and Pablo Urbina. Start with Argentum (2017), the short curtain-raiser that opens the album — a few minutes, and enough to tell you whether the scoring does anything for you. Then the Trombone Concerto (2021) in its three movements, written for Peter Moore during the 2020 lockdowns and premiered by the Royal Liverpool Philharmonic in June 2021; the trombone is a hard instrument to write a concerto for and that difficulty is the piece's subject. Then Coalescence (2019) if you want the purely orchestral side.

Where she sits. She belongs to the current British generation writing large, brightly-scored concert pieces for mainstream orchestras without apology — the opposite of a composer writing for a specialist new-music audience. If the first three minutes of Argentum do nothing for you, you have lost three minutes.

Something to study

One theme, two sets of variations, written months apart in the same house during its collapse — and published together in one volume. Both scores are free, and you can hear one of them on Monday night.

Clara's seven variations and Brahms's sixteen, on the same theme of Robert's

Clara Schumann, Op. 20 (1853) · Brahms, Op. 9 (1854)

The theme is the Albumblatt, No. 4 of Robert's Bunte Blätter, Op. 99. In May and June 1853 Clara wrote seven variations on it as a birthday present for him. In February 1854 Robert attempted his life and was taken to the asylum at Endenich. Brahms then wrote sixteen variations on the same theme and gave them to Clara. At Brahms's request Breitkopf & Härtel published both sets simultaneously, in November 1854. Both are in F sharp minor. This is as close as the repertoire gets to a controlled experiment.

What to actually do with it. Put the two scores side by side and work out where each of them stops trusting the theme. Clara's seven stay inside its harmonic frame and its phrase lengths; the theme is always locatable, and the set reads as homage — which was its function, it was a gift. Brahms's sixteen leave the frame, and the question for you is when: find the first variation in which you could no longer notate the original underneath, and then look at what he replaced it with. That is the technical difference between the two sets, and it is visible on the page rather than something you have to take on trust.

The reason this is the right week for it: you have said Brahms's seamlessness is what frightens you — that it is hard to know where you are in it. A variation set is the one form where he has to declare his position repeatedly, because each variation starts again. Sixteen consecutive restarts from a composer whose whole instinct is to hide joins is the most legible Brahms you will get, and Clara's set beside it is the control: same theme, same key, no seams to find.

IMSLP has the Breitkopf first editions of both — and for Clara's, her own 1853 holograph, now in the Universitäts­bibliothek Basel. Read her manuscript, then hear Gigashvili play it on Monday at 7.30pm.

Live near Colchester

One concert, nine miles away, and a correction to last week's issue.

Elgar, Cello Concerto · Mahler, Symphony No. 4

Wild Arts Ensemble · Orlando Jopling, conductor

Saturday 24 October, 7pm, St Peter ad Vincula Church, Coggeshall. Sébastien Van Kuijk plays the Elgar; Ellie Neate is the soprano in the Mahler. On paper this is the single best local date of the autumn for you — the Elgar concerto and a Mahler symphony, two of your declared loves, in a parish church about twenty minutes' drive from Colchester.

The reason to go rather than stream it: Mahler 4 is the one symphony of his that genuinely gains from a small room and a small band. It is his most chamber-like — no trombones, a solo violin tuned up a tone for the second movement, and a finale that is really a song with orchestra. In St Botolph's-sized acoustics you will hear the inner parts that a concert hall blends away, which is precisely the detail a composer goes for. Expect a reduced ensemble rather than a full Mahler orchestra, and treat that as the point.
