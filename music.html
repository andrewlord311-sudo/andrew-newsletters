Listen this week

Three, none of them from me before. The Pejáčević is the one to clear the time for; the Tabakova is fifteen minutes and arrives again on Thursday night, so you can hear it twice in a week by accident.

Symphony in F sharp minor, Op. 41

Dora Pejáčević (1885–1923)

A full-scale late-Romantic symphony, written during the First World War by a Croatian countess who died at thirty-seven, and not recorded at all until 2011. That last fact is the one worth sitting with: this is not a miniature or a curiosity salvaged from a drawer, it is a four-movement symphonic argument of about forty-five minutes that simply went unheard for roughly a century. You say the continuing Romantic line is where you live and that there must be composers in it you have not got to. Here is a whole symphony's worth.

Why it should suit you specifically: the idiom sits between the Schumann inheritance you are obsessed with and the Mahlerian scale you love, and it is unembarrassed about melody in the way Schumann is. The first movement runs about fourteen minutes on its own — an Andante maestoso that opens out into an Allegro con moto — and the slow movement arrives around sixteen minutes in. Listen as a composer and the interesting question is one of pacing: she takes her time establishing material in a way a more cautious writer would have cut, and whether that reads as spaciousness or as inexperience is genuinely arguable. Decide for yourself rather than taking a reviver's word for it.

And you get a second door into her on Monday at 1pm, free: Radio 3's live Wigmore relay opens with her Violin Sonata in D, Op. 26 ‘Spring’, played by Elena Urioste and Tom Poster (see On the radio below). Chamber and orchestral Pejáčević in the same week is more than this country has managed in most decades.

Four movements, around forty-five minutes · the reference recording is Sakari Oramo and the BBC Symphony Orchestra (Chandos CHSA 5299, 2022), which fills the disc out to about seventy minutes with her Piano Concerto, Op. 33, played by Peter Donohoe

Symphony No. 21 in F sharp minor, Op. 51 ‘Symphony-Fantasy’ (1940)

Nikolai Myaskovsky (1881–1950)

The short one, and the one with a story attached that makes it strange. Myaskovsky wrote twenty-seven symphonies, beginning in 1908, and this — a single unbroken span of about fifteen minutes, Andante sostenuto into an Allegro non troppo, ma con impeto and back again — is the one that got recorded. It is his most-recorded work by some distance. The oddity is the commission: it was written for the Chicago Symphony Orchestra's fiftieth anniversary. A Soviet symphonist in 1940, writing to order for an American orchestra, in the year that gives the arch form its particular tightness.

Hear it in the same week as Tuesday's Shostakovich Fifth (below) and the point makes itself. Both men were writing inside the same system, with the same thing at stake, and the Fifth is the piece everybody reaches for when they want to talk about what that did to music. Myaskovsky was the senior figure in that world and the answer he gives is completely different in temperament: no public rhetoric, no peroration, a single arch that returns to where it started. Fifteen minutes is a very small ask for a direct comparison that most people never make.

About fifteen minutes in Vasily Petrenko's Oslo Philharmonic recording (Lawo, 2020), which pairs it with Prokofiev's Fifth — a useful coupling, since it hangs the unfamiliar piece on one you know; Svetlanov takes it slower, nearer nineteen

Suite in Old Style ‘The Court Jester Amareu’

Dobrinka Tabakova (b. 1980)

The living composer of the three, and the one closest to a taste you have already declared. You like Arvo Pärt and you like Elgar and Vaughan Williams; reviewers of Tabakova keep reaching for exactly those two poles, describing a gift for string writing that connects English lyricism to Pärt's cooler, more sombre textures. This particular piece is the neoclassical one — scored for viola, harpsichord and strings, a set of short dance movements, and openly channelling Rameau's energy rather than imitating anybody modern.

Which makes it a direct answer to something you say you are curious about: re-using very old music. The harpsichord is not there as decoration, it is there because the suite means its title, and the question for you as a composer is how she avoids pastiche while keeping the dance forms intact. Worth knowing how it got heard at all: Manfred Eicher heard this suite at Gidon Kremer's Lockenhaus festival in 2008, and it led to ECM giving her a whole disc — String Paths, with Maxim Rysanov and the Lithuanian Chamber Orchestra, Grammy-nominated in 2014. Reviewers generally call the Suite the best thing on it.

Then on Thursday at 7.30pm Timothy Ridout and the Munich Chamber Orchestra play it at the Wigmore, in a programme with Mozart, a reconstructed Bach viola concerto and Britten (below). So you can hear a studio reading now and a live one on Thursday, and the two viola players will not agree.

Short — a handful of brief dance movements rather than a span · on ECM New Series 2239, String Paths (2013)

A composer to explore

Russian, tonal, and the largest single body of symphonies written in the twentieth century — which is a strange thing for a man you have probably never deliberately played.

Nikolai Myaskovsky, 1881–1950

Why now. On Tuesday evening Radio 3 broadcasts Shostakovich's Fifth, and this week you will hear a good deal about what it cost to write symphonies in Stalin's Russia. Myaskovsky is the person missing from that conversation. He began his first symphony in 1908, before the revolution, and was still writing them in 1950 — twenty-seven in all, which spans the whole arc from late Russian Romanticism to the Soviet post-war settlement in one continuous output by one hand. You enjoy Tchaikovsky, Dvořák and the Russian school, and you say there are twentieth-century composers you have not explored. This is not a gap, it is a crater.

The route in. Step one is the listen item above: the Twenty-First, fifteen minutes, single span. It is deliberately the easiest door — short, his most-recorded piece, and available in good modern sound. Step two: stay on the Petrenko disc and play the Prokofiev Fifth that sits beside it, back to back with the Myaskovsky. The two men were close contemporaries working the same seam, and hearing them consecutively tells you more about what is characteristic in each than any amount of reading will. Step three, only if the first two land: Yevgeny Svetlanov recorded the complete cycle with the State Academic Symphony Orchestra of Russia, so all twenty-seven are actually available to you — which is unusual, and means an exploration here has somewhere to go rather than petering out after the one famous piece.

The honest caveat. Twenty-seven symphonies by a composer the repertoire dropped is not twenty-seven masterpieces, and I am not going to pretend otherwise or rank them for you from a distance. What I can say is that the entry point is fifteen minutes long and the cycle is complete on record, which makes this one of the cheapest large explorations available to you.

Something to study

One score, free, and a live performance of it on the radio at one o'clock on Monday. This is the week to do the reading first.

Violin Sonata No. 3 in D minor, Op. 108 — the development that refuses to move

Brahms (1886–88), dedicated to Hans von Bülow

You have said the thing that frightens you about Brahms is the seamlessness — that it is sometimes hard to work out where you are in it. This movement is the best available counter-example, and therefore the best way to understand the problem, because here Brahms does the opposite of hiding the joins: he plants a landmark so large you cannot miss it, and then makes the music restless on top of it.

What to look at. Open the first movement and find the development. The piano's left hand takes the dominant — a low A — and holds it. One published programme note counts forty-six bars of it. Above that single unmoving note the violin works a variant of the first subject in bariolage, and the music moves through a succession of keys while the bass goes nowhere at all. The usual comparison, and a good one, is the pedal A hammered out by the timpani at the opening of the First Symphony: the same device, the same composer, a different scale of patience.

Why this answers your question. A development is conventionally where you lose your footing — the harmony is in motion and the themes are in fragments. Brahms inverts it. The harmony stands perfectly still and the surface agitates, so for once the listener knows exactly where they are standing and it is the drama that has gone missing, postponed until the recapitulation finally releases the bass. That is the Brahmsian trick seen from behind: the seamlessness you cannot navigate elsewhere is not vagueness, it is him choosing, usually, not to give you a landmark. Here he gives you one, and you can measure everything else against it.

Then hear it, the same day. Monday 1pm, Classical Live, live from Wigmore Hall: Elena Urioste and Tom Poster play this sonata, after the Pejáčević ‘Spring’ sonata and a Dvořák nocturne. Read the development in the morning and then listen for the moment the pianist's left hand settles and simply stays. Live, with no edit points, you also get to hear how a real pianist keeps forty-odd bars of one note alive.

Score free on IMSLP — several complete scores including the Henle · the movement is about eight minutes; allow an hour with the score and a recording, and keep Monday lunchtime free
