Week of 5 September 2026

The Proms finish this week, and the one to clear your diary for is Friday: Mahler's Ninth played on the instruments Mahler actually knew, by an orchestra assembled specifically to do that. The rest of this issue follows a single thread — what one composer takes from another when the borrowing is documented rather than guessed at. Bach writing a violin part over a lute suite someone else had already finished; Britten shrinking a Mahler movement down to fit a small orchestra. Both are on this week, both scores are free, and both tell you more in an hour than a term of analysis.

Listen this week

Three, and two of them turn up on Radio 3 on Wednesday lunchtime without you having to do anything.

The London Manuscript — lute sonatas (British Library Add. MS 30387)

Sylvius Leopold Weiss (1687–1750)

You play the lute and you are fond of Bach's instrumental output, so this is the piece of common ground between those two facts that nobody hands you. Weiss was the last great lutenist of the instrument's working life — the highest-paid instrumentalist at the Dresden court — and the London Manuscript holds twenty-six complete sonatas, plus preludes, fugues, fantasias and tombeaux. It is the largest body of serious solo lute music written after the repertoire you already know from Dowland, and it is written by someone who was still solving new problems on the instrument rather than curating an old style.

Here is the reason to spend an evening on him rather than simply noting he exists. Weiss and Bach knew each other; Weiss went to Leipzig in 1739 and stayed four weeks. Bach took Weiss's Suite in A major, SW 47 — a finished lute piece — and wrote a concertante violin part over the top of it. That is BWV 1025, and it sat in the Bach catalogue as Bach's own until the sources were compared and the keyboard part turned out to coincide exactly with Weiss's lute writing. Only the opening Fantasia is Bach's from scratch. So you can put the two scores side by side and watch Bach decorate a completed structure he did not write, without altering it. Not a reworking, not an arrangement — an overlay. There is almost no other case this clean of one great composer's hand visible on top of another's finished page, and it is free to read.

Dip in rather than sit through — the sonatas are sequences of short dance movements · both scores free on IMSLP

Sextet in C major, Op. 37 (1935)

Ernő Dohnányi (1877–1960)

You love Bartók. This is the other answer to Bartók's question, written in the same country in the same decade by the man who programmed and championed Bartók's music when almost nobody else in Budapest would. Bartók went out to the village recordings and built a new harmonic language from what he found; Dohnányi stayed inside the Brahms inheritance and tried to keep extending it. Neither is the coward's option, and hearing them a day apart makes the choice legible — Bartók's Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta, written the following year, is on Radio 3 on Friday lunchtime, and this Sextet is on Wednesday's.

What makes it worth a composer's attention rather than just a listener's is the ensemble itself. Clarinet, horn, violin, viola, cello, piano — premiered in June 1935, and his last piece of chamber music. That is a genuinely awkward group: a horn and a piano together will flatten a string trio without much effort, and there is no second violin to thicken the top. Watch how he keeps the strings audible — where he puts the horn, when he lets the piano take the bass so the cello can sing, how often the clarinet is doing the job a missing second violin would do. It is a balance problem you could set yourself, solved in front of you by someone who wrote for this combination once and got it right.

Military Sinfonietta, Op. 11 (1936–37)

Vítězslava Kaprálová (1915–1940)

You like the Czech line and you have said there are many twentieth-century composers you have not explored. Kaprálová is the one whose absence is hardest to justify. She started this in May 1936, finished it in February 1937, and conducted its premiere herself with the Czech Philharmonic in Prague that November. Then, on 17 June 1938, aged twenty-three, she stood in front of the BBC Orchestra in London and conducted it as the opening work of the sixteenth ISCM Festival — broadcast on to America by CBS. Bohuslav Martinů was her teacher. She died in 1940, aged twenty-five.

Listen to it as a piece of orchestral writing by someone who conducted her own orchestra and therefore knew exactly what would speak in a hall. It is short, it moves as one continuous span rather than in movements, and the scoring is lean in a way that is hard to fake — there is very little doubling for safety, which is the commonest tell of a young composer hedging. The title misleads: this is not a march. It was written as Czech independence was closing in on itself, and it is defiant rather than martial. If it lands, her Suita rustica is on Radio 3 on Wednesday at 1pm from this year's Proms, so you can hear a second piece without lifting a finger.

A composer to explore

One, with a route in — which piece first, then what.

Why now — Wilhelm Stenhammar (1871–1927) Last month this section went to Suk and then to Myaskovsky — the Czech and the Russian doors out of the nineteenth century. Stenhammar is the Nordic one, and in Britain he is close to invisible: two symphonies, two piano concertos, six string quartets, all tonal, and a working life spent as chief conductor of the Gothenburg Symphony rather than as a full-time composer. He was close to Nielsen and knew Sibelius, but he sounds like neither. He is the most direct case available of a composer who took the Brahms problem seriously — how do you keep writing large-scale symphonic music in that language after Brahms has finished with it — and answered it by going further into counterpoint rather than away from it.

Route in: Serenade in F major, Op. 31

Start here

Start with the Serenade rather than a symphony, because it is the piece where his ear for orchestral colour is least encumbered by argument — five movements, light on its feet, and it will tell you within ten minutes whether you want the rest. Blomstedt and the Gothenburg Symphony, the orchestra Stenhammar himself ran, is the obvious recording and it pairs the Serenade with the Second Symphony on one disc, which is convenient because that is exactly the order you want.

Then: the Symphony No. 2 in G minor, Op. 34 — the big statement, and the one that will speak to what you have said about Brahms's seamlessness frightening you, because Stenhammar is chasing the same continuity by openly contrapuntal means and you can therefore hear the joins he chooses to leave visible. After that: the six string quartets, which are the least-recorded corner of him and where he is at his most personal. The Serenade's score is free on IMSLP if you want to see the colour up close.

Something to study

For the composer's ear, not the listener's. Last week's two-scores-side-by-side worked, so here it is again with a harder question.

This week — what Britten threw away from Mahler's Third Wednesday's Prom includes Mahler's “What the Wild Flowers Tell Me” — the second movement of the Third Symphony — in Benjamin Britten's arrangement for small orchestra. Britten's own account of first hearing a Mahler symphony, not long after leaving school, was: “My new god.” This is that god, reduced by a young composer to fit a band a fraction of the size. You have the Planets score on the shelf, so you already know how much of Mahler's generation's effect is a function of sheer forces. Here is the controlled experiment on that.

Mahler, Symphony No. 3, second movement — the full score against Britten's reduction

The exercise

Mahler's Third is scored for one of the largest orchestras he ever asked for. Britten's arrangement keeps the movement and throws away most of the means. The whole score of the Third is free on IMSLP: open it at the second movement, put Wednesday's broadcast on, and mark the score as you go — not what you like, but what is missing, bar by bar.

The question worth carrying through it: which of Mahler's doublings were structural and which were insurance? Every orchestrator doubles for two quite different reasons — because the line genuinely needs the combined timbre, or because they are frightened it will not carry. A reduction made by a composer as good as Britten is a ruthless audit of that distinction, because he had to decide, for every bar, which category each doubling fell into. Where the music still works with a fifth of the players, Mahler was insuring. Where it goes thin or the harmony loses its floor, the doubling was load-bearing and Britten has had to invent a substitute — find those moments and see what he swapped in.

Then turn it on yourself, which is the actual point: your own scores almost certainly double for insurance in places, and this is the cheapest available method of learning to hear the difference before a rehearsal teaches you the hard way.

On the radio

The last week of the Proms. Friday is the one that matters; the rest of this list is what survives a hard filter.

When What
Fri 11 Sep
8.00pm
Radio 3, live		 Mahler, Symphony No. 9 — Mahler Academy Orchestra, Philipp von Steinaecker. Clear the evening for this. It is the Mahler Academy Orchestra's Proms debut, and the whole point of the project is to recreate the instruments Mahler himself would have known and get as close as possible to the work's original sound and style — students playing alongside professionals from Europe's leading ensembles. You have heard Mahler 9 on modern instruments many times. You have almost certainly never heard what the first-movement string writing does when the strings are gut and the horns are narrower-bored, and that movement is the one Berg called the greatest thing Mahler ever composed. The whole symphony, no interval concerto, nothing else on the bill.
Wed 9 Sep
7.00pm
Radio 3, live		 BBC Scottish Symphony Orchestra, Paweł Kapuła, Natalya Romaniw. Henze's orchestral fantasy on Schubert's Erlkönig, then Schubert's Eighth — the Unfinished — and after the interval Mahler's “What the Wild Flowers Tell Me” in Britten's arrangement and Strauss's Four Last Songs. Schubert speaks to you more than Beethoven does, so the Eighth alone earns the slot; the Britten arrangement is the study item above. A well-built programme rather than a bag of pieces: every work on it is about something being left unfinished or said last.
Thu 10 Sep
7.00pm
Radio 3, live		 Bach, Mass in B minor — Arcangelo, Jonathan Cohen. You have said Bach loses you when he goes too cerebral. This is the case for the defence, and it is the strongest one available: the B minor Mass is a compendium of styles assembled at the end of a life, and its contrapuntal choruses are built to overwhelm rather than to be admired. If the Art of Fugue is Bach with the audience removed, this is Bach with the audience firmly in the room. Arcangelo have made these Proms Baroque relays their own; Jonathan Cohen directs from the harpsichord.
Sat 12 Sep
7.00pm
Radio 3, live		 Last Night — BBC SO, Sakari Oramo, Yuja Wang. Listed with the usual caveat that it is a variety bill, but two things on it are worth setting a reminder for: Yuja Wang plays Barber's Piano Concerto, which is a genuinely serious and rarely programmed piece to find on a Last Night, and there are two BBC commissions receiving world premieres — Samy Moussa's Dithyramb and Anne Dudley's Iris of Life. Jupiter from the Planets is in the second half, which you have in score.

Two lunchtime notes, both on Classical Live at 1pm. Wednesday carries the Dohnányi Sextet from the Feldafing Festival and Kaprálová's Suita rustica from the Proms — two of this week's three recommendations, in one programme, for free. Monday has Mendelssohn's Octet from the Mecklenburg-Vorpommern Festival with Julia Fischer and Lena Neudauer leading, which is useful if Mendelssohn is still growing on you.
