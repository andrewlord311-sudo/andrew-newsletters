Listen this week

Three, spanning about four hundred years, and none of them longer than half an hour. The ratings say this is the section you act on most, so it gets the most thought: an obscurity, a canon, and something still warm.

The three Voluntaries for Double Organ

John Lugge (1580 – c.1647)

You have written down that you are curious about re-using very old music, especially by less well known composers. Lugge is about as precise an answer to that as England produces. He was organist at Exeter Cathedral for roughly forty years, he is barely recorded, and his three free voluntaries are — on the musicology's own account — probably the earliest surviving examples of the English “double voluntary”, the form Blow and Purcell later brought to maturity. John Steele's verdict is that they are the best examples of this peculiarly English genre written before the Civil War.

Why it should suit you specifically: the double voluntary is a texture problem before it is anything else. Two manuals, two contrasted registrations, and a solo line that has to stay audible against an accompaniment on a different keyboard with a different colour — which is orchestration, two centuries before the word meant anything. For a composer wondering what to steal from very old music, this is the kind of material that transplants: the voice-leading is modal, the spans are short, and the ideas are not so finished that they resist being taken somewhere else.

The timing is luck. William Whitehead recorded the first-ever disc of Lugge's organ music last year — The Forbidden Fruit (Hortus 273), taped in a French church chosen because its acoustic resembles Exeter's — and he is Hannah French's guest on The Early Music Show tomorrow, Sunday, at 5pm. Hear him talk about it first, then go to the voluntaries.

Three voluntaries, roughly five to eight minutes each · the Whitehead disc is the only complete survey there has ever been · Radio 3, Sunday 25 October, 5pm

Symphony No. 44 in E minor, ‘Trauer’

Joseph Haydn (1772)

You like Haydn and you are in the middle of a Mozart quartet score-study, which makes this the right week for the piece that shows what Haydn could do that almost nobody else dared. The Menuetto is a strict canon — Menuetto in canone, the second part following the first at the octave, a bar behind, all the way through — and then the trio inverts the manoeuvre into the major. It is a party trick that happens not to sound like one: played straight, it reads as an unusually severe minuet and the device stays underground.

That is the interesting thing for you rather than for a general listener. You have said Brahms frightens you because the seams are invisible; here is the same problem a century earlier and solved in the open, where you can actually see the mechanism on the page. Haydn sets himself a rule that removes most of his freedom and then writes music that does not sound constrained. Put it next to a Mozart quartet minuet from the Haydn set and the question answers itself: Mozart almost never needs a scaffold, Haydn builds one and makes it load-bearing.

The nickname is posthumous and the story attached to it — that Haydn asked for the slow movement at his own funeral — is reported rather than documented, so take it as flavour, not fact. The Adagio is the reason either way.

Four movements, about 25 minutes · the score is free on IMSLP · the first movement alone opens Thursday's Aberfan commemoration on Radio 3, 7.30pm

Epifania, for string orchestra

Pēteris Vasks (b. 1946)

Ten minutes, strings only, and the closest thing to Pärt's stillness that is not actually Pärt. Vasks is Latvian, a Baptist pastor's son who spent the Soviet years writing music that could not say what it meant, and his string writing has the same quality you respond to in Pärt — slowness that is doing something rather than merely being slow. Epifania is one of his quietest pieces: Schott put it at ten minutes flat, and it was first played in 2011 by Juha Kangas and the Ostrobothnian Chamber Orchestra, the ensemble he has written for most consistently.

Why he earns your time rather than a general listener's: Vasks is a double-bass player, and it shows in the way the bottom of the texture is voiced. He writes bass lines that move — not pedals, not drones, but genuine contrapuntal motion underneath music that appears static. That is a specific, learnable piece of craft, and it is the thing that keeps his slow music from going inert in the way a great deal of contemporary “spiritual minimalism” does. He is not a minimalist, for what it is worth: the harmonic rhythm is irregular and the forms are arch-shaped, not additive.

And it is on the radio: Dalia Stasevska conducts the BBC Symphony Orchestra in Epifania on Classical Live, Wednesday at 1pm. Ten minutes is a very small ask, and it is the one piece in this issue you could have heard and formed a view on before lunch.

About 10 minutes · string orchestra · Radio 3, Wednesday 28 October, 1pm

A composer to explore

A Czech who married into the family business and then had the worst two years imaginable.

Josef Suk (1874–1935)

He was Dvořák's best composition pupil and then his son-in-law, which is the kind of fact that gets a composer permanently filed as a footnote to someone else. It should not have. In 1904 Dvořák died; fourteen months later Suk's wife Otilie — Dvořák's daughter — died too, aged twenty-seven. He was thirty-one. What came out of that is the Asrael Symphony, named for the angel of death, and it is one of the genuinely great symphonies nobody in this country programmes.

Why now, and why you. You have written that the ongoing Romantic line into the twentieth century is where you live and that there must be composers in it you have not reached. Suk is the strongest remaining case. He is squarely in the Dvořák inheritance you already enjoy, but Asrael pushes the harmony much further out — chromatic, often bitonal at the edges, never atonal — and the orchestration is closer to Mahler than to his teacher. If you want to hear what happened to the Czech school after Dvořák without it turning into Janáček's very different problem, this is the route.

The route in. Do not start with Asrael. Start with the Serenade for Strings in E flat, Op. 6 — written at eighteen, about half an hour, and the piece Dvořák told him to write after complaining he was composing too much in the minor. It is sunlit, beautifully made, and it establishes the baseline you need. Then go to Asrael, and when you do, know that the first three movements were written for Dvořák and the last two were added after Otilie died; the hinge is audible and knowing where it falls changes the piece. After that, Ripening (Zrání, 1917) if you want the full late manner. Both scores below are free.

New music

One 21st-century tonal composer a week. This one has a world premiere broadcast on Tuesday, so the timing does the arguing.

Gavin Higgins (b. 1983)

Born in Gloucestershire, raised in Whitecroft in the Forest of Dean, and taught to play by the local colliery brass band before he ever saw a conservatoire. He went to Chetham's as a French horn player, studied composition at the RNCM and then with Kenneth Hesketh at the RCM, and became Rambert's first Musician-in-Residence. His publisher's own description of his music is “driven, aggressive, punchy and exciting; visceral in a word” — which is accurate and slightly undersells the formal discipline underneath it.

Why you in particular. He is the clearest living answer to the Bartók question: how do you write music with that much physical energy without the harmony collapsing into either modernist grey or folk pastiche? Higgins is pitch-centred throughout — this is not atonal music — but the harmony is pulled about by the rhythm rather than the other way round, and the folk material behind it is Gloucestershire and brass band rather than Hungarian. The other thing a composer notices immediately is the brass writing. Almost nobody currently working orchestrates brass as well, because almost nobody else learned it from the inside of a colliery band. His Concerto Grosso for Brass Band and Orchestra — premiered at the 2022 Proms by the Tredegar Band and BBC NOW under Ryan Bancroft, and winner of the 2023 RPS Award for large-scale composition — is the piece where that becomes the actual subject: two ensembles with incompatible tuning traditions and vibrato conventions, made to argue.

The route in. Start with the Concerto Grosso — it is the award-winner, it is the one with a commercial recording behind it, and it puts the brass writing in the foreground where you can study it. His Proms pieces Rough Voices (2020) and Der Aufstand (2012) are the next stop if it lands. Then, for the timely one, Tuesday night's Clarinet Concerto: four movements of “Nocturnes”, around thirty-five minutes, written for Mark Simpson, premiered live in Liverpool a week ago and broadcast on Radio 3 at 7.30pm on Tuesday. Bachtrack's reviewer reported an unusually long ovation. A fair warning in both directions: the same programme ends with The Rite of Spring, and Higgins also writes for the stage — stay with the concert works and you are on solid ground.

Something to study

Two scores, both free, side by side — and one of the most open acts of borrowing in the symphonic repertoire.

Dvořák's Sixth against Brahms's Second

Brahms finished his Second Symphony in 1877. Dvořák finished his Sixth in 1880. Both are in D major. Both open in 3/4, quietly, with a rocking figure low in the texture. Both run four movements to roughly the same proportions, and both finales begin sotto voce and build to a blazing D major coda. Dvořák was not hiding it: Brahms had got him his publisher and his career, and the Sixth is a deliberate, public act of homage to a man who was still alive to notice.

What to actually do with the scores. Put the two first movements side by side and read only the opening thirty bars of each. The question to hold is the one you keep returning to about Brahms — why you cannot tell where you are. Here is a controlled experiment, because Dvořák has copied the furniture and not the method. Brahms's opening withholds the downbeat and keeps the phrase lengths irregular, so the ear never gets a grid to count against. Dvořák's opening uses the same gesture but squares the phrases off into clean four-bar units, and the result is that you always know exactly where you are. Same key, same metre, same texture, opposite effect. The difference is phrase rhythm, and it is visible on the page in about ten minutes of looking.

Then jump to Dvořák's third movement, which is the place he stops being deferential: a Furiant, cross-accented two-against-three, and completely un-Brahmsian. The structure is borrowed; the dance is not. That is the whole argument of the piece in one movement.

Conveniently, Radio 3's Through the Night carries Dvořák's Sixth from Norway in the small hours of Friday morning, so the catch-up will be sitting there if you want the sound in your ears before you open the score.

Live near Colchester

Mahler's Fourth, twice, tonight, three miles apart. And an apology.

Last week's “correction” was the error. The 17 October issue told you that the Colchester Symphony Orchestra concert did not exist — that it was “wrong on all three counts” and the real event was Wild Arts at Coggeshall. That was over-correcting. Both concerts are real, both are tonight, and both play Mahler 4. The CSO's own booking page confirms its venue, programme and £18 price; Bachtrack confirms the Coggeshall one. Apologies — if you crossed the Colchester date out of the diary, put it back.

Mahler, Symphony No. 4 · Strauss, Four Last Songs

Colchester Symphony Orchestra · Elinor Rolfe Johnson, soprano

Tonight, Saturday 24 October, 7.30pm, St Botolph's Church, Colchester. £18, free for under-25s. Fifteen minutes from your front door for a Mahler symphony and the Four Last Songs is an absurd piece of value, and the soprano carries both halves — Strauss before the interval, then Das himmlische Leben to end the Mahler. Hearing one voice do the valedictory Strauss and then Mahler's child's-eye heaven in a single evening is a genuinely well-built programme, not a scratch pairing.

The concert opens with the Meistersinger overture, which is — by your own rule — precisely the one Wagner exception you allow yourself. Arrive on time rather than skipping it; it is eight minutes and it is the one.
