Listen this week

Four. Two English symphonies you have almost certainly never heard, one piece of very late Schumann, and fifteen minutes of Hungarian dance music to clear the palate.

Symphony in G minor

Ernest John Moeran (1894–1950)

Moeran wrote one symphony, took three years over it, and then spent the rest of his life not writing the second. It is the piece English music keeps almost remembering: a full four-movement Romantic symphony from 1934–37, in a language that sits between Sibelius’s bleakness and the pastoral line you already like, with a slow movement that is as good as anything Walton managed.

Why it should suit you: this is exactly the gap you identified in your own note. You like Elgar, Walton and Vaughan Williams and you suspect there is more of it — there is, and this is the best single piece of evidence. The first movement does the thing you admire in Brahms and find unnerving, growing a whole argument out of a short modal figure, but the seams are visible, so you can hear the joins you cannot find in Brahms. Use it as the diagnostic.

Symphony No. 2 in B major, Op. 30

Ruth Gipps (1921–1999)

Written in 1945, when she was twenty-four, and cast as a single unbroken movement that runs through six tempo spans — Moderato, Allegro moderato, Andante, a march, Adagio, and back to Allegro moderato. Gipps was a Vaughan Williams pupil, a professional oboist and pianist, and a conductor the profession spent thirty years refusing to employ, so she founded her own orchestras and programmed herself.

Why it should suit you: you are a composer, and this is a formal problem rather than a piece of advocacy. A symphony in one movement has to solve the thing you keep circling — how you know where you are — without the help of four full stops, and she does it with tempo rather than with key. Twenty-two minutes is also a low price for finding out whether the 1940s English symphony is a seam worth mining, and at this length you will actually finish it.

Gesänge der Frühe, Op. 133

Robert Schumann (1810–1856)

Five short pieces from 1853, dedicated to the poet Bettina von Arnim, and among the last coherent music he wrote — the Rhine and the asylum were months away. They are not late Schumann in the sense of tired Schumann. They are strange: bare two- and three-part writing, chorale shapes that will not resolve where the ear expects, a harmonic logic that is clearly deliberate and clearly not the logic of the Fantasie or the Kreisleriana.

Why it should suit you: you call him your Prince of Music and you are obsessed with that family, which usually means the 1830s piano cycles and the symphonies. This is the far end of the same mind, and the interesting part of the story is that Clara could not make sense of it — the person who knew his music better than anyone alive found these enigmatic. Thirteen minutes to hear the one corner of Schumann that the Schumann devotees mostly skip, with the score free alongside.

Five pieces · about 13 minutes · 1853 · Mitsuko Uchida

Divertimento No. 1, Op. 20 — ‘Old Hungarian Dances’

Leó Weiner (1885–1960)

Five dances for string orchestra from 1934, built on old Hungarian tunes: a csárdás, a fox dance, a waltz from Marosszék, a verbunkos and a stamping dance. Weiner is the third name in Hungarian music of that generation and the one nobody abroad knows, largely because he spent his life as the Budapest Academy of Music’s chamber-music teacher — the man who trained the players and conductors who then went and performed everyone else’s music.

Why it should suit you: you love Bartók, and Weiner is the control experiment. Same country, same folk sources, same decade — and he stayed inside functional tonality and lush string writing while Bartók went where he went. Hearing the material you know from Bartók handled by someone with no interest in dismantling it tells you a lot about what Bartók was actually choosing to do. Fifteen minutes, and the string writing alone is worth the time.

A composer to explore

Eleven symphonies, a pupil of the man whose Planets score sits on your shelf, and the nearest thing in English music to the seamlessness that frightens you in Brahms.

Edmund Rubbra (1901–1986)

Rubbra came to composition sideways — a railway clerk’s son from Northampton who worked in a factory, taught himself from Cyril Scott, and then studied with Holst at the Royal College. He wrote eleven symphonies between 1937 and 1979 and is now almost completely absent from concert programmes, which is harder to explain than the usual neglect stories: Barbirolli premiered the Fifth, took it into the studio at Abbey Road in December 1950 with the composer in the room, and the piece still did not stick.

Why you in particular: Rubbra’s music is contrapuntal from the ground up. He does not write themes and then develop them — he starts a line and the whole movement is that line continuing to grow, which produces exactly the quality you described in Brahms, where it “sounds SO seamless that it’s sometimes hard to work out where I am in it”. The difference, and the reason he is useful to you rather than merely pleasant, is that Rubbra’s textures are thinner than Brahms’s. You can hear the individual strand doing the work. If you want to understand the trick that unsettles you, this is the composer who performs it with the lid off.

The route in. Start with the Fifth Symphony (1947–48) — the most immediately graspable of the eleven, four movements, and the one that nearly made it into the repertoire. Then the Seventh (1956–57), whose third movement is a Passacaglia and Fugue: a composer working in inherited strict forms in the middle of the twentieth century without irony or apology, which is worth your attention on its own. After that, the cycle is on YouTube end to end, and the Third and Ninth are where people who stay usually end up.

New music

One 21st-century tonal composer a week. This one is an orchestrator before she is anything else, which is the part of your stated interests this section has not served yet.

Andrea Tarrodi (b. 1981)

A Swedish composer based in Stockholm, and Sveriges Radio P2’s composer-in-residence from 2011 to 2013. She writes orchestral and chamber music in a language that is tonal and modal rather than systematic — built from colour, slow harmonic motion and very precisely calculated instrumental blend. No minimalism, no process, no grid: the pieces are through-composed and they go somewhere.

Why you in particular: your note lists orchestration alongside form and technique as a standing interest, and that is the thing she is genuinely exceptional at. She is also the straightest continuation available of the Nordic line after Sibelius, which connects to music you already like without sounding like a pastiche of it. And she fits your own description of the century — flying off in all directions — while staying somewhere your ear can follow. If you want one living composer to study for scoring rather than for ideas, she is the pick.

The route in. Birds of Paradise first — nine minutes, written for strings in 2008 and re-scored for the Västerås Sinfonietta in 2013, and prompted by the birds-of-paradise footage in the BBC’s Planet Earth. It is the quickest way to find out whether the ear suits you. Then Liguria (2012), in three movements named for waves, the horizon and a blue path; the Royal Stockholm Philharmonic gave its UK premiere at the Proms in 2017, and the recording below is the Frankfurt Radio Symphony under Dalia Stasevska. Then Miroirs, her first string quartet, where you can hear the same ear working with four instruments instead of eighty — the better test of whether the craft is real.

Something to study

You wrote that Brahms terrifies you because it sounds so seamless you cannot tell where you are. There is a concrete, mechanical answer to that, and the Fourth Symphony is where you can see it with a pencil.

Brahms, Symphony No. 4 in E minor, Op. 98 — the chain of thirds

The thing to actually do this week. Download the full score from IMSLP (five editions, all free — the Breitkopf and the Dover reprint of it are the cleanest to read) and open the first movement. The opening violin melody looks like a tune. It is not. It is a chain of falling thirds — B–G, E–C sharp, A–F sharp, D sharp–B — with the gaps between each pair filled by rising sixths, which are the same interval turned upside down. Take a pencil and bracket every third in the first sixty bars.

Then the part that answers your question. Once you have marked them, keep going, because the second subject, the accompaniment figures and the transitions are made of the same interval, inverted, filled in, stretched across different note-values. That is why you cannot find the joins: there are no joins, because there is no new material to join on. Everything is a transformation of bar one. The technical name is developing variation, and the reason it unsettles a composer specifically is that it dissolves the thing you were taught to rely on — the arrival of a contrasting theme as a signpost.

And the finale, which is the same lesson in strict form. The last movement is a chaconne: thirty variations and a coda over an eight-bar bass, adapted from the chaconne that ends Bach’s cantata Nach dir, Herr, verlanget mich, BWV 150. Brahms raised one note of Bach’s line by a semitone to make it work. So the symphony that sounds most like free organic growth is bolted to the most rule-bound form available — which is the actual answer to how he does it, and it is not mystical.
