Week of 15 August 2026

A thread ran through this week's research without being planned: variation over a fixed idea. Dowland builds an hour out of one falling four-note figure, Walton ends his Second Symphony with a passacaglia and Radio 3 broadcasts it on Sunday, and Haydn drops three full fugues into the ends of the Op. 20 quartets to see what the form can carry. That last one is the study item, and it lands on the question you keep asking — where the eras actually collide.

Listen this week

Four, and none of them likely to be already on your shelf.

Octet for Strings in C minor, Op. 15a

Woldemar Bargiel (1828–1897)

The Schumann family from the inside. Bargiel was Clara's half-brother — same mother — and Robert's protégé, and he wrote this octet at about twenty-one, deep inside that household's orbit. If you are obsessed with that family, this is the piece of evidence you have not heard: not Robert imitated but a talented young man in the same room, writing dense C minor string music that sounds like the Mendelssohn octet being pulled somewhere darker. It also answers a question the biographies don't — what the Schumann circle actually sounded like when it wasn't Schumann.

Lachrimae, or Seaven Teares (1604)

John Dowland

You know the lute songs. This is the other Dowland: five viols and lute, seven pavans, every one of them built on the same falling four-note figure — Lachrimae Antiquae, Antiquae Novae, Gementes, Tristes, and so on down. It is a composer re-using his own material twenty-one ways in public, which is exactly the thing you say you're curious about with very old music, except here the person doing the reviving is the composer himself. Sit with how little he changes each time and how far it travels anyway. Savall's Hespèrion XX recording is the one that made the case for the set.

About 70 minutes complete; the seven pavans alone run about 35 · Jordi Savall / Hespèrion XX

Das klagende Lied — Part I, "Waldmärchen"

Gustav Mahler

The Mahler you almost certainly have not sat with, and the argument for it is a composer's argument rather than a listener's. He wrote this at nineteen and twenty, before the First Symphony, before anything — then cut this entire opening part and suppressed it, and it was not performed until the 1930s. What is startling is how little he had left to learn: the offstage effects, the fairy-tale cruelty, the marches breaking in from somewhere else, the way a texture goes hollow at exactly the right word. It is a cantata on a folk ballad, so nothing here troubles your exclusions. Listen to Waldmärchen on its own first, then decide whether he was right to cut it.

Waldmärchen about 27 minutes; all three parts about 65

String Quartet No. 4 (1951)

Grażyna Bacewicz (1909–1969)

If you love Bartók, this is the next room along, and hardly anyone in this country has been in it. Bacewicz was a first-rate violinist as well as a composer, which you can hear in how the writing sits under the hand — hard-edged rhythm, Polish folk material worked hard rather than quoted, and a slow movement that earns its stillness. Crucially for you it is centred, not atonal: she took Bartók's rhythmic drive and motivic density without following anyone into the Second Viennese cul-de-sac. It won first prize at the Liège international competition the year it was written. The Silesian Quartet recorded all seven for Chandos if this one lands.

A composer to explore

One, with a route in — which piece first, then what.

Why now — Nikolai Myaskovsky (1881–1950) You say there are 20th-century composers you have not explored. Myaskovsky is the largest of them: twenty-seven symphonies and thirteen string quartets, all of it tonal, almost none of it played here. He taught at the Moscow Conservatory for thirty years and was close to both Prokofiev and Shostakovich — close enough that when the 1948 Zhdanov decree went after "formalism", his name was on the list beside theirs. The reason he vanished is not quality; it is that twenty-seven symphonies is an intimidating door with no obvious handle. Here is the handle.

Route in: Symphony No. 21 in F sharp minor, Op. 51 (1940)

Start here

Single movement, about seventeen minutes, and the most concentrated thing he wrote. It opens with a bare clarinet solo and closes on quiet string chords, and everything between is one continuous argument rather than a symphony in disguise — which makes it the rare late-Romantic symphonic piece you can hold entirely in your head on one hearing. Ormandy took it up in America almost immediately, which tells you how obviously good it seemed at the time. Then: the Cello Concerto (1944), which is the piece that will make you a convert if anything does — Elgar's autumn light with a Russian accent. After that: Symphony No. 6, the big one, where the scale finally justifies itself.

Symphony No. 21 about 17 minutes · Svetlanov and the State Academic Symphony Orchestra of Russia on the link below

Something to study

For the composer's ear, not the listener's.

This week — the fugue that would not stay dead Your standing question is how the Baroque, Classical and Romantic eras ran headlong into each other. Here is one collision you can watch happen in two scores, eighteen months apart, and you already own half of them.

Haydn, Op. 20 (1772) → Mozart, K. 168 and K. 173 (1773)

The exercise

In 1772 Haydn ends three of the six Op. 20 quartets — No. 2 in C, No. 5 in F minor, No. 6 in A — with a full worked fugue. Not a fugato passage: a fugue, labelled as such, No. 2's headed Fuga a 4 soggetti and No. 6's Fuga a 3 soggetti. This is Baroque machinery being bolted onto the end of the newest, lightest form going, at the exact moment counterpoint was supposed to be finished. Note how much of each fugue is marked to be played quietly, so the entries have to be heard rather than announced — that restraint is the whole trick, and it is what stops the movement sounding like an academic exercise.

The following year Mozart, aged seventeen and in Vienna, writes his six "Viennese" quartets and puts fugal finales on two of them, K. 168 in F and K. 173 in D minor. He is not being coincidental: K. 168 takes thematic material straight out of Haydn's Op. 20 No. 5. So put the two side by side. What does Mozart keep, what does he not yet understand, and where does his fugue turn back into a tune when Haydn's would have held its nerve? Then jump forward twelve years to the finale of K. 387, where he finally does the thing properly — and you have your own complete quartets in score for that.

An evening with two scores and no recording needed, though both are on record everywhere

On the radio

Proms season is at its midpoint. Every Prom goes out live on Radio 3 and stays on BBC Sounds for thirty days afterwards — the season opened on 17 July, so the first fortnight's concerts are dropping off the catch-up shelf as you read this. Start times vary; confirm on the Proms site before you commit an evening.

Sun 16 Aug Elgar, Cello Concerto · Walton, Symphony No. 2
Royal Philharmonic Orchestra, Vasily Petrenko; Pablo Ferrández (cello, Proms debut). The Walton is the reason to set a reminder — a thirty-minute symphony from 1960 that is barely ever programmed, and its finale is a passacaglia, which makes it an accidental companion to the Dowland above.
