Symphony No. 2 in D major, Op. 11

Norbert Burgmüller (1810–1836)

Schumann finished this piece. Not wrote it — finished it: the orchestration of the scherzo was still incomplete when Burgmüller died at twenty-six, and Schumann completed it in 1851, fifteen years later. He also set out to build a finale from the sketches and abandoned the idea, so the symphony has no finale and never will. It was first performed in Düsseldorf on 22 April 1837 under Julius Rietz with only two movements playable, and nobody published it until 1864. Schumann's obituary is the line worth carrying around: “Since the early death of Franz Schubert, nothing more deplorable has happened than that of Burgmüller.”

Why it should suit you: you have written down that you want to understand how the Baroque, Classical and Romantic eras ran headlong into each other. This is the seam itself, with the stitching exposed. A man who died the year before Victoria came to the throne, working inside a frame that is still recognisably Beethoven's, with harmony in it that is already Schumann's — and then the music simply stops, because he did. One reviewer notes that it opens in a bucolic D major and ends in a surprising state of agitation, which is partly the writing and partly the fact that the end is not an ending. String Octet in C minor, Op. 15a

Woldemar Bargiel (1828–1897)

You have written that you are obsessed with the Schumann family. Here is the member of it nobody plays. Bargiel was Clara's half-brother — she was nine years older — and so Robert's brother-in-law, and he grew up inside exactly the circle this week's radio is about. He wrote the octet in 1849–50 and it sat unpublished until 1877.

The thing that makes it interesting rather than merely biographical is the choice of forces: four violins, two violas and two cellos, which is Mendelssohn's Op. 20 line-up, taken up roughly two decades after Mendelssohn did it and by someone who knew precisely whose shadow he was standing in. Reviewers put his chamber writing between Mendelssohn and Brahms, and for once that lazy formula is useful, because you like both ends of it and the interest is in watching which end wins in any given passage. It opens Adagio – Allegro appassionato, so the argument starts slowly and then commits.

Three movements, about 34 minutes · composed 1849–50, published 1877 · score free on IMSLP · recorded by the Orpheus Quartet and friends for CPO

Symphony in G minor, Op. 43

William Sterndale Bennett (1816–1875)

Start with the radio on this one: on Monday at 1pm Steven Isserlis and Connie Shih play Bennett's Sonata Duo for cello and piano live from Wigmore Hall on Classical Live, which is a free twenty-minute audition before you commit to the symphony. Bennett is the Englishman inside the Leipzig circle: he made several long stays there, and both Schumann and Mendelssohn thought highly of him. Then he came home, became a teacher, conductor and administrator, and largely stopped — which is the actual tragedy of his reputation.

Op. 43 dates from 1863–64, written after he had mostly given up composing, and it is the best case for what was lost. MusicWeb's reviewer hears a resemblance to Schumann's Fourth — the same driving energy relieved by quiet lyrical stretches — but notes that Bennett avoids the cyclic machinery and the neurotic undertone, and keeps the mood affirmative. For you that is the interesting comparison rather than a selling point: the same materials, deliberately not wired together the way Schumann wires them. One warning — there are two Bennett symphonies in G minor, and the earlier unpublished one (WoO 31, mid-1830s) gets mislabelled on YouTube constantly. The link below is Nicholas Braithwaite's Lyrita recording of Op. 43, which is the right piece.

String Quartet No. 6 in F minor, Op. 80

Felix Mendelssohn (1809–1847)

You have said Mendelssohn is growing on you. This is the piece that should finish the job, because it is the one place where the thing people hold against him is simply absent. He wrote it in 1847 after his sister Fanny died, and he was dead himself within months; it is widely known as the “Requiem for Fanny”.

The fluency is gone — or rather, it is being refused. Tremolando writing, unison outbursts, a first movement that will not settle into the easy symmetry he could produce in his sleep. Put it directly against your Brahms problem: Brahms terrifies you because the joins are invisible, and here is a composer who could always make things join, deciding not to, in the one work where it would have been a lie. Mendelssohn is also the host of this entire week — Gewandhaus conductor, the man who premiered Gade's First and championed Bennett — so it is worth hearing him at his least characteristic while the rest of the week circles him.

A composer to explore

The Dane whom Mendelssohn hired after Copenhagen turned his symphony down — and he is on Radio 3 three times this week, which makes this an unusually cheap week to try him.

Niels Wilhelm Gade (1817–1890)

The biography is the hook. Copenhagen rejected his First Symphony, so Gade sent the score to Leipzig instead; Mendelssohn received it warmly and conducted it at the Gewandhaus in March 1843, to an enthusiastic reception. Schumann thought the Scherzo the most original thing in it. Mendelssohn then engaged him as assistant conductor of the Gewandhaus orchestra and a teacher at the Conservatoire, and when Mendelssohn died in 1847 Gade became chief conductor — at twenty-nine, in the most important concert job in Germany. He went home in 1848 when war broke out between Prussia and Denmark, and spent the rest of his life building Danish musical institutions instead.

What makes him worth a composer's attention is that you can hear Leipzig happen to him. The Echoes of Ossian overture won a Copenhagen Musical Society prize and was first played there on 19 November 1841, then twice in Leipzig in early 1842 — so it is a provincial twenty-four-year-old's prize piece, written before he had met any of these people. The First Symphony is what got him the job eighteen months later, and it is built on a Danish folk melody (the title translates as “On Sjølund's fair plains”), which is a nationalist gesture made in order to be accepted by a German institution. The distance between those two scores is the whole story.

The route in. Echoes of Ossian, Op. 1 first — about 15 minutes, and the quickest way to find out whether his sound appeals to you at all. If it does, go to the Symphony No. 1 in C minor, Op. 5 (about 31 minutes) and listen for the Scherzo that Schumann singled out. Then let the radio do the rest of the work: Echoes of Ossian and the First Symphony are both on Classical Live on Thursday at 1pm, and the Hamlet overture follows on Friday at 1pm with the BBC Symphony Orchestra under Martijn Dendievel.

New music

One 21st-century tonal composer a week. This one has written six symphonies, and you will struggle to hear any of them on the radio.

Matthew Taylor (born London, 6 December 1964)

An English composer and conductor who won a music scholarship to Queens' College, Cambridge in 1983 and studied with Robin Holloway there, then took his composition further with Robert Simpson and David Matthews. His catalogue runs to six symphonies plus string quartets and concertos — which is to say he has spent a career writing the forms you actually care about, tonally, while that was deeply unfashionable.

Why you in particular. Nielsen's symphonies matter to Taylor the way they mattered to Simpson: MusicWeb's reviewer puts it well, saying the Dane's spirit is present in the Fourth without ever dominating it. His Fourth and Fifth were recorded by Kenneth Woods with the BBC National Orchestra of Wales and the English Symphony Orchestra, released on Nimbus Alliance (NI6406) on 6 November 2020 as part of the label's 21st Century Symphony Project — which Nimbus itself describes as a rebuttal to modernists who think the symphonic form is outmoded. That is an argument you are already standing inside, and it is being made in actual four-movement symphonies rather than in essays. The Symphony No. 4, Op. 54 (2015–16, about 29½ minutes) was written in memory of his friend the composer John McCabe, who died in 2015; the Fifth was commissioned for the English Symphony Orchestra's 21st Century Project and premiered at Cadogan Hall in June 2019.

The route in. Start with the Adagio of the Fourth with Woods's own spoken introduction — five minutes, and the most honest short sample of how he writes a slow movement. Then the first movement of the Violin Concerto (2011), Allegro con forza, about 13 minutes, which is where the Bartókian bite is easiest to hear. If both hold up, the Fourth and Fifth complete are on the Nimbus disc.

Something to study

A free score, timed about as well as it ever gets: Building a Library surveys this symphony at around 3pm today, and Classical Live broadcasts a live performance of it on Thursday.

Schumann, Symphony No. 2 in C major, Op. 61 (1845–46)

IMSLP holds seven public-domain full scores of this symphony. The one to download is Clara Schumann's own edition for Breitkopf & Härtel, 1887, from Robert Schumanns Werke, Series I — 134 pages, bookmarked by movement. Dover's 1980 reprint is the same plates, so given the family obsession you may as well read it in the edition his widow prepared. Schumann wrote in a letter that the symphony “told a tale of many joys and sorrows”, and it is habitually read as autobiographical; it is dedicated to Oscar I of Sweden and Norway.

Two things to look for, both contrapuntal.

One: the brass call in the slow introduction. Mark where it first appears, then track every return. The finale is the obvious place and you will find it there; the real question is how much of it you can locate in between, and then whether you think the symphony has earned that return by the end or is simply asserting it. That is a judgement about your own practice as much as about Schumann's, which is why it is worth an hour with a pencil rather than a listen.

Two: the Adagio espressivo, against the canons. This symphony comes out of the mid-1840s stretch when Schumann was working hardest on counterpoint — the same period as the Studies for Pedal Piano in canonic form, Op. 56. On Monday at 1pm Steven Isserlis plays No. 4 of those canons in his own arrangement, live from Wigmore Hall, roughly twenty minutes before the same programme broadcasts the Spring Symphony. So: hear the canon, then open the Adagio and read its subject and the answer that follows it, and work out what he kept and what he threw away when the same habit of mind had to carry a symphonic slow movement instead of a study.
