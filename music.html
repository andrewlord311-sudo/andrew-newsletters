Symphony in C minor, VB 142

Joseph Martin Kraus (1756–1792)

Kraus was born the same year as Mozart and outlived him by a year, and he spent his working life a very long way from Vienna — German-born, he made his career in Stockholm in the service of Gustav III. This is the symphony of his that people actually play. The Mozartists’ note for their recording dates it to Vienna, probably 1783, and reports that Haydn’s remarks on the piece helped its reception — reported rather than documented, so treat it as the sort of story that attaches itself to music people liked.

Why it should suit you: this is the other answer to your question about how the eras ran headlong into each other. Not Mozart, but a man of exactly Mozart’s age writing hard C minor Sturm und Drang at the same moment, with a slow introduction that behaves like a door being forced rather than opened. Hearing it immediately raises the real question, which is not “why was Mozart better” but “what, precisely, is the difference” — and that is a question you can answer from the score, because both are free.

String Quartet No. 3 in E flat major

Juan Crisóstomo de Arriaga (1806–1826)

Born in Bilbao in 1806, wrote three string quartets in his teens, and was dead at nineteen. IMSLP dates the set to around 1823 and holds it as Arriaga published it — Trois Quatuors, “Livre 1”, as though there were going to be a second book. There wasn’t. The third is the one to start with: Allegro, a Pastorale – Andantino, a Menuetto with trio, and a finale marked Presto agitato.

Why it should suit you: this is the control experiment for the Mozart question. Here is a genuinely gifted teenager with a complete command of the Classical quartet frame, working forty years after Mozart with all of Haydn, Mozart and early Beethoven available to him — and the gap is still audible. Finding out where it sits is much more interesting than being told Mozart was a genius. The Cuarteto Quiroga issued the complete quartets in 2025 and the Cuarteto Casals recorded them for harmonia mundi, so this is not an obscurity you have to suffer through in a bad performance.

Serenade in C major for string trio, Op. 10

Ernő Dohnányi (1877–1960)

You love Bartók. Dohnányi is the senior Hungarian who made Bartók’s generation possible and then got written out of the story for not being radical enough — which is exactly why he is useful to you now. The Serenade is from 1902–3, and it is in five movements: a Marcia, a Romanza, a Scherzo, a Tema con variazioni and a Rondo.

Why it should suit you: you have written that Brahms’s seamlessness terrifies you — that it is sometimes hard to work out where you are in it. This is Brahms-schooled writing reduced to three instruments, which means the seams have nowhere to hide. Twenty minutes, five clearly labelled movements, one of them an explicit variation set: it is the same craft problem in a form where you can see every join. Heifetz, Primrose and Feuermann recorded it in 1941, which tells you what players have always thought of it. And IMSLP has both the score and a complete recording, so the whole thing costs nothing.

Symphony No. 4 in C minor, Op. 12

Sergey Taneyev (1856–1915)

Taneyev was Tchaikovsky’s pupil and then, after his death, the man who edited and orchestrated his late music — so he is inside the Russian school you already enjoy, but from the technical end of it. He wrote four symphonies and published this one as his First, which is why the numbering looks wrong wherever you meet it. It dates from 1896–98: Allegro molto, Adagio, a Scherzo (Vivace), and a finale marked Allegro energico, molto maestoso.

Why it should suit you: he is the Russian composer who went at counterpoint the way Brahms did, and the result is a big tonal symphony with a structural rigour the rest of the Mighty Handful tradition does not much bother with. If Balakirev’s Tamara landed with you three weeks ago as colour, this is the same world argued rather than painted. Thomas Sanderling recorded it with the Novosibirsk orchestra for Naxos, Valeri Polyansky for Chandos in 2001, and Svetlanov left a 1988 account with the USSR State Symphony — three quite different cases for the same piece.

A composer to explore

The late-Romantic Austrian symphonist in the gap you say you have — and Bruckner’s Ninth is on Radio 3 on Thursday night, which gives you a free side-by-side this week.

Franz Schmidt (1874–1939)

Born in Pressburg — Bratislava now — into a family of Hungarian descent, Schmidt moved to Vienna and studied cello with Ferdinand Hellmesberger and theory and composition with Robert Fuchs at the Conservatory. Then the detail that should get your attention: from 1896 he sat in the cello section of the Vienna Court Opera orchestra, where Mahler repeatedly picked him out to play the cello solos while his pay stayed that of a rank-and-file player. He taught at the Conservatory, became Director of the Vienna academy in 1925, was forced into retirement by ill health in early 1937, and died at Perchtoldsdorf in February 1939.

So he is a man who learned the late-Romantic orchestra from the inside of it, with Mahler on the podium, and then wrote four symphonies that almost nobody outside Austria programmes. That is the unexplored 20th century you have written about, and it is tonal from first bar to last.

The route in. Start with the Fourth Symphony (1932–33), which he subtitled Requiem für meine Tochter after his daughter Emma died in childbirth; Oswald Kabasta conducted the premiere in Vienna in 1934. It is in C major, runs about three quarters of an hour, and is laid out as four sections played without a break — the only real pause falls between the Adagio and the Molto vivace. The piece opens and closes with a solo trumpet, and the lament at the centre is carried by a long cello solo, which given the paragraph above is not a coincidence worth passing over. Then the Second Symphony, the showpiece of the four for orchestral virtuosity. After that, if the language has taken, the big choral work — the oratorio Das Buch mit sieben Siegeln, which is the piece Austrians would name first.

Paavo Järvi recorded all four live with the Frankfurt Radio Symphony, and the 1934 Universal Edition score of the Fourth is free on IMSLP — public domain in the UK and the EU, though not in the United States, so download it from here rather than wondering.

New music

One 21st-century tonal composer a week. This one has five symphonies, five Naxos albums to himself, and virtually no UK air-time at all.

Jonathan Leshnoff (b. 1973)

An American composer whose output is the one you keep saying you wish you had time to find: symphonies, concertos and string quartets, written tonally, on purpose, by someone with no interest in either minimalism or the avant-garde. Naxos has now given him five albums of his own, the Nashville Symphony commissions him, and the Pittsburgh and Baltimore orchestras play him — and he is almost never broadcast here. His own site quotes the Baltimore Sun calling the music “boldly dissonant or hauntingly lyrical, wildly animated or intriguingly contemplative”, which is a critic admitting the range is wider than the label.

Why you in particular: he is a working symphonist who has decided that the inherited frames — sonata shapes, concerto ritornello, the four-movement symphony — are still load-bearing, and he builds inside them rather than around them. That is your problem too, and his solutions are legible. The harmonic language sits nearer Shostakovich and Brahms than anything from the last fifty years of fashion, and much of the recent work is organised around Jewish mystical texts, which gives it a Pärt-adjacent stillness in the slow music without any of the repetition you dislike.

The route in. Starburst first — a short orchestral curtain-raiser, the filler track on the Nashville disc, and the quickest way to hear whether the ear suits you. Then the Fourth Symphony, “Heichalos” (2018), commissioned and premiered by the Nashville Symphony and recorded by them under Giancarlo Guerrero; it is in two movements, and the recording was made with the Violins of Hope — instruments recovered from the Holocaust — and released on Holocaust Remembrance Day, 2 May 2019. Then the Double Concerto for clarinet and bassoon, written for Michael Rusinek and Nancy Goeres of the Pittsburgh Symphony; the link below follows the printed score through the first movement, which is the version you want.

Something to study

You own the complete quartets in score and you want to know how he got so far ahead. The “Haydn” set is the one place his own manuscripts answer you — and the answer is not what the legend says.

Mozart, String Quartet No. 18 in A major, K.464 (1785)

Start with the evidence that he worked at it. Artaria published the six quartets dedicated to Haydn in Vienna in 1785; the dedicatory letter is dated 1 September and the set was announced in the Wiener Zeitung on the 17th. In that dedication Mozart calls the quartets the fruit of “a long and laborious endeavour” — the Morgan Library’s rendering; the German is langer und mühsamer Arbeit, and other translations go as far as “long and painful labour”. The dates on the autograph bear him out: K.387 is dated 31 December 1782 and K.421 June 1783, so the set took the best part of three years. Nobody commissioned them. Haydn heard them at two private gatherings at Mozart’s own home in January and February 1785, and Artaria paid 100 ducats for the rights.

The thing to actually do this week. The autograph is British Library Add MS 37763, published in facsimile by Alan Tyson in 1985 — and the British Library’s scan of the holograph is on the IMSLP page for K.464, free. These are the quartets where Mozart’s manuscripts stop being clean. Put your printed score beside his corrected one for a single movement and the question answers itself: he got that far ahead by rewriting, like everybody else, only harder.

Then the oddity in K.464 itself. Four movements, about half an hour, and the order is Allegro – Menuetto – Andante – Allegro: the minuet comes second and the variation set is the slow third movement. Beethoven took that over wholesale in Op. 18 No. 5 — same key, same movement order, a variation slow movement in the same place, and something very close to a quotation of the finale’s theme.

And the part worth knowing, because it is usually told wrong. The standard reference is Jeremy Yudkin’s “Beethoven’s Mozart Quartet” (JAMS 45, 1992), which reads Op. 18 No. 5 as straightforward anxiety of influence. But the Cambridge article below queries the anecdote underneath it — the story that Beethoven copied out K.464 by hand comes via Czerny, and the copy the Beethoven-Haus in Bonn actually holds is of K.387, not K.464. So the biographical detail is shakier than the books imply, while the musical evidence inside Op. 18 No. 5 needs no anecdote at all. A good week to read the piece and decide for yourself which kind of proof you find more convincing.
