Listen this week

Four works that withhold the composer's signature — two juvenilia, one overlooked relative, and one living composer you already love.

Octet in C minor, Op. 15a (1850)

Woldemar Bargiel (1828–1897)

You have said you are obsessed with that family, and this is the member of it you were never handed. Bargiel was Clara's half-brother — same mother, different father — nine years her junior, brought into Robert's and Mendelssohn's orbit through her, and he stayed inside that circle for life: decades later he sat alongside Clara and Brahms editing the complete Schumann edition, which is a peculiar fate, spending your later years putting your family's papers in order. The Octet is from 1850, written at twenty-two, and the striking thing is how little it sounds like a young man imitating his brother-in-law. Where Robert would fragment an idea, Bargiel lets the eight parts stay long-breathed and dark; the C minor is real C minor, not a mood. Play it directly after the Mendelssohn Octet, which is the obvious comparison and the one the Hyperion disc invites, and hear how differently two composers use the same eight instruments — Mendelssohn for brilliance and speed, Bargiel for weight.

Piano Quartet in A minor (c. 1876)

Gustav Mahler (1860–1911)

Mahler is a massive favourite of yours, which is exactly why this is worth twelve minutes: it is the only chamber music of his that survives, written at about sixteen as a Vienna Conservatory student, and it contains almost nothing of the composer you love. No irony, no march breaking in from the next field, no collapse into banality and out again. What it has instead is a single unbroken Nicht zu schnell in sonata form, competently and rather beautifully done in the manner of the Brahms everyone in Vienna was writing then, with a long violin cadenza near the end that is the one moment where somebody unusual is clearly at the desk. The interest for you is not the piece's quality — it is the gap. You can hear a gifted student who has absorbed the tradition completely and has not yet found a single reason to break it. Everything that makes Mahler Mahler arrived later and from outside music. There is also a fragment of an intended scherzo, which breaks off after a couple of minutes.

Piano Quintet in C major, Sz. 23 (1904)

Béla Bartók (1881–1945)

The same experiment as the Mahler, run on the composer you love most in that century. This is Bartók at twenty-three, finished in the summer of 1904 and premiered in Vienna the following November by the Prill Quartet with the composer himself at the piano — and it is a big, sprawling, unembarrassed late-Romantic quintet. Brahms is all over it. Strauss is all over it. The Hungarian material that does appear is verbunkos, the urban recruiting-dance idiom every Hungarian composer used, not the village music he had not yet gone out and collected. Hearing it matters because it dates the change precisely: within two years of this piece he and Kodály were in the field with a phonograph, and everything you think of as Bartók's language starts from that trip rather than from his training. He suppressed the quintet afterwards and it stayed unpublished for decades, which tells you what he came to think of it. Listen anyway — you cannot hear how far someone travelled without knowing where they started.

Airs from Another Planet (1986)

Judith Weir (b. 1954)

The counterweight, and the only living composer here. You love Weir, so this is not an introduction — it is the specific piece to put on after three works about influence, because it is a piece about influence that has been thought through as a joke and then executed seriously. Written for the Nash Ensemble, wind quintet and piano, and the conceit is that a colony of Scots emigrated to another planet some generations ago and their traditional music has gone on evolving there without us. So you get strathspey and pibroch shapes that are recognisably themselves and recognisably wrong — the grace-note patterns intact, the intervals drifted, the drone gone slightly out of true. What makes it more than a conceit is the economy. Weir gets a whole imagined tradition across in about twelve minutes with six players and no explanation, which is the thing she does better than almost anyone: the material carries the story so the composer never has to narrate it. Worth studying for that alone.

A composer to explore

One, with a route in — which piece first, then what.

Why now — Josef Suk (1874–1935) You already love Dvořák, and Suk is the door out of Dvořák that leads somewhere you have not been. He was Dvořák's best composition pupil and then his son-in-law, married to his daughter Otilie, and second violin in the Bohemian Quartet for forty years — so he spent his life inside the tradition, not reacting against it. Then, in 1904, Dvořák died. Suk began a symphony in his memory; while he was writing it, in 1905, Otilie died too, aged twenty-seven. The piece absorbed both losses and came out the other side as something no longer Dvořákian at all: harmonically far stranger, structurally much longer-limbed, still entirely tonal. It is the single clearest case in the repertoire of a composer being pushed out of his teacher's language by events rather than by theory — which is exactly the question you keep circling about how eras give way to each other.

Route in: Asrael, symphony in C minor, Op. 27 (1905–06)

Start here

An hour, five movements, named for the angel of death, and it does not behave like a memorial piece — there is far more anger in it than grief for the first half. The structure is worth knowing before you start: the first three movements were written for Dvořák, and then Otilie died and Suk added two more, so the work visibly changes purpose in the middle. The fourth movement is her portrait and it is the most tender thing he wrote. The finale does not resolve so much as exhaust itself into C major. Give it the full hour in one sitting; it does not survive being sampled. Then: the Serenade for Strings, Op. 6, from 1892 — twenty-eight minutes of pure sunlight, written at eighteen because Dvořák told him he was writing too much in minor keys, and useful precisely as the before-picture. After that: Zrání (Ripening), Op. 34, the late tone poem where he goes furthest out; harder work, and the payoff is real.

Something to study

For the composer's ear, not the listener's.

This week — the eight bars that made Mozart raise his game You own the Mozart quartets in score and you are working through them, and you keep asking how he was so far ahead. Part of the answer is that in 1781 somebody got ahead of him first, and he noticed.

Haydn, String Quartet in B minor, Op. 33 No. 1 (Hob. III:37) — opening

The exercise

Haydn advertised the Op. 33 set as written "in an entirely new and special manner", which was partly sales copy and partly true. Mozart read them, and the six quartets he dedicated to Haydn — the ones on your desk — are the direct answer, three years of labour he described as the fruit of long and laborious effort, which is not language he used about anything else. So the useful thing is not to study Mozart harder. It is to study what Mozart was answering.

Open Op. 33 No. 1 and read the first eight bars without looking at the key signature. Haydn starts the quartet on a D major triad, and voices and continues it so that every instinct says D major — until the cadence arrives and reveals that you have been in B minor from the first note, with the D major functioning as its relative. He does not modulate to get there. He simply declines to state the tonic and lets you assume, then withdraws the assumption. It is a joke about tonal expectation, and it is placed as the very first gesture of a set announcing a new manner — the manner being, in part, that the listener's orientation is now material the composer can compose with.

Two things to do with a pencil. First, write out those bars and try to rebar them so the D major reading holds — you will find Haydn has blocked every exit. Second, and this is where it pays for you: go to the opening of Mozart's K. 428 or the notorious introduction to K. 465, and ask what problem he is solving. Both withhold the tonic. Both make you commit to a reading and then take it away. Mozart did not invent that device; he found it in Haydn, understood it faster than anyone else alive, and pushed it further in about eighteen months. That is a much more useful account of him being ahead than genius is.
