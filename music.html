Week of 3 October 2026

This week is about unfinished and borrowed work. The main listening item is a symphony by Mahler's room-mate, who died at twenty-five and whom Mahler called the founder of the new symphony; Radio 3 gives Thursday evening to Mahler's own unfinished Tenth. In between there is a sonata that three composers wrote jointly as a puzzle for a violinist, and a study item about the Mozart quartet that Beethoven copied out by hand before daring to write his own.

Listen this week

Three, none of them from me before. The Rott is the one to clear an hour for — and if you can, hear it before Thursday's Mahler 10 rather than after.

Symphony in E major (1878–80)

Hans Rott (1858–1884)

Mahler is a massive favourite of yours, so here is the one piece that sits upstream of him. Rott was Mahler's contemporary at the Vienna Conservatory and for a time his room-mate. He finished this symphony at twenty-two, was committed to an asylum, and died at twenty-five. Mahler wrote in 1900: “It is completely impossible to estimate what music has lost in him. His First Symphony soars to such heights of genius that it makes him without exaggeration the founder of the New Symphony as I understand it.” That is not a critic's claim about influence — it is Mahler naming his own source.

What makes it worth your hour rather than a general listener's is that ideas from this score turn up again in Mahler's Second, Third, Fifth and Seventh. So you are not listening to a precursor in the vague sense; you are listening to a 22-year-old assemble the vocabulary — the gait, the brass chorales, the sudden rustic intrusions — that someone else then spent thirty years building a career on. Go to the third movement first if you only have a quarter of an hour: Frisch und lebhaft is where the Mahlerian scherzo already exists, fully formed, years before Mahler wrote one. The uncomfortable composer's question the piece puts to you is the one about priority: how much of a style is invention and how much is being the one who lived long enough to develop it.

Four movements, about fifty-six minutes — the finale alone runs over twenty · the reference recording is Jakub Hrůša and the Bamberger Symphoniker (Deutsche Grammophon 486 2932, 2022), which fills out the disc with Mahler's Blumine

Symphony in D minor, Op. 20 (1869)

Albert Dietrich (1829–1908)

You are obsessed with the Schumann family, so this is for the part of that circle you have almost certainly never played. Dietrich studied composition with Schumann at Düsseldorf and was in the room when Brahms arrived; the two became close friends. In October 1853 the three of them wrote a violin sonata together as a gift for Joseph Joachim, built on the notes F–A–E for Joachim's motto Frei aber einsam — and it was Dietrich who wrote the substantial first movement, in sonata form, with Schumann contributing an Intermezzo and Brahms the Scherzo. Joachim's game was to guess who had written what. If you only know one thing about Dietrich it is probably that; this symphony is what he did with the rest of his career.

It is dedicated to Brahms and was one of the most frequently performed new symphonies of its day, which is the interesting part: this is what a competent, well-connected, Schumann-trained symphonist sounded like in 1869 — seven years before Brahms finally released his own First. So it is a control sample. Listen to it as a composer rather than as a consumer and the question answers itself: everything in the Brahmsian manner is here except the thing that makes Brahms frighten you. Dietrich's joins are visible. You can always hear where you are. Which is the clearest possible demonstration of what the seamlessness actually costs and why almost nobody else managed it — you learn more about Brahms from an hour of Dietrich than from another hour of Brahms.

Four movements, around thirty-five minutes · recorded by the Solistes Européens, Luxembourg under Christoph König for Naxos (8.574507), coupled with his Violin Concerto

Four Transylvanian Dances, for string orchestra (1943–49)

Sándor Veress (1907–1992)

The short one, and the one that fills an actual gap. You love Bartók and you say there are many 20th-century composers you have not explored: Veress is the hinge. He studied composition with Kodály and piano with Bartók, was Bartók's assistant at the Budapest Academy, and did field research on Hungarian, Transylvanian and Moldavian folk music — the same work, in the same places. He was born in Kolozsvár in Transylvania, so these are his own region's dances, not picturesque borrowings. Then he went to Switzerland in 1949 and taught Ligeti, Kurtág and Heinz Holliger. One man connects Bartók's folk collecting to the next two generations, and he is barely played.

Thirteen minutes, tonal and modal throughout, four dances with their Hungarian dance-type names intact — Lassú (slow), Ugrós (jumping), Lejtős, Dobbantós (stamping). What to listen for: the first three were written in 1943 and toured Transylvania in 1944, and the fourth was added in 1949 to a commission from Paul Sacher, after he had left Hungary for good. So the set has a seam in it, and the question is whether you can hear which movement was written by an exile. It is also a practical model for the thing you say you are curious about — re-using very old material — because the technique on show is not quotation but how to take a dance type's rhythmic skeleton and write freely inside it.

About thirteen minutes · Camerata Bern under Heinz Holliger recorded them for ECM New Series (1555) on an all-Veress disc — Holliger was his pupil, which makes it something close to an authorised reading

A composer to explore

Czech, tonal, prolific, and standing exactly where you already like to stand — and Radio 3 hands you his teacher's Seventh Symphony on Tuesday, which is a free way in.

Josef Suk, 1874–1935

Why now. You love Dvořák and the Czech and Russian line, and you think there are 20th-century composers you have not got to. Suk is both at once. He went to the Prague Conservatory in 1885, studied violin with Bennewitz and composition with Stecker and then Dvořák — and in 1898 he married Dvořák's daughter Otilie. So he is the literal heir: pupil, then son-in-law, then the man who had to work out what to do with that inheritance after it collapsed. In May 1904 Dvořák died; about eight months later Suk began a funeral symphony; and while he was writing it Otilie died too. The piece that came out of that is the Asrael Symphony, and it is why he matters rather than merely being someone's son-in-law.

The route in — and do these in order. Start with the Serenade for Strings in E flat, Op. 6 (1892). He was eighteen, it is sunny and completely unclouded, and both Dvořák and Brahms praised it — half an hour, no emotional demands, and it establishes the baseline you need. Then go to the Asrael Symphony in C minor, Op. 27 (1906), named for the angel of death, around an hour. Hearing them the right way round is the whole point: on its own, Asrael is a long sad symphony by someone you don't know. After the Serenade it is audibly the same man fourteen years later, with the harmony pulled apart from the inside — and the distance between the two is the most compressed illustration you will get of how a late-Romantic idiom turned into a 20th-century one without anybody deciding to be modern. If you want more after that, the piano cycle About Mother and the tone poem A Summer's Tale are the next rung.

The thing to carry away as a composer: Suk never abandoned tonality, and never resolved back into comfort either. That is a narrow path and very few people walked it — which is the live answer to your question about how the eras ran headlong into each other. Some of them did not collide. Some of them bent.

Something to study

Two scores, both free, both in A major, written fourteen years apart. You already own one of them.

Mozart, K. 464, and what Beethoven did with it

Mozart (1785) & Beethoven, Op. 18 No. 5 (1799)

This is aimed directly at your two live questions — how Mozart was so far ahead of everyone, and how the eras ran into each other — and it needs no new purchases, because you own the complete Mozart quartets in score and are working through them. Here is the fact to start from: while preparing to write his own first set of quartets, Beethoven copied out the whole of Mozart's A major Quartet, K. 464, by hand. Scholars who have examined the relationship argue he imitated K. 464 more openly than he imitated any other work by any other composer. Then he wrote Op. 18 No. 5 in the same key, put the minuet second as Mozart had, and modelled both the variation slow movement and the finale on Mozart's.

What to actually do, in about ninety minutes. Take K. 464 off the shelf and read the third movement — the Andante, a theme with six variations. Note what Mozart does in the last of them: a drum-like cello ostinato underneath, so the variation set does not end by getting faster or louder but by acquiring a floor. Now open Op. 18 No. 5 on IMSLP alongside it and read its variation movement. You are not looking for quotation; you are looking at a 29-year-old solving the same formal problem with the answer sheet open in front of him, and the places he departs are the interesting ones, because each departure is a decision he had to justify to himself against a model he admired enough to transcribe.

Then follow the thread out. The same scholarship traces K. 464's influence past the quartet: Beethoven imitated it most literally of all in the finale of the Kreutzer Sonata, and its harmonic experiments fed the G major Sonata Op. 31 No. 1, the Waldstein, and the first Razumovsky quartet. So this is not a young man's homage he grew out of. One Mozart quartet is still working on him in 1806. That is a more precise answer to “how did the Classical become the Romantic” than any period label will give you: not a transition, a specific document, copied out in a specific hand.

Both scores free on IMSLP · the scholarly treatment is the article “What Beethoven learned from K464” in Eighteenth-Century Music (Cambridge University Press) · allow ninety minutes with both parts open

On the radio

All times BST. Thursday is the day to protect — a lutenist at 5pm and Mahler's Tenth at half seven. Skip the weekend: Saturday and Sunday are given over to a 90th-birthday retrospective of a minimalist, so the week starts on Monday for you.

When What
Mon 5 Oct
1pm
Classical Live		 Mark Padmore and Jonathan Biss, live from Wigmore Hall. Schubert and Schumann songs — your Prince of Music, performed live rather than played off a disc, by a tenor at the end of a long career with this repertoire and a pianist who thinks in paragraphs. Later in the same programme the Calidore Quartet play Schubert chamber music from the East Neuk Festival, and there is Dvořák's second Slavonic Dance from the Baltic Sea Festival. If you want one afternoon of song this month, this is it.

1pm
Classical Live		 Dvořák's Seventh, Czech Philharmonic under Semyon Bychkov. From the Baltic Sea Festival, and the Seventh is the one where he is most obviously wrestling Brahms rather than charming anybody — worth hearing in the same week as the Dietrich above, and useful groundwork for the Suk. The rest of the programme is unusually good value too: Mozart's Serenade K. 375 from the English Concert Winds, Christian Zacharias playing Scarlatti, Couperin and Poulenc, and — the genuine rarity — Berwald's Grand Septet, which you will almost never get offered again.
Wed 7 Oct
7.30pm
Radio 3 in Concert		 Haydn's ‘Farewell’ Symphony and Shostakovich's Ninth. BBC National Orchestra of Wales under Johanna Malangre. Two composers you like in one sitting, and a well-made pairing: both pieces are jokes with a serious purpose — Haydn's players walk off the stage one by one, and Shostakovich's Ninth is the brittle little symphony he produced in 1945 when everyone was expecting a victory monument. Also on the bill: Joan Tower's Fanfare for the Uncommon Woman and Margaret Bonds's The Montgomery Variations, written in response to the civil rights movement.
Thu 8 Oct
5pm
In Tune		 Toby Carr, lutenist, live in the studio. Aimed squarely at you: you play the lute, and lute players do not often turn up on afternoon radio. He is presenting music from a new album, Softest Thunder, released on Delphian the following day. Twenty minutes of your own instrument played properly, and a record to go and find on Friday.
Thu 8 Oct
7.30pm
Radio 3 in Concert		 Mahler's Tenth Symphony. The week's event. BBC Scottish Symphony Orchestra under Antony Hermus, preceded by Willem Jeths's Mors Aeterna. Mahler left the Tenth unfinished at his death in 1911, and what you usually get in concert is the Adagio alone — a complete performance is the rarer thing, and it necessarily means someone's performing version is being used. The billing does not say whose, which is itself worth listening for: you know Mahler's orchestration from the inside well enough to hear where the authentic draft thins out and an editor starts making choices. Pair it with the Rott at the top of this issue and you have the two ends of the same career in one week — the symphony Mahler learned from, and the one he did not live to finish.
Fri 9 Oct
1pm
Classical Live		 Arvo Pärt's Greater Antiphons, and Sibelius's Kullervo. The Pärt comes from the Leipzig Gewandhaus Orchestra under Vasily Petrenko — an orchestral setting of the Advent O-antiphons, and not the Pärt everybody plays. Kullervo is early Sibelius, enormous, from the Baltic Sea Festival, and almost never programmed in Britain. Shostakovich's First Piano Concerto is in the same programme from the Estonian National Symphony Orchestra.

Two more if you have the time: Piano Mondays, Monday at 7.30pm, has Francesco Piemontesi at the Schubertiade playing Schubert's G major Sonata, D894 — which Schumann called the most perfect sonata in form and conception, and that sentence alone makes it worth the hour. The second half is Liszt, so stop there. And Thursday at 1pm has Brahms's Third Symphony from the Estonian National Symphony Orchestra under Olari Elts, Kodály's Háry János suite with cimbalom, songs by Alma Mahler, and Schubert's Trout Quintet with Christian Zacharias.

Live near Colchester

One, three weeks out, and it is the best local booking of the autumn — worth putting in the diary now rather than discovering afterwards.

When What & where
Sat 24 Oct
7.30pm
St Botolph's,
Colchester		 Colchester Symphony Orchestra — Mahler's Fourth Symphony. Your massive favourite, done by your own town's orchestra, ten minutes from the middle of Colchester, for £18 (under-25s free). Mahler 4 is the one to hear live in a church rather than a big hall: it is his most chamber-scaled symphony, no trombones, and the detail that gets flattened on a recording — the solo violin retuned a tone high in the second movement, scraping away as Freund Hein — is exactly what you will hear in a room this size. Strauss's Four Last Songs before it with Elinor Rolfe Johnson, and the programme opens with the Meistersinger overture. Tickets are usually available on the door, but it is worth booking.
