Week of 5 September 2026

This week is about ancestry — three of these four pieces sit directly upstream of music you already love, and the fourth is a line that simply stopped. There is a symphony written before Mahler wrote any of his that already sounds like him, a book of 1599 consort dances that turns out to be the grammar behind English music three hundred years later, and a quartet by a boy who shared Mozart's birthday and did not reach twenty. And the radio is unusually good: Rattle conducts an all-Schumann Prom on gut strings on Monday.

Listen this week

Four pieces, each one the thing behind a thing — 1599, 1824, 1880, 1916.

Symphony No. 1 in E major (1878–80)

Hans Rott (1858–1884)

Mahler is a massive favourite, so start here: Rott shared a room with Mahler at the Vienna Conservatory, studied organ with Bruckner from 1874 to 1877, and finished this symphony in 1880 — a decade before Mahler's First was heard. Mahler called him a musician of genius and said the two of them were “two fruits from the same tree”. Brahms, judging a competition Rott entered, told him he had no talent and should give up music. Rott died of tuberculosis in 1884, aged twenty-five. The symphony was not performed until 1989 — a hundred and five years later, by the Cincinnati Philharmonia under Gerhard Samuel.

The reason to hear it is not the biography, it is what it does to the question of where the Mahler idiom came from. Go straight to the scherzo. The gestures you think of as Mahler's signature — the Ländler that curdles, the brass chorale arriving as an apotheosis rather than a climax, the triangle and the sudden rustic banality dropped into a symphonic argument — are all here, and Mahler had heard them. Some of the melodic and structural correspondences with Mahler's own symphonies are close enough to be startling. What Rott lacks is the orchestration: he cannot yet balance the thing he has imagined, and hearing a great idea imperfectly scored is far more instructive for a composer than hearing a good one scored perfectly. You can hear exactly where the technique runs out.

Pavans, Galliards, Almains and other short Aeirs (London, 1599)

Anthony Holborne (c. 1545–1602)

You play the lute, you like Dowland, and you have said you are curious about re-using very old music by lesser-known composers. Holborne is Dowland's exact contemporary and the 1599 book is sixty-five pieces — the largest surviving English collection of its kind. Dip in rather than play it through; each dance is three or four minutes.

Here is the part that should interest you as a composer. The title page offers the music “for Viols, Violins, or other Musicall Winde Instruments”. Holborne is writing five real parts with the timbre deliberately unspecified — so none of the effect can come from orchestration, because he does not know what will be playing it. Every bit of the character has to be carried by voice-leading, register and harmonic rhythm alone. Try the exercise: take one pavan off IMSLP and work out what makes it grave rather than light when you have no dynamics, no articulation and no instrumentation to help you. It is the strictest possible discipline in making counterpoint do expressive work, and it is the reason this repertoire kept being raided by English composers three centuries later — which is the next item.

Piano Quartet in A minor, Op. 21 (1916)

Herbert Howells (1892–1983)

Play this straight after the Holborne, in one sitting. That is the actual recommendation. Howells is filed under cathedral music and almost nobody reaches for his chamber output, which is a waste, because the instrumental pieces are where the harmonic method is exposed without a text to carry it. Everything that makes the church music sound the way it does — false relations, the flat seventh, modal mixture, cadences that go plagal when you expect them to go perfect — is lifted straight out of the Tudor consort repertoire you have just been listening to. Hearing 1599 and 1916 next to each other makes the borrowing audible in a way that no amount of reading about English pastoralism does.

It sits squarely in the Elgar and Vaughan Williams line you already like, and it has a story that is hard to shake: it is dedicated “To the Hill at Chosen and Ivor Gurney who knows it” — Chosen Hill, between Gloucester and Cheltenham, looking across to the Malverns. Howells revised the quartet in 1936, by which time Gurney's health had collapsed entirely. The slow movement is the one that will get you.

String Quartet No. 1 in D minor (published 1824)

Juan Crisóstomo de Arriaga (1806–1826)

You own the Mozart quartets in score and you are studying them, and one of your standing questions is how Mozart got so far ahead of everyone. Arriaga is the strangest possible control experiment. He was born in Bilbao on 27 January 1806 — the same baptismal names and the same date as Mozart, fifty years later — wrote his three quartets at sixteen, had them published in Paris in 1824 as the only works printed in his lifetime, and died on 17 January 1826, ten days short of his twentieth birthday. He had studied with Baillot, Cherubini and Fétis at the Paris Conservatoire. Cherubini, hearing his Stabat Mater, is supposed to have said “you are music itself”.

What makes it worth an hour of your study time rather than a sentimental listen: this is Classical-language quartet writing produced in 1824, the year Beethoven finished the Ninth. So it is a live sample of your other question — how the eras ran into each other — taken from someone who had absorbed Haydn and Mozart thoroughly and was working in Paris while the Romantic era went on around him. Track the D minor first movement's transitions and the finale against the Mozart quartets you have open. The craft is genuinely at the level; what is missing is not technique. Deciding for yourself what is missing, at sixteen, is the most direct answer you will get to how far ahead Mozart actually was.

A composer to explore

One, with a route in — which piece first, then what.

Why now — Nikolai Myaskovsky (1881–1950) You like the Russian school and Shostakovich, and you have said there are many twentieth-century composers you have not explored. Myaskovsky is the largest unexplored one available to you: twenty-seven symphonies and thirteen string quartets, tonal throughout, and almost entirely absent from concert programmes. He taught Khachaturian and Kabalevsky, and he was named alongside Shostakovich and Prokofiev in the 1948 decree that denounced them all for formalism. He is the missing middle term between Tchaikovsky and Shostakovich — the composer who carried a long Romantic melodic line into the Soviet symphony without either breaking it or turning it into irony, which is exactly the manoeuvre Shostakovich is usually credited with inventing.

Route in: Symphony No. 21 in F sharp minor, Op. 51 (1940)

Start here

Under twenty minutes, one continuous movement, and by common consent the most tightly made thing he wrote — a sonata form with a slow introduction and a matching conclusion. It became his best-known work and Ormandy recorded it with the Philadelphia. Start here precisely because twenty-seven symphonies is an intimidating door and this one is a single span you can hold in your head at first hearing.

Listen for the thing he does that is worth stealing: the long melody in the introduction is not developed by sequence, which is the standard Romantic move and the one that makes long tunes sound padded. He re-harmonises it instead, so the same line returns under different light rather than being wound up a step at a time. That is how he sustains melody at length without the seams showing. Then: the Cello Concerto in C minor (1944), his most-loved piece and the easiest to fall for. After that: Symphony No. 6 (1923), the big, dark, early one, where he is a much wilder composer than the later reputation suggests.

Something to study

For the composer's ear, not the listener's.

This week — the Mozart quartet Beethoven copied out by hand You have the complete quartets in score and you are working through them. This is the one to put on the desk next, because there is an unusually direct piece of evidence attached to it: we know what another great composer took from it, because he wrote it out.

Mozart, String Quartet No. 18 in A major, K. 464 (1785) — the Andante, and then Beethoven's Op. 18 No. 5

The exercise

K. 464 is the fifth of the six quartets Mozart dedicated to Haydn. Beethoven admired it enough to copy the last two movements into score by hand, and then wrote his own Op. 18 No. 5 in the same key, with a variation-set slow movement, modelled directly on it. That gives you something you almost never get: a documented, deliberate act of study by one composer of another, with both scores free and open in front of you.

Do it in this order. First, the Andante of K. 464 on its own — a theme and variations, and the reason the quartet is nicknamed “the Drum”. In the late variation the cello drops into a repeated dotted figure and stays there, a rhythmic ostinato underneath everything else. Notice what that costs him and what it buys: the bass can no longer articulate the phrase structure, so the harmonic rhythm has to be carried entirely by the upper three, and the texture goes from four independent voices to three-over-a- pedal-in-motion. That is a real compositional gamble in a genre built on four equal parts.

Then open Beethoven's Op. 18 No. 5 alongside it and ask the only question that matters here: what did he keep and what did he refuse? He kept the key, the placing of a variation set, and the idea of constraining one voice to free the others. Work out where he declines to follow — and you will have a much sharper answer than any textbook gives you to why Mozart's quartet writing sounds effortless and Beethoven's sounds willed. The Brentano Quartet's programme note is a good short companion; the Cambridge article “What Beethoven Learned from K464” is the scholarly version if you want to go further.

On the radio

The Proms are in their closing week. Four nights worth clearing, and Monday is the one.

When What
Mon 7 Sep
7.00pm
Radio 3, live		 All-Schumann: Isabelle Faust, Freiburg Baroque Orchestra, Sir Simon Rattle. The Genoveva overture, then the Violin Concerto and the Symphony No. 2. Your Prince of Music, played by a period-instrument orchestra — which is the whole point. Schumann's orchestration is permanently accused of being thick and badly balanced, and most of that charge was laid by conductors working with modern brass and steel strings on a big stage. Gut strings, narrow-bore horns and a smaller band is the sound he actually wrote for, and the Second Symphony is the best possible test case because its slow movement is the passage everyone cites as proof he could not score. Listen to it as an argument about whether the accusation was ever true. The interval talk with Laura Tunbridge on the Second is worth staying for.

Bartók's Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta and Rachmaninov's Third Symphony, opening with Varèse's four-minute solo flute Density 21.5 played by Matthew Higham. You love Bartók, and the Music for Strings is a piece whose antiphonal string layout only really declares itself in a hall — the Albert Hall relay will give you the spatial separation that recordings flatten. The Rachmaninov Third is the underrated one of his three.
