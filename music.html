Week of 19 September 2026

Radio 3 has accidentally built a whole week around the same idea: people who were famous and then were not. Composer of the Week is five days of nineteenth-century celebrity virtuosos nobody now plays — a Venezuelan pianist, a Devon harpist, a violinist every major composer wrote for — and scattered across the concert schedule are three substantial tonal works by composers you have almost certainly never played a note of, two of whom were the most respected musicians in their cities while they were alive. This issue takes that as the brief. Everything below except the study item is by someone who was not forgotten for being bad.

Listen this week

Three, all broadcast in the next seven days, all tonal, none of them anybody you have had from me before. Start with the first — it is the shortest and the most surprising.

Sinfonia in C major (c. 1770)

Marianna von Martines (1744–1812)

She grew up in the same Vienna house as Metastasio and Nicola Porpora, was taught keyboard by Haydn when he was a penniless lodger in the attic of that building, and in later life held a salon at which Mozart came regularly to play piano duets with her. So she is not a footnote you are being asked to be charitable about: she is a trained professional at the exact centre of the world you are currently trying to understand, and she left symphonies, concertos and a great deal of sacred music.

Why it is worth a quarter of an hour to you specifically: you have said your live question is how the Baroque, Classical and Romantic eras ran headlong into each other. This piece is a specimen of the first collision, caught mid-air. It is in three movements with no minuet, which is the old Italian overture shape rather than the four-movement symphony Haydn was then busy inventing next door; the outer movements have the drive and the bustling repeated-note accompaniments of the 1760s, and then the harmony keeps doing slightly old-fashioned things a 1780s composer would have smoothed out. Listen to it as a dated artefact rather than as a lost masterpiece and it tells you more about 1770 than a good piece would.

Nonet in E flat major, Op. 38 (1849)

Louise Farrenc (1804–1875)

This is the one to actually play today. Farrenc taught piano at the Paris Conservatoire for thirty years and was paid less than the male professors until this piece was such a hit — the young Joachim led the premiere — that she used it as leverage and got equal pay. That is a good story, but the reason it is here is that she was taught by Reicha and Hummel, which puts her squarely in your Schumann-contemporary territory, and almost nobody programmes her chamber music even now.

The scoring is the interesting part and the reason this is better than her symphonies as a way in: flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon and horn against violin, viola, cello and double bass — five winds, four strings, no piano to hide behind. That is the Spohr nonet lineup, and it sets a genuine problem, because five winds will bury four strings unless every entry is engineered. Listen to how she does it: the strings almost never try to compete on volume, they get the material first and the winds answer, and when she does want weight she doubles at the octave rather than in unison. As a composer that is the thing to steal. Radio 3's Music Map spends a whole hour building a route towards this piece on Sunday afternoon, which is an unusually generous bit of scheduling to have handed you for free.

Quintet in D major, Op. 42 (1893)

Zdeněk Fibich (1850–1900)

You like the Czech school and you have had Dvořák and Martinů from me. Fibich is the third name in that line and the one who gets left out of it — he sits between Smetana and Dvořák in age, he was the most Germanic-trained of the three, and the Czech revival quietly sidelined him for exactly that reason. He is the answer to a question you might not have thought to ask: what did a Czech composer sound like who was not trying to sound Czech?

The Quintet is his best chamber piece and the scoring is odd in a way that pays off: piano, clarinet, horn, violin and cello. No viola, and a horn in a chamber group, which means the sustaining instruments and the articulating ones are unusually separated — you can hear the harmony being held by the horn and clarinet while the strings and piano do the moving, which is a texture you simply cannot get from a piano quintet. He also reuses a theme across movements in a way that is worth your attention: not a Franckian grand return, more a melody that keeps turning up wearing different clothes. The Berlin Philharmonic players do it from Edinburgh on Classical Live on Friday, and the score is free if you want to follow.

A composer to explore

One, with a route in — which piece first, then what.

Why now — Anton Rubinstein (1829–1894) Radio 3 gives him an hour on Friday, which is more attention than he has had in decades, and he is a genuine hole in your map. You enjoy Tchaikovsky and the Russian school: Rubinstein is the man who built it. He founded the St Petersburg Conservatoire, and Tchaikovsky was in its first intake and studied composition with him. Then he spent the rest of his life being attacked from both sides — the nationalists thought him a German imposter, the Germans thought him a Russian one — and the music went down with the argument. Six symphonies, five piano concertos, ten string quartets, and he was in his own day considered the greatest pianist alive after the obvious rival. This section has gone to Zelenka, Suk, Holmboe, Stenhammar, Myaskovsky and Weinberg; Rubinstein is bigger than any of them and less played than all of them.

Route in: Piano Concerto No. 4 in D minor, Op. 70 (1864)

Start here

Start with the Fourth Concerto because it is the piece that explains his reputation and his disappearance in one sitting. For fifty years it was standard repertoire — Rachmaninov played it, Josef Hofmann played it, it was as normal a choice as the Grieg — and then it fell off the edge. Hear it and the reason for both halves of that is obvious: the material is generous and the tunes are real, and the structure is looser and more sectional than the pieces that displaced it. It is the missing link you have probably wondered about without naming, because it is plainly where Tchaikovsky's First Concerto came from and just as plainly where Rachmaninov's Second was going. Hamelin's Hyperion recording is the one that argues for it hardest.

Then: the String Quartet in C minor, Op. 17 No. 2, to hear him working small and under Beethoven's eye rather than playing to a hall. After that: the Symphony No. 2, Ocean — the piece he could not leave alone, expanding it from four movements to seven across thirty years, which makes it an unusually well-documented case of a composer revising in public and not necessarily improving. That last one is a curiosity rather than a masterpiece, and I would rather tell you so than oversell it.

Something to study

Two free scores, twenty-three years apart, both played by the same band on the same broadcast on Monday. This is the best-set-up study item I have been able to give you.

This week — when the orchestra stopped being a group of soloists Monday's Dunedin Consort concert puts Haydn's Symphony No. 6, Le matin (1761) and Mozart's Piano Concerto No. 14, K449 (1784) in the same hour, on period instruments, under one director. Both scores are free on IMSLP. Read them side by side and you can watch a specific thing happen to the orchestra in twenty-three years — and it is not the thing the textbooks put first.

Haydn's Le matin against Mozart's K449 — the detachable orchestra

The exercise

Take Haydn first and mark every bar where a single named player is exposed. You will be marking constantly: a solo flute, a solo violin, a solo cello, and in the minuet's trio a solo double bass, which is the detail everybody remembers because it is so odd. This is a 1761 symphony that still thinks like a concerto grosso: the orchestra is a collection of individuals who step forward and step back, and the texture is built out of alternating blocks of few-against-many inherited straight from Baroque practice. Now try the thought experiment that matters — remove the flute part. The piece collapses. Those players are structural.

Now K449, and here is the fact that makes the comparison sharp rather than merely chronological: Mozart wrote this one so that the wind parts are optional. He said as much — it can be played "a quattro", with string quartet accompaniment and no winds at all, and it still works. Take the oboes and horns out on paper and read what is left. Nothing structural has gone. The harmony is complete in the strings, the counterpoint is complete, and yet with the winds in it sounds richer and more blended than anything in the Haydn. That is the whole change in one experiment: between 1761 and 1784 the orchestra stopped being a set of indispensable soloists and became a blended body with removable colour on top. Haydn's individuals are load-bearing walls; Mozart's winds are paint — and the paint is why it sounds better.

The reason this is worth your afternoon rather than a paragraph: it inverts the usual story. We are taught the Classical orchestra grew by adding winds. What the two scores show is that it grew by making winds dispensable — by moving their work into the strings so the winds were free to do nothing but colour. For someone writing now, that is the more useful lesson of the two, because it is a decision you still have to make every time you score something: is this line carrying the piece, or is it making the piece sound like something.
