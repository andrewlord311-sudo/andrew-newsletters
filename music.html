You have said that Brahms unsettles you — that the seams are invisible and it is sometimes hard to work out where you are in it. This week's issue takes that complaint seriously and follows it outwards: four pieces, spanning 1720 to 1914, in which the difficulty of finding your place is the point rather than a fault. The study item goes after the mechanism directly, in a score you can download in the next two minutes.

Listen this week

Four, deliberately spread across two centuries and four countries, and none of them German symphonic.

L'Infidèle — suite in A minor for baroque lute

Silvius Leopold Weiss (1687–1750)

You play the lute, so this one is not a suggestion but an omission being corrected. Weiss was the finest lutenist in Europe, spent thirty years at the Dresden court on a salary that outstripped almost every singer there, and knew Bach — the two of them reportedly sat and improvised against each other. He left something like six hundred pieces, and almost nobody outside the lute world plays any of them. L'Infidèle is the one to start with because it is the least polite: an Entrée that behaves like an orchestral overture reduced to thirteen strings, and a Sarabande that is the equal of anything Bach wrote in that dance. Listen for how he keeps three real voices going on an instrument that cannot sustain — the counterpoint is implied by placement and decay rather than held, which is a problem you have solved yourself on the instrument.

Six Hamburg Symphonies for strings, Wq. 182 (1773)

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach (1714–1788)

This is the direct answer to your standing question about how the eras ran into each other, and it is not a gradual answer — it is a collision you can hear inside a single bar. Baron van Swieten commissioned these and, by the account that has come down, told Emanuel to write with no regard for the difficulty and no regard for what players would think. He took the instruction literally. Phrases stop mid-sentence. A tutti drops to nothing without warning. Modulations arrive that would not be normal again for fifty years, and the movements run into one another so you cannot count your position in the form. It is 1773 — one year after the Haydn quartets everyone cites as the arrival of the Classical style — and it sounds like neither the era behind it nor the era in front. Start with the G major, Wq. 182/1, then hear the B minor for how far the temper goes.

Each symphony about 11 minutes; all six about 75 · the Spotify album below, or Gli Incogniti under Amandine Beyer for the G major alone

Piano Quintet in G minor, Op. 30 (1911)

Sergei Taneyev (1856–1915)

If you like the Russian school and you like Brahms, this is the piece sitting in the gap between them that nobody handed you. Taneyev was Tchaikovsky's pupil and then, awkwardly, his editor and conscience — he wrote Tchaikovsky letters telling him exactly what was wrong with his symphonies, and Tchaikovsky kept asking for more. He then spent his life on counterpoint with an obsessiveness that was considered eccentric in Moscow, publishing a treatise on invertible counterpoint that is still in print. The quintet is where the two halves of him finally stop arguing: full Russian melodic weight carried on a contrapuntal frame that never lets the texture go slack. The Largo is a passacaglia. The finale brings the whole work's material back at once and it does not sound like a stunt. Forty-five minutes is a real commitment — take the Largo first if you need convincing.

Duo for Violin and Cello, Op. 7 (1914)

Zoltán Kodály (1882–1967)

You love Bartók, and the standard move is to send you further into Bartók. This is the better move: the piece Bartók's closest colleague wrote in the same years, out of the same folk-collecting trips, that gets further on less. Two instruments, twenty-five minutes, and it sounds like four — Kodály keeps double-stopping one line while the other carries a melody, so the ear insists on a quartet that is not there. The material is Hungarian and Transylvanian but worked rather than quoted, and the tuning of the folk sources pulls the harmony sideways without ever leaving a centre. As a composer you will want the third movement, where a slow free introduction turns into a dance by degrees so gradual you cannot name the bar it happened in — the same trick you are complaining about in Brahms, done with two players and no cover.

A composer to explore

One, with a route in — which piece first, then what.

Why now — Vagn Holmboe (1909–1996) You say you love Bartók and that there are 20th-century composers you have not explored. Holmboe is the join between those two sentences. As a young Dane he went to Romania to collect folk music — the same fieldwork, the same decade, the same instinct as Bartók — married a Romanian pianist, and came home with a way of writing that grows a whole movement out of one cell rather than stating and developing themes. He called it metamorphosis and built thirteen symphonies and twenty-one string quartets on it. All of it is tonal. Almost none of it is played here, and the reason is not quality: it is that he wrote steadily in Denmark for sixty years and never once did anything to draw attention.

Route in: Symphony No. 8, Op. 56, "Sinfonia boreale" (1951)

Start here

Four movements, about half an hour, and the most immediately gripping thing he wrote — it opens Allegro molto intensivo and means it. This is the one that will tell you within four minutes whether he is for you, which is exactly what you want from a composer with a catalogue this size. Note what he is not doing: no second subject arriving on cue, no recapitulation to relax into. The material simply keeps becoming a different version of itself, and the form is the record of that happening. Then: the string quartets, where the method is at its most exposed — start anywhere in the middle of the run, they do not need to be taken in order. After that: the Chamber Concertos, thirteen of them, if the quartets have you. The Aarhus Symphony Orchestra under Owain Arwel Hughes recorded the complete symphonies for BIS, and that set is still the only complete one.

Something to study

For the composer's ear, not the listener's.

This week — why you lose your place in Brahms Your words: seamless, and sometimes hard to work out where you are in it. That is not a failure of attention. It is a deliberate, locatable technique, and once you can point at it in a score you can steal it.

Brahms, Symphony No. 3 in F, Op. 90 — first movement

The exercise

Most writing about Brahms's seamlessness talks about harmony and motive. The thing that actually disorients you is metre. Open the first movement and try to conduct it. The main theme is written in 6/4, but its accents fall so that the bar keeps sounding like 9/4 — three groups of three against the notated two groups of three — and Brahms never resolves the ambiguity, he just alternates which reading is on top. Then look at the second subject: it is notated in 9/4. He has taken the conflict that was implied in the first theme and made it the literal metre of the second. That is why the transition does not feel like a transition. There is no seam to find because the second subject is the first subject's rhythmic problem, promoted.

Do this with a pencil. Mark every bar where you hear the downbeat somewhere other than where the barline is, and count how many bars in a row he sustains it before letting you land. Then count the phrase lengths at the same time — Brahms will run five-bar and seven-bar groups under a texture smooth enough that you never notice the count is wrong. Two ambiguities at once, metre and hypermetre, is the whole recipe, and the reason nobody else's music feels quite like this. The third movement, the famous Poco allegretto, is the same trick slowed down far enough that you can watch it in real time — use it as the answer key.
